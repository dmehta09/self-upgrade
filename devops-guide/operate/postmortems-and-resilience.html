Postmortems & resilience.

The incident is over — now extract the lesson and build the system that survives the next one. Blameless postmortems turn outages into upgrades, and resilience patterns make failure boring instead of catastrophic.

01 Blameless postmortems

A postmortem documents an incident to prevent recurrence, not assign blame. It captures a timeline, impact, root cause, and action items with owners + due dates — and focuses on the systems and contributing factors, never the individual.

"Blameless" is not "no accountability" — it's the recognition that good engineers make mistakes in complex systems. You fix the guardrails (the missing test, the easy-to-misclick button), not the person.

In plain English analogy

A blameless postmortem is an airline crash investigation. The NTSB doesn't want a pilot to scapegoat — they want the unvarnished truth, even the embarrassing mistakes, so they can change the cockpit and the checklist and stop the next crash. Punish the pilot and the next one hides the truth.

1

Timeline

What happened, minute by minute, from the scribe's log. Just facts.

2

Impact

Who was affected, how badly, for how long (users, revenue, duration).

3

Root cause

Use the 5 Whys — keep asking "why" until you hit a systemic, fixable cause.

4

Action items

Concrete fixes, each with an owner and a due date — or the postmortem changes nothing.

Blameful
  • "Who pushed the bad config?"
  • People hide details to avoid blame.
  • Fix = "be more careful."
  • The same failure repeats.
Blameless
  • "Why did the system let a bad config ship?"
  • Honest, complete timeline.
  • Fix = a validation check + a canary.
  • That failure mode is now impossible.
Common trap gotcha

Blame makes people hide information — and a postmortem built on a sanitized story finds the wrong cause, so the same failure repeats. Also deadly: action items with no owner or due date. An unassigned "we should add a test" is a wish, not a fix.

02 Resilience patterns

Resilience means a single failure degrades gracefully instead of cascading. These patterns are the standard toolkit — most are a few lines of client config, not a rewrite.

Timeouts
Never wait forever on a dependency. A request with no timeout ties up a thread until the whole service stalls.
Retries + backoff + jitter
Retry a transient failure, but wait exponentially longer each time, plus a random jitter so clients don't all retry in lockstep.
Circuit breaker
After N failures, "trip" and fail fast for a cooldown instead of hammering a dying dependency — then probe to see if it recovered.
Bulkhead
Isolate resources (separate connection pools/thread pools) so one drowning dependency can't sink the whole ship.
Graceful degradation
Shed non-essential features under stress — serve a cached or read-only view instead of a full error page.
Common trap gotcha

Retries without backoff + jitter cause a retry storm (a.k.a. thundering herd): a service hiccups, every client retries at once, and the synchronized flood finishes off the recovering service. Exponential backoff spaces them out; jitter de-synchronizes them.

backoff.py
import random, time

def backoff(attempt, base=0.1, cap=10):
    # exponential window, then pick a RANDOM point in it (jitter)
    window = min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt)
    return random.uniform(0, window)

for attempt in range(5):
    if call_dependency():
        break
    time.sleep(backoff(attempt))   # spread + de-sync retries

03 Chaos engineering

Chaos engineering is deliberately injecting failure — killing instances, adding latency, blocking a dependency — to find weaknesses before they find you, in a controlled experiment with a hypothesis and a blast-radius limit.

A game day is the human version: schedule a planned failure drill so the team practices the runbook and the on-call muscle memory while everyone's awake and watching.

Hypothesis
"If we kill one AZ, traffic fails over and users see no errors." You test it on purpose.
Blast radius
Start tiny (one instance, off-peak), with an abort switch. Grow confidence before scaling the experiment.
Game day
A scheduled, attended drill — practice incident response and validate runbooks on a calm day, not at 3am.

Why intentionally break your own production system with chaos experiments?

Right. Failure is inevitable; the only choice is whether you meet it on your terms (small blast radius, daytime, an abort button) or its terms (3am, full outage, no idea what breaks next).

04 Backups & disaster recovery

DR planning hinges on two numbers that the business — not the engineer — sets. They drive how much you spend on replication and standby capacity.

RTO — Recovery Time Objective
How fast you must recover. "Back up within 1 hour." Lower RTO → warmer standby (and more cost).
RPO — Recovery Point Objective
How much data loss is tolerable. "At most 5 minutes of data." Lower RPO → more frequent backups / continuous replication.
Common trap gotcha

A backup you've never restore-tested isn't a backup — it's a hope. Plenty of teams discover at the worst moment that the snapshots are corrupt, missing a table, or take 9 hours to restore (blowing the RTO). Rehearse the restore on a schedule.

Pro tip tip

On AWS, lean on managed primitives: RDS/Aurora automated backups + point-in-time recovery (tunes RPO), cross-region read replicas or snapshots for regional failover (tunes RTO), and S3 versioning for object data. But still fire-drill the restore — the feature existing isn't the same as it working.

05 Interview rapid-fire

Why run blameless postmortems?warm-up
Model answer

Because the goal is to prevent the next outage, not punish this one. Blame makes people hide details, so you find the wrong root cause and the failure recurs. Going blameless gets the honest timeline, points the fix at the system (guardrails, tests, automation), and ends with action items that have owners and due dates.

Say it out loud say it

"Like a crash investigation: we want the unvarnished truth to fix the system, not a scapegoat. Blame just teaches people to hide what actually happened."

RTO vs RPO — explain the difference.core
Model answer

RTO is time, RPO is data. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) = how fast you must be back up — drives standby warmth. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) = how much data loss is acceptable — drives backup/replication frequency. "1-hour RTO, 5-minute RPO" means recover within an hour, losing at most five minutes of writes. Both are business decisions; lower = more cost.

Say it out loud say it

"RTO is how long until we're back; RPO is how much data we can afford to lose. Tighter on either one costs more, so the business sets the targets."

Follow-ups to expect
  • Why isn't a backup enough on its own? (You must restore-test it — an untested backup is just a hope.)
  • How would you hit a near-zero RPO? (Continuous/synchronous replication to a standby, accepting higher cost and write latency.)
What's a circuit breaker, and why add jitter to retries?core
Model answer

A circuit breaker trips after repeated failures to a dependency and fails fast for a cooldown — so you stop hammering a dying service and let it recover, then probe before closing again. Jitter randomizes retry timing on top of exponential backoff so clients don't all retry in the same instant; without it, synchronized retries become a thundering herd that re-kills the service just as it recovers.

Say it out loud say it

"A circuit breaker stops you from beating a downed dependency to death. Backoff slows retries down; jitter spreads them out so a thousand clients don't retry in lockstep and cause a stampede."

What is chaos engineering?stretch
Model answer

Deliberately injecting failure into a system — killing instances, adding latency, severing a dependency — as a controlled experiment with a hypothesis and a limited blast radius, to find weaknesses before a real outage does. The human counterpart is a game day: a scheduled drill to rehearse runbooks and incident response while everyone's calm and watching.

Say it out loud say it

"We break things on purpose, in small controlled doses, to find the weak spots before a 3am outage finds them for us — and game days let the team rehearse the response."
