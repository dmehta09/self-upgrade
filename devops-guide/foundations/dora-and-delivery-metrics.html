DORA & delivery metrics.

The DORA four keys turn "are we good at shipping?" into numbers. Crucially, the research found elite teams are both fast and stable — speed and safety aren't a trade-off when the system is well-built.

01 The four keys

DORA measures delivery on throughput (how fast) and stability (how safe). Two metrics each.

From years of research by the DevOps Research and Assessment group, these four predict software delivery performance. They describe the system, not any individual engineer.

In plain English analogy

Judge a sports car on both 0–60 and braking distance. A great car is quick and stops short — "fast" and "safe" aren't opposites when it's well engineered. Cheap cars force the trade-off; good design removes it. Same with delivery.

Left two = speed. Right two = safety. Elite teams push all four — they don't trade one for the other.
Deployment frequency
How often you successfully release to production. Elite = on-demand, many times a day.
Lead time for changes
Time from commit merged to that change running in prod. Elite = under an hour.
Change failure rate
Share of deployments that cause a failure needing a fix (rollback, hotfix, patch).
Failed-deployment recovery time
How long to restore service after a bad deploy (the modern name for MTTR here).

02 Speed vs stability is a false choice

The headline finding: throughput and stability move together. Teams that deploy more often also break prod less and recover faster — because small, frequent, automated changes are easy to test, review, and reverse.

Pro tip tip

"Slow and careful" is the trap interviewers want you to reject. Large batches feel safe but are the hardest to debug and the slowest to recover. The path to both speed and stability is smaller changes, not more sign-offs.

03 Performance tiers

DORA buckets teams into Elite / High / Medium / Low across the four keys. You don't need exact thresholds in an interview — know the shape: elite is on-demand deploys, sub-hour lead time, low failure rate, and recovery in minutes.

Heads up scope

DORA's four keys are delivery metrics — they're about your pipeline and process. The production telemetry that detects failures and feeds recovery — metrics, traces, dashboards, SLO burn-rate alerts — lives in the companion Observability guide.

04 Improving them — and gaming them

The four keys are connected, so the same fixes lift several at once. Shrink the batch, automate the path, and add safe rollback — throughput and stability climb together.

1

Smaller batches

Trunk-based dev, small PRs behind feature flags. Cuts lead time and makes failures tiny and easy to revert.

2

Automate the pipeline

Fast CI gates + automated deploys remove manual delay — lead time and deploy frequency both improve.

3

Make rollback trivial

Promote immutable artifacts; one-command rollback or canary. Slashes recovery time and the cost of a bad deploy.

Common trap gotcha

These are learning signals, not a leaderboard. Tie them to a person's review and people game them — splitting one commit into ten to inflate "deployment frequency," or never tagging incidents to fake a low failure rate. Measure the team and the system; punishing individuals just corrupts the data.

A manager wants to boost "deployment frequency" by rewarding whoever deploys most. What's the risk?

Right. "When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure." Use DORA to find bottlenecks and learn, never as an individual scoreboard — and always read it alongside the stability keys.

05 Interview rapid-fire

Name the four DORA metrics.warm-up
Model answer

Deployment frequency and lead time for changes are the throughput pair; change failure rate and failed-deployment recovery time (MTTR) are the stability pair. Together they capture how fast and how safely a team delivers software.

Say it out loud say it

"Two for speed — deployment frequency and lead time. Two for safety — change failure rate and recovery time. Throughput and stability."

Do you have to trade speed for stability?core
Model answer

No — that's the central DORA finding. Elite teams score high on both because small, frequent, automated changes are easier to test and faster to roll back. The illusion of a trade-off comes from big-batch releases: they feel careful but are hard to debug and slow to recover. Better engineering removes the trade-off rather than balancing it.

Say it out loud say it

"They go together. Smaller batches and automation make you both faster and safer — like a well-engineered car that's quick AND stops short. Big releases are what force the false trade-off."

Follow-ups to expect
  • Why are small batches safer? (Less to test, smaller blast radius, easy to revert.)
  • What's the role of feature flags? (Decouple deploy from release; ship dark, enable gradually.)
How would you improve a team's lead time for changes?stretch
Model answer

First measure where time actually goes — usually waiting in review, slow CI, or batched manual deploys. Then attack the bottleneck: trunk-based development with small PRs, fast parallelized CI, and automated deployment so there's no manual release gate. Smaller changes also lower change failure rate, so lead time rarely improves alone.

Say it out loud say it

"I'd find where time is lost — review, CI, or manual deploys — then cut it with small PRs, fast CI, and automated deploys. Lead time and failure rate usually drop together."
