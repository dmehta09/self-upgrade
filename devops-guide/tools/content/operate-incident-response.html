Incident response.

When prod breaks, calm process beats heroics. One person leads, the team triages by severity, and you stop the bleeding before you diagnose the disease — because every minute of outage is users and money.

01 The ER model

An incident is any unplanned disruption to a service. The response is a structured drill: detect, declare, assign a leader, mitigate, resolve, then learn — not a free-for-all on a 30-person call.

The job in the moment is not to be clever; it's to restore service fast and keep everyone coordinated. Clever happens later, in the postmortem, once nobody is on fire.

In plain English analogy

Incident response is a hospital ER. You triage by severity — the cardiac arrest jumps the queue ahead of the sprained ankle. One doctor leads the trauma bay (the Incident Commander), and you stabilize the patient — stop the bleeding, restart the heart — before you order the MRI to find the underlying disease.

1

Detect

An alert fires (or a user reports it). On-call gets paged.

2

Declare + assign IC

Call it an incident, set a severity, and name an Incident Commander. Open a dedicated channel/bridge.

3

Mitigate

Stop the bleeding — roll back, fail over, or flag the feature off. Restore service first.

4

Resolve

Confirm recovery, update the status page, and stand down the response.

5

Schedule postmortem

Book the blameless review while it's fresh — that's where root cause and fixes happen.

02 Severity levels

Severity sizes the response: who wakes up, how fast, and how loudly you communicate. A SEV1 pulls in leadership and a status-page update; a SEV3 can wait for business hours.

SeverityWhat it meansResponse expectation
SEV1Major outage — service down or data at risk for many users.All-hands now, 24/7. IC + comms lead. Status page + exec visibility.
SEV2Significant degradation — a key feature broken or a partial outage.Page on-call immediately; assign an IC; communicate to affected users.
SEV3Minor issue — small blast radius, a workaround exists.Handle in business hours; track as a ticket; light or no external comms.
On-call rotation
A schedule (e.g. weekly) of who answers pages. Tools like PagerDuty / Opsgenie route the alert to the person on duty.
Escalation policy
If the primary doesn't ack in N minutes, it escalates to a secondary, then a manager — so a missed page never becomes a silent outage.

03 The Incident Commander & crew

The Incident Commander (IC) coordinates — they do not fix. They own decisions and the timeline, freeing the responders to actually debug. One throat to choke, one brain not buried in logs.

Incident Commander
Runs the response: drives priorities, decides to mitigate/roll back, delegates, keeps the timeline. Hands stay off the keyboard.
Comms lead
Owns stakeholder + status-page updates so responders aren't interrupted by "any update?" every two minutes.
Ops / responders
The engineers actually executing the fix — rolling back, failing over, querying.
Scribe
Logs actions and timestamps in the channel. This becomes the postmortem timeline.
Common trap gotcha

A 20-person call with no IC is pure chaos — five people poke prod at once, nobody owns the decision, and the status page goes stale. And never debug root cause while prod burns: mitigate first. A rollback is your friend; the "why" can wait an hour.

No command
  • Everyone talks; nobody decides.
  • Three people try conflicting fixes.
  • Stakeholders ping responders directly.
  • Root-cause spelunking while users are down.
Incident command
  • IC sets one priority at a time.
  • Comms lead shields the responders.
  • Mitigate now (roll back / fail over).
  • Diagnose later, in the postmortem.

04 Communication & the clock

Clear, frequent updates buy trust even while you're still fixing. Post short status-page notes ("investigating → identified → monitoring → resolved") on a cadence; silence reads as "they don't know it's broken."

MTTA
Mean time to acknowledge — page fired → human owns it. Bad escalation policies blow this up.
MTTR
Mean time to recovery — incident start → service restored. The headline reliability outcome; mitigation (not root cause) is what shrinks it.

05 Interview rapid-fire

What does an Incident Commander actually do?warm-up
Model answer

The IC coordinates the response, they don't fix the bug. They own decisions (mitigate now? roll back?), set the one current priority, delegate tasks, shield responders from noise, and keep the timeline. The point is a single decision-maker so the actual engineers can focus on restoring service.

Say it out loud say it

"The IC runs the room, not the keyboard. One person owns the decisions and the timeline so the responders can actually fix it."

Mitigate vs fix — which comes first, and why?core
Model answer

Mitigate first. Restoring service (roll back, fail over to a healthy region, toggle a feature flag off) is fast and cuts user impact now. Root-causing is slow and belongs in the postmortem. Mitigation is what drives MTTR down; chasing the "why" while prod burns just extends the outage.

Say it out loud say it

"Stop the bleeding before you diagnose the disease. Roll back or fail over to restore users, then find the why in the postmortem."

Follow-ups to expect
  • What are your go-to mitigations? (Roll back to last-good, fail over, feature-flag off, scale out, shed load.)
  • What's a runbook? (Pre-written, step-by-step response for a known failure — turns 3am panic into following a checklist.)
How do severity levels and on-call escalation work together?core
Model answer

Severity sizes the response: a SEV1 (major outage) pulls in an IC, comms, and 24/7 effort with a status-page update; a SEV3 (minor, has a workaround) waits for business hours. On-call is the rotation that answers the page (PagerDuty/Opsgenie); the escalation policy bumps an unacked page to a secondary, then a manager — so a missed alert never becomes a silent outage.

Say it out loud say it

"Severity decides how hard we respond; on-call decides who, and escalation guarantees someone actually picks up — a SEV1 wakes people, a SEV3 waits for morning."
