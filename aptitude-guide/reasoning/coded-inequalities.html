Logical Reasoning · coded inequalities

Coded inequalities.

Symbols stand for >, <, ≥, ≤, =. Decode the chain once, then ask: does the conclusion must follow, or only sometimes? Pure pattern speed — high OA yield.

01 The idea

You're given a coding key (e.g. ★ means ≥) and a statement like P ★ Q $ R. Translate to ordinary inequalities, then test conclusions the same way as syllogisms: only tick what is forced.

Almost every bank question is: (1) decode, (2) write one combined chain if possible, (3) check each conclusion against the worst legal case.

In plain English analogy

It's a secret handshake for comparing heights. Once you know ★ means "at least as tall as," the sentence "P ★ Q ★ R" just means P is at least as tall as Q, who is at least as tall as R — so P is at least as tall as R. The fancy symbols are noise; the chain is the signal.

02 Decode → chain → conclude

1

Write the key

Copy ★ = ≥, $ = <, etc. on scratch paper. Don't keep it in your head.

2

Rewrite the statement

Replace every symbol. Prefer a single left-to-right chain: P ≥ Q > R = S.

3

Test conclusions

For each claim, ask: can I pick numbers that obey the statement but break the conclusion? If yes → not definite.

P ≥ Q > R  ⇒  P > R is definite # ≥ then > forces strict > across
GivenConclusionVerdict
A ≥ B ≥ CA ≥ CDefinitely true
A ≥ B ≥ CA > CNot definite (all equal allowed)
A > B ≥ CA > CDefinitely true
A ≥ B < CA ? CNo forced relation
Pro tip tip

When the chain breaks (e.g. ≥ then < facing opposite ways), stop — you usually cannot compare the ends. Don't invent a link.

03 Either–or and "not definite"

Some option sets include either I or II: exactly one of two conclusions must hold, but you don't know which (classic when A ≥ B and conclusions are A > B vs A = B).

Either–or pattern

Statement: A ≥ B. Conclusions: (I) A > B (II) A = B. Neither alone is definite, but together they exhaust the ≥ — pick either I or II.

Neither follows

Statement: A ≥ B ≤ C. Conclusion: A ≥ C. Counterexample A=1, B=1, C=5 — reject.

Common trap gotcha

Treating ≥ as >. If the statement only gives ≥, equality is still allowed — so A > C is often not forced even when A ≥ B ≥ C.

P ≥ Q > R. Which is definite?

P > R. Even if P = Q, Q > R forces P > R. Equality P = R is impossible.

04 Solve it fast

Key: ★ means ≥, # means =. Statement: A ★ B # C. Does A ≥ C follow?warm-up
Solve it

Decode: A ≥ B = C ⇒ A ≥ C. Yes — definite.

The fast way shortcut

Equality is transparent — swap B with C mentally and keep the ≥.

A ≥ B ≥ C. Conclusion: A > C. Follows?core
Solve it

Counterexample: A = B = C = 5. Statement holds, A > C fails. Does not follow.

The fast way shortcut

Try the all-equal case whenever every link is ≥ or ≤.
