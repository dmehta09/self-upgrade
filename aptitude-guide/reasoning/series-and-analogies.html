Series, analogies & classification.

A row of numbers or letters hides one rule — find it and the next term falls out. The whole skill is testing a few suspects fast, not staring and hoping.

01 The idea

Every series obeys one hidden rule. Don't guess — write the gaps between terms and read the pattern off: constant difference, a ratio, squares/cubes, or two patterns interleaved.

Analogies and odd-one-out are the same move in disguise: name the relationship (or shared property), then apply it or break it. Find the rule once and the answer is mechanical.

In plain English analogy

It's a "guess the rule" game. You're handed 3, 6, 11, 18 and asked what comes next — you don't stare at the numbers, you note how each one jumps to the next and spot the rhythm. Read the gaps, not the terms.

02 Number series: write the gaps

First thing, always: subtract neighbours and write the difference row underneath. If the gaps are equal it's arithmetic; if each term is a fixed multiple it's geometric (×2, ×3); if the gaps themselves form a pattern (3, 5, 7…), the rule lives one level down.

3 · 6 · 11 · 18 — read the +gaps
The terms look random; the gap row 3, 5, 7 is the real series — so the next gap is +9 and the term is 18 + 9 = 27.
1

Write the differences

Subtract each term from the next. Equal gaps → add a constant. A clean pattern in the gaps (3,5,7) → keep going.

2

If gaps grow fast, try ratios

2,4,8,16 isn't "+2,+4,+8" worth memorising — it's simply ×2. Big jumps usually mean multiply.

3

Suspect squares & cubes

2,5,10,17 = n²+1. Numbers near 1,4,9,16,25 or 1,8,27,64 are squares/cubes in disguise.

4

Still stuck? Split alternate terms

Read positions 1,3,5… and 2,4,6… as two separate series — many "weird" series are two simple ones zipped together.

03 Letters, analogies & odd-one-out

Letter series are number series in disguise: convert to positions (A=1 … Z=26) and the shift becomes obvious. For analogies, state the rule in words first; for odd-one-out, find the property four share and one breaks.

Common trap gotcha

More than one rule can fit a short series — pick the simplest consistent one (the test-setter did). And before you panic, check for a mixed series: two patterns interleaved (e.g. 1, 2, 4, 4, 7, 8 = odd-spots +3, even-spots ×2). Split the alternate terms.

Find the next term: 2, 4, 8, 16, ?

32. The gaps 2, 4, 8 are themselves doubling — that's the tell for a ratio rule (×2), not an add rule. 16 × 2 = 32.

04 Solve it fast

Next term: 3, 6, 11, 18, ?core
Solve it

Gaps: 6−3=3, 11−6=5, 18−11=7. The gaps go 3, 5, 7, so the next gap is 9 → 18 + 9 = 27.

The fast way shortcut

Don't hunt for a formula — just write the gap row. Consecutive odd numbers (3,5,7,9) is the most common "growing gap" pattern there is.

Odd one out: 4, 9, 16, 20, 25.warm-up
Solve it

4=2², 9=3², 16=4², 25=5² — all perfect squares. 20 is the only one that isn't, so the misfit is 20.

The fast way shortcut

For odd-one-out, scan the obvious clubs first: squares (1,4,9,16,25), cubes, primes, even/odd. Four will share one — the fifth is your answer.

Analogy: Hand : Glove :: Foot : ?warm-up
Solve it

State the rule: a glove is what you wear on a hand. The thing you wear on a foot is a shoe — not a sock or a toe.

The fast way shortcut

Always verbalise the bridge ("glove covers hand") before looking at options. The right answer fits the same sentence; distractors (sock, toe) fit a different one.
