Logical Reasoning · seating & puzzles

Seating & puzzles.

Five clues, one arrangement — who sits where, or who lives on which floor? Lock the firmest clue first, sketch the rest, and the picture finishes itself.

01 The idea

Every arrangement puzzle is a grid you fill from the most certain clue outward. Draw the seats (a row or a circle), place the fixed person, then branch the maybes and eliminate.

Don't juggle clues in your head — commit to paper. The order you process clues in matters: definite positions first, "relative" clues last.

In plain English analogy

It's a Sudoku for people. You never guess wildly — you find the one cell a clue pins down for certain, write it in pen, and that single fact unlocks the neighbours. Start where you're sure; the rest cascades.

02 Linear vs circular: the facing flip

In a row, everyone faces you, so left/right is fixed. In a circle facing the centre, each person's left is your right — directions are mirrored. Facing outward flips it back.

A 5-seat row and a 6-seat circle
Row of 5: the middle is seat 3. Circle of 6: the seat opposite any person is +n/2 = +3 (seat 1 ↔ seat 4).
Common trap gotcha

In a circle facing the centre, a person's left/right is mirrored from your view — their right hand points to your left. And "X sits between Y and Z" doesn't fix an order: X could be on either side, so branch both until another clue decides.

03 The method: lock, branch, eliminate

A reliable three-move recipe turns any arrangement into a fill-in-the-blanks exercise.

1

Lock the most-certain clue

Find the clue that pins an exact seat ("A is at the head", "C is 3rd") and write it in. Fixed positions beat relative ones.

2

Branch the possibilities

For a clue with two options ("between", "either end"), draw both mini-diagrams and carry them forward in parallel.

3

Eliminate with the rest

Apply the remaining clues; any branch that contradicts one is dead. Usually a single arrangement survives.

In a row, P sits 2nd to the right of Q. If Q is in the 1st seat, where is P?

3rd. "2nd to the right of Q" counts two places along: seat 1 → seat 3. The trap reads it as merely "to the right" (seat 2); the ordinal matters.

04 Floor & order puzzles

Floor puzzles are a vertical row — number them bottom (1) to top (n) and treat "above / below" exactly like "right / left". Order puzzles (rank, age, height) are the same single line.

Anchor
Place absolutes first: "X is on the top floor", "the tallest is …". These are your locked clues.
Gap clues
"two floors below", "three places above" — count the gap; watch for whether the clue includes or skips the endpoints.
Direction word
Fix once whether higher number = higher floor (it should: floor 6 is above floor 4), then stay consistent.
Pro tip tip

Distinguish "immediately above" (adjacent floor) from "above" (any floor higher). One is exact; the other only sets an inequality you'll pin down later.

05 Solve it fast

Five people sit in a row. A is exactly in the middle. A's seat number?warm-up
Solve it

The middle of an odd row is (n+1)/2. For 5 seats that's seat 3 — two seats on each side.

The fast way shortcut

Odd row → unique middle at (n+1)/2. Even rows have no single middle, so a "middle" clue there is a flag to re-read.

Six people sit in a circle facing the centre. Who is directly opposite the person in seat 1?core
Solve it

Opposite seat = current + n/2. Here 1 + 6/2 = 1 + 3 = seat 4.

The fast way shortcut

In an even circle, "opposite" is just +n/2 (wrap around if you pass n). Only even-sized circles have a true opposite seat.

A 6-floor building: A lives on the top floor; B lives 2 floors below A. B's floor?stretch
Solve it

Top of 6 is floor 6 (the anchor). "2 floors below" → 6 − 2 = floor 4.

The fast way shortcut

Lock the absolute clue (top = 6), then subtract the gap. "2 below" means subtract 2 — not "the 2nd floor below counting from 1".
