Verbal Ability · completion & jumbles

Sentence completion & para-jumbles.

Both reward one habit: read the joining words. A connector like "but" or "because" tells you the answer before you've weighed the options — and the same logic links scrambled sentences into order.

01 The idea

The connector decides the direction. Contrast ("but / despite / although") needs an opposite; cause ("because / so") needs a consequence; addition ("and / moreover") needs more of the same. Match the word to the logic, not the vibe.

For jumbles, the same links order the sentences: find the opener that needs no setup, then follow pronoun and transition chains and lock any mandatory pairs together.

In plain English analogy

Connectors are road signs. "But" is a U-turn, "because" is a downhill (cause → effect), "and" is straight ahead. You don't guess the route — you read the sign and the blank fills itself. Jumbles are just signs telling you which sentence comes next.

02 Fill-in-the-blank: read the connector

Spot the signpost first, then pick the word whose direction matches. Collocations (fixed word pairings) settle most ties.

Pro tip tip

Lock the collocation. "Known for", "good at", "result in", "capable of" are fixed — the right word is the one English habitually pairs, even if another is a synonym.

03 Para-jumbles: find the opener, then chain

Don't try all orderings. Find the sentence that stands alone (no "this / it / however" pointing backward), then thread the rest by their links.

1

Find the opener

It introduces the topic with a full noun and no backward reference. A sentence starting "This is why…" or "But they…" cannot be first.

2

Follow pronoun chains

A pronoun needs its noun named earlier. "He…" must come after the sentence that first names the person.

3

Follow transition chains

"However / therefore / then" point at the sentence before. Use them to glue neighbours in the right direction.

4

Lock mandatory pairs

Some two sentences clearly belong together (cause then effect, claim then example). Fix that pair, then slot the block.

Valid opener

"A new letter arrived at the office." Full subject, no backward word — it can stand first.

Can't be first

"He opened it eagerly." Both "He" and "it" need earlier nouns, so this comes later.

Common trap gotcha

Transition words flip the meaning — picking "but" where the logic needs "and" reverses the sentence. And a pronoun needs its noun to appear first: any sentence opening with "this / it / they / he / she" without a prior noun cannot be the opener.

"He studied hard ___ he still failed the test." Best connector?

"but". Hard study and failing point opposite ways, so you need a contrast word. "Because/so" would wrongly make failure the result of studying.

04 Solve it fast

"Despite the rain, they ___ the match." Fill the blank.warm-up
Solve it

"Despite" signals contrast — rain would normally stop play, so they did the opposite and kept going. continued.

The fast way shortcut

"Despite / in spite of" = expect the surprising, against-the-grain choice. Stopped/cancelled is what rain expects, so the answer flips it: continued.

What's the first step in a para-jumble?core
Solve it

Find the opening sentence — the independent one that introduces the topic without referring back to anything. Find the opener.

The fast way shortcut

Eliminate every sentence that starts with this/it/they/however — they can't open. Often only one survivor remains, and that's your first sentence.

Order them: (P) He opened it. (Q) A letter arrived. (R) He smiled.stretch
Solve it

"It" in P needs a noun first → that's the letter (Q). The reaction (R) comes last: arrive → open → react. Q P R.

The fast way shortcut

Chase the pronoun: "it" must follow the letter, so Q precedes P. Q is the only backward-reference-free opener — order falls out instantly.
