Reading comprehension.

Long passage, a clock ticking, four near-identical options. The trick isn't reading faster — it's answering from the text and binning the options that overreach.

01 The idea

RC is an evidence hunt, not a memory test. Skim for structure → read the question → locate the line that proves the answer. If you can't point at the sentence, it isn't the answer.

You're not graded on understanding every word — you're graded on matching each option to the passage. Map the shape first, then dive back for the one fact each question needs.

In plain English analogy

Treat the passage like a reference manual, not a novel. You don't read a manual cover-to-cover to fix one thing — you scan the headings, jump to the right page, and copy the line. RC rewards the same "look it up" instinct.

02 The attack plan

Do it in this order. Reading the question before hunting saves you from re-reading the passage five times.

1

Skim for structure

One pass for the skeleton: what's the topic, where does each paragraph turn, what's the author's stance? Don't memorise details — just build a map.

2

Read the question first

Know what you're looking for before you re-enter the text. Note the type: main idea, detail, inference, tone, or vocab-in-context.

3

Locate the evidence

Jump to the relevant paragraph and find the proving line. Keep your finger on it while you test the options.

4

Eliminate the overreach

Cut options that are too extreme, out of scope, or true-in-reality-but-not-in-passage. The survivor is your answer.

Question typeWhat it asksWhere to look
Main ideaThe whole passage's pointFirst/last lines + the through-line, not one detail
DetailA directly stated factThe exact line — it's written there
InferenceWhat's implied, not statedOne small step from a line, never a leap
Tone / attitudeThe author's feelingAdjectives & charged words (e.g. "sadly", "remarkable")
Vocab-in-contextA word's meaning hereThe surrounding sentence, not the dictionary

03 Eliminate the tempting wrong option

Most wrong answers fail in one of three ways: extreme ("always/never/only"), out of scope (true elsewhere, not in this passage), or distorted (right words, wrong claim). The correct option is usually the cautious, hedged one.

Evidence-based (right)

"The author suggests automation may reduce some routine roles." Hedged, and you can point to the line that backs it.

Extreme (the trap)

"Automation will eliminate all jobs." One "all" too strong — the passage never claims it. Sounds bold, fails the proof test.

Common trap gotcha

Answer from the passage, not your own knowledge — an option can be factually true in real life yet wrong because the passage never says it. And distrust absolutes: "always / never / only / all / none" options are usually too strong to be defensible.

A passage says a policy "could ease traffic in some cities." Which option is safest?

The hedged one. It mirrors "could" and "some". Option 2 over-claims with "all/everywhere"; option 3 is outside knowledge the passage never states.

"The author would most likely agree that…" — how do you pick?core
Solve it

This is an inference framing. Pick the option best supported by the passage, and reject anything extreme or unstated. The one the passage backs.

The fast way shortcut

"Would agree" still means "is supported by the lines". Stay inside the passage; the hedged option usually wins.

A question asks for the "tone" of the passage. What is tone?stretch
Solve it

Tone is the author's attitude toward the subject — read it off the charged adjectives and verbs, not the facts. The author's attitude.

The fast way shortcut

Scan for emotion words ("sadly", "remarkable", "troubling"). They reveal attitude faster than re-reading the argument.
