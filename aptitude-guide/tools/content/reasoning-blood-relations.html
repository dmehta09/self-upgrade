Logical Reasoning · blood relations

Blood relations.

"My mother's brother's only daughter" sounds like a riddle until you draw it. A two-minute family tree turns these into free marks every time.

01 The idea

Never track relations in words — draw a family tree. Use □ for male, ○ for female, stack generations as rows, and join them with lines: a vertical line for parent–child, a horizontal one for marriage.

Then it's just reading off the picture. Coded versions ("A + B means A is the father of B") decode into the same tree, one symbol at a time.

In plain English analogy

It's building a tiny family tree on a napkin. You'd never untangle "my dad's sister's husband" in your head — you'd sketch the boxes, draw who married whom and who's whose kid, then point to the answer. The pen carries the load.

02 The family-tree method

Put the oldest generation on top, children below, on the same row as their siblings. Marriages sit side-by-side (──), and a line drops to each child. Mark gender with the shape; leave it unmarked if the puzzle never tells you.

Three generations — grandparents, parents, children

1

Decode each clue to a symbol

"A + B = A is father of B" becomes □A with a line down to B. Translate the legend (+, −, ×, ÷) before touching the chain.

2

Place by generation

Parents above children, spouses side-by-side. Keep rows clean so a "grandfather" is literally two rows up.

3

Read off the answer

Trace from one person to the other along the lines and name the link. Up one row = parent; same row, same parents = sibling.

03 Coded relations & the "photo" lines

"Pointing to a photo" questions hinge on whose relative is described — anchor the sentence on the speaker, then walk outward. "His father is my father's son" with no siblings means the speaker is the father → the boy is his son.

Common trap gotcha

Three classics: "only son/only daughter" is the load-bearing word — it forces the link (the speaker becomes that person, or their sibling). Maternal vs paternal — "mother's brother" is a maternal uncle, a different node than "father's brother". And gender is not always determinable: "X is the child of Y" tells you nothing about X's sex, so "cannot be determined" is a real answer.

A woman says of a man: "He is my mother's only son." How is he related to her?

Her brother. "My mother's son" is a male child of her own mother — i.e. her brother. The speaker is female, so it can't be herself. "Only" just rules out other brothers.

04 Solve it fast

A is B's father. B is C's sister. How is A related to C?warm-up
Solve it

B and C are sisters (same row, shared parents), and A is B's father — so A is also C's father. A is C's father.

The fast way shortcut

Siblings share both parents. The moment "B is C's sister" lands, B and C sit on one row, so anything true of B's parent is true of C's.

P is Q's son. Q is R's wife. How is R related to P?core
Solve it

Q is the mother (R's wife and P's parent). R is married to Q and is P's parent too, so R is P's father.

The fast way shortcut

Draw the marriage line Q──R, drop a line to P. R is the husband on a parent row → father. The "wife" word fixes Q's gender for you.

X is the brother of Y. Y is the sister of Z. How is X related to Z?core
Solve it

X, Y, Z share a row as siblings, and X is explicitly male, so X is Z's brother. Z's own gender doesn't matter here.

The fast way shortcut

"Brother" tells you X is male — that's all you need to name X's link to any sibling. Don't get baited into wondering about Z's sex.
