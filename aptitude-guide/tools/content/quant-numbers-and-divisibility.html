Numbers & divisibility.

The bedrock of every quant section — divisibility shortcuts, HCF/LCM, remainders and last digits. None of it needs long division if you know the tricks.

01 The idea

Every whole number is a product of primes. Once you see that skeleton, divisibility, HCF (shared primes, lowest powers) and LCM (all primes, highest powers) fall out for free.

The exam never wants the long division — it wants you to test divisibility on sight, combine factors, or read a remainder off a repeating cycle.

In plain English analogy

Primes are LEGO bricks; every number is one build. 12 = 2·2·3. HCF is the biggest tower you can make from bricks both numbers own; LCM is the smallest build that contains both towers whole.

HCF × LCM = a × b # two numbers only · find one from the other three

02 Divisibility rules on sight

Don't divide — test. Each rule reads a number's digits, not the whole value. Combine two coprime rules to cover a bigger divisor.

Pro tip tip

To test ÷6, check ÷2 and ÷3. ÷12 = ÷3 and ÷4. ÷15 = ÷3 and ÷5. The trick works only when the two factors are coprime.

Common trap gotcha

"Divisible by 3 and 4 ⇒ divisible by 12" works only because 3 and 4 share no factor. But divisible by 2 and 4 does not give 8 (they overlap) — the product 8 is wrong; the real test is just ÷4. Multiply factors only when they're coprime.

03 HCF & LCM by prime factors

Factorise both numbers. HCF = common primes at their lowest power; LCM = every prime at its highest power. Their product always equals a × b.

24 = 23·3   36 = 22·32  →  HCF = 22·3 = 12,   LCM = 23·32 = 72
12HCF (low powers)
72LCM (high powers)
86412 × 72 = 24 × 36
Common trap gotcha

Don't swap them. HCF ≤ both numbers (a shared chunk); LCM ≥ both (a common multiple). "Largest tile to pave a floor" = HCF; "when do two bells ring together again" = LCM. If your HCF is bigger than the numbers, you've mixed them up.

04 Remainders & last digits

For powers, don't compute the giant number — find the cycle. The unit digit of an repeats every 1–4 powers; the remainder of an ÷ m repeats too.

Pro tip tip

Unit digit of 7n: take n mod 4 → pick from 7,9,3,1. 74 → mod 4 = 0 → last in the cycle = 1. Same idea for remainders: 23 = 8 ≡ 1 (mod 7), so 210 = (23)3·2 ≡ 2.

A number is divisible by both 3 and 4. It must be divisible by:

12. Coprime factors multiply: LCM(3,4) = 12. There's no reason it hits 24, and adding divisors (the "7" trap) is meaningless.

05 Solve it fast

HCF of 24 and 36?warm-up
Solve it

24 = 23·3, 36 = 22·32. Common primes at lowest power: 22·3 = 12.

The fast way shortcut

Subtract-and-repeat (Euclid): 36 − 24 = 12, and 12 divides 24 → HCF = 12. No factorising needed.

Remainder when 210 is divided by 7?core
Solve it

23 = 8 ≡ 1 (mod 7). So 210 = 29·2 = (23)3·2 ≡ 1·2 = 2.

The fast way shortcut

Find the power that gives remainder 1 (here 23), then chip the exponent down to a tiny leftover. Never expand 1024.

Largest 3-digit number divisible by 18?stretch
Solve it

999 ÷ 18 = 55.5, so take 18 × 55 = 990. Check: 990 is even (÷2) and digit sum 18 (÷9) → ÷18. ✓

The fast way shortcut

Divide the ceiling (999) by the divisor, drop the decimal, multiply back. 18 = 2·9, so verify with the ÷2 and ÷9 rules — no trial division.
