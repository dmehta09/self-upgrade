Clocks, calendars, cubes & dice.

Four tiny topics, four tiny formulas. Memorise the degree-per-unit and the counting rules, and these turn into instant marks instead of fiddly arithmetic.

01 The idea

Each of these is just steady motion or fixed counting: hands sweep at a constant rate, days roll forward in 7s, and a painted cube's small cubes are sorted by how many faces show.

No insight required — pick the right rule, plug numbers, done. The win is recalling the four formulas under time pressure.

In plain English analogy

A clock is two runners on a circular 360° track at fixed speeds — the gap between them is just a subtraction. A calendar is an odometer that resets every 7. Treat them as machines with a known speed, and you only ever compute one difference.

02 Clocks: angle = |30H − 5.5M|

The minute hand moves 6°/min (360° in 60). The hour hand moves 30°/hr plus 0.5°/min (it creeps as minutes pass). The angle between them collapses to one formula.

angle = | 30H − 5.5M | # 30H from the hour, 5.5M nets both creeps
3:00 — hands 90° apart
At 3:00: H = 3, M = 0 → |30·3 − 5.5·0| = 90°. The minute hand on 12, the hour hand on 3, a clean right angle.
Common trap gotcha

The hour hand also creeps as minutes pass — that's the −5.5M term, which folds the hour's 0.5°/min into the minute hand's 6°/min. At 3:30 the hour hand is past the 3, so it's not 90°. And if the formula gives more than 180°, the real angle is 360° minus it.

03 Calendars: odd days mod 7

Days of the week cycle every 7, so only the remainder mod 7 — the "odd days" — shifts the weekday. Add the odd days to today's weekday and you've jumped any gap.

future day = today + (gap mod 7) # 15 days → 15 mod 7 = 1 odd day
Odd days
The leftover after removing whole weeks: gap mod 7. A 1-day shift moves Monday → Tuesday.
Ordinary year
365 = 52×7 + 1 → 1 odd day; the same date next year shifts forward by one weekday.
Leap year
366 → 2 odd days. Watch leap years (÷4, but century years only if ÷400).

If today is Monday, what day falls 15 days later?

Tuesday. 15 = 7 + 7 + 1, leaving 1 odd day. The trap rounds "two weeks" to exactly 14 and forgets the spare day.

04 Painted cubes: count by faces shown

Paint a big cube, cut it into n×n×n small cubes — then sort the little cubes by how many painted faces they have. The positions never change, so memorise the four counts.

Pro tip tip

A cube has 8 corners, 12 edges, 6 faces — the counts come straight from that. The four groups must add to n3; use that as a checksum. For a 3×3×3: 8 + 12 + 6 + 1 = 27. ✓

05 Dice: opposite faces

On a standard die, opposite faces sum to 7 (1-6, 2-5, 3-4). For "dice problems" with two snapshots, the face common to both views can't move, so the two side faces from one view are opposite each other.

opposite of x = 7 − x # standard die: 1↔6, 2↔5, 3↔4
Common trap gotcha

The "sum to 7" rule only holds for a standard die — if a question shows dot patterns, derive opposites from the two views instead of assuming 7. Faces that appear together in any single view can never be opposite each other.

06 Solve it fast

How far does the minute hand move in 20 minutes?warm-up
Solve it

The minute hand sweeps 6° per minute. 6 × 20 = 120°.

The fast way shortcut

Minute hand = 6°/min, so 20 min is a third of the dial = 120°. Each 5-minute mark is 30°.

A cube painted on all faces is cut into 27 equal cubes. How many have exactly 3 painted faces?core
Solve it

3 painted faces means a corner cube, and a cube always has 8 corners → 8.

The fast way shortcut

"3 faces" = corners = always 8, for any n. Don't bother with n; the corner count never changes.

On a standard die, what is opposite the face showing 2?stretch
Solve it

Opposite faces sum to 7, so the face opposite 2 is 7 − 2 = 5.

The fast way shortcut

Just compute 7 − x for a standard die. Only fall back to the two-views method when the question gives non-standard dot patterns.
