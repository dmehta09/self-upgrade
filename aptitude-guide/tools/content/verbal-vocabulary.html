Vocabulary.

You'll always meet a word you haven't memorised. The win is decoding it on the spot — from its root, its affixes, and the sentence around it — instead of betting on a hunch.

01 The idea

Don't memorise a dictionary — decode. Most hard words are root + affix: "bene-" = good, "-vore" = eater, "-phile" = lover. Crack the parts and the meaning falls out, even for a brand-new word.

When the root doesn't help, lean on context (the sentence's drift) and connotation (does it feel positive or negative?). Together they eliminate most options.

In plain English analogy

Words are Lego. "Benevolent" snaps together from "bene" (good) + "vol" (wish) — someone who wishes good. Once you know the bricks, you read an unseen word the way you'd guess a function from its name: parse the parts.

Pro tip tip

For one-word substitutions, build from the affix: "a person who eats everything" → omni (all) + vore (eater) = omnivore. The clue's keyword maps straight to a known brick.

03 Context & connotation: the tie-breaker

When two options look close, ask: does the sentence want a positive or negative word? Connotation alone kills the wrong-shade option. Beware false friends — look-alikes that mean different things.

Right shade

"The frugal shopper saved money." Frugal = thrifty (neutral/positive) — fits "saved".

Wrong shade (trap)

"The stingy shopper saved money." Stingy = mean (negative) — same idea, but its sour shade clashes with a praising sentence.

Common trap gotcha

Watch false friends and connotation. "Childish" and "childlike" both relate to children but carry opposite shades (negative vs positive); "ingenious" (clever) is not "ingenuous" (naive). The right answer matches the sentence's positive-or-negative tilt, not just the rough topic.

A "bibliophile" is best described as someone who:

Loves books. "biblio-" = book + "-phile" = lover. "-phobe" would be fearer; "-grapher" would be writer. The affix decides it.

Solve it

Abundant means present in large quantity — i.e. plentiful. plentiful.

The fast way shortcut

Sort by connotation: "abundant" is a "lots of it" word, so drop the "little" options (scarce, tiny, rare) in one sweep. Plentiful is the only "plenty" word left.

"Benevolent" most nearly means?core
Solve it

"bene-" = good, so benevolent = well-meaning, kind. kind.

The fast way shortcut

Spot "bene" → positive word. That instantly rules out "cruel". Wealth/cleverness aren't about goodness, so "kind" stands.

Solve it

"everything" → omni- (all); "eats" → -vore (eater). Combine: omnivore.

The fast way shortcut

Map clue-words to bricks: "all" = omni, "meat" = carni, "plants" = herbi, all + "-vore". "All eater" = omnivore — no memorisation needed.
