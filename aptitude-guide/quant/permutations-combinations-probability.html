Permutations, combinations & probability.

Counting questions are decided by one choice up front: does order matter? Get that right and nPr, nCr, and basic probability all fall out of multiplying choices.

01 The idea

The fundamental counting principle: if step 1 has m ways and step 2 has n ways, together they have m × n ways. Everything else is bookkeeping on top of this.

A factorial n! = n × (n−1) × … × 1 just counts the ways to arrange n distinct items in a line. From there, ordering picks (permutations) or unordered picks (combinations) are two specialisations.

In plain English analogy

Picking an outfit: 3 shirts and 2 jeans give 3 × 2 = 6 outfits — you multiply because each shirt pairs with every pair of jeans. Counting is just chaining "how many choices at this step" together.

total ways = (choices step 1) × (choices step 2) × …  ·  n! = n × (n−1) × … × 1

02 Permutation vs combination

Same pool, one question: does the order count? If yes, it's a permutation (a lineup photo); if no, it's a combination (picking a team). nPr is always bigger — it counts each group r! times.

Permutation — order matters

A "lineup photo": gold, silver, bronze. ABC ≠ BCA. Use nPr. Ranks, seats, passwords, podiums.

Combination — order doesn't

A "team pick": choose 3 players. ABC = BCA, same team. Use nCr. Committees, handshakes, picking a subset.

nPr = n!(n − r)!  ·  nCr = n!r! (n − r)! = nPrr! # nCr = nPr ÷ r!
Common trap gotcha

Decide P vs C before you compute. "How many ways to rank" = permutation; "how many ways to choose" = combination. Words like arrange / seat / order signal P; select / group / committee signal C.

From 5 people, in how many ways can you choose a 2-person committee (no titles)?

10. A committee has no order, so it's ⁵C₂ = 20 ÷ 2! = 10. Picking ⁵P₂ = 20 double-counts each pair (AB and BA), the most common trap.

03 Basic probability — dice, coins, cards

For equally-likely outcomes, P(event) = favourable ÷ total. Count the favourable outcomes, count all outcomes, divide.

P(event) = favourable outcomestotal outcomes # 0 ≤ P ≤ 1
SetupTotal outcomesExample
One die6P(even) = 3/6 = ½
Two coins4 (HH, HT, TH, TT)P(exactly one head) = 2/4 = ½
One coin2P(head) = ½
Deck of cards52P(a king) = 4/52 = 1/13
Common trap gotcha

Check with vs without replacement. Drawing a second card without replacing the first means the total drops to 51 — the denominator changes. "Replacement" keeps every draw at the original count.

04 Solve it fast

In how many ways can the letters of CAT be arranged?warm-up
Solve it

3 distinct letters in a row → 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6.

The fast way shortcut

"Arrange all distinct items" is just n!. For 3 letters that's 6 — no formula juggling. (Repeated letters would divide by the repeats' factorials.)

How many ways to choose 2 students from 5 for a project (no roles)?core
Solve it

Order doesn't matter → ⁵C₂ = (5 × 4) ÷ (2 × 1) = 10.

The fast way shortcut

For nCr, write r factors on top counting down from n and r factors on the bottom: (5×4)/(2×1) = 10. No need to expand full factorials.

Two fair coins are tossed. Probability of exactly one head?stretch
Solve it

Outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT (4 total). Exactly one head = HT, TH = 2 → 2/4 = 1⁄2.

The fast way shortcut

List the 4 outcomes once; "exactly one head" picks HT and TH = 2/4 = ½. Don't confuse it with "at least one head" (that's 3/4).
