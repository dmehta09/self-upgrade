LLD case studies · the method

Low-level design, as a repeatable method.

A "design a parking lot" prompt feels open-ended and scary. It isn't — there's a process. This page gives you a six-step recipe that turns any vague brief into classes, relationships, and runnable code, the same way every time.

01  What is LLD (and what the interview wants)

Design happens at two altitudes. High-level design (HLD) is the city map: which services exist, how they talk, where data lives, how it scales. Low-level design (LLD) is the floor plan of one building: the classes, their methods, and how they relate. LLD is where OOP, SOLID, and the design patterns you've learned finally earn their keep.

City map vs floor plan analogy

HLD is the urban planner deciding where the hospital, school, and roads go. LLD is the architect drawing the rooms, doors, and wiring of one house. An LLD interview hands you one "house" — a parking lot, a vending machine, an elevator — and asks you to draw its rooms in code.

High-level design (HLD)Low-level design (LLD)
Unit of thoughtServices, datastores, queuesClasses, methods, objects
Typical questionsHow does it scale? Where's the bottleneck?Which classes? Who owns what behavior?
DeliverableBox-and-arrow architecture diagramUML class diagram + working code
Skills testedSystems, trade-offs, networkingOOP, SOLID, patterns, clean code

An LLD round (often called a machine-coding round) usually asks for three things: a short list of the core classes with clear responsibilities, a class diagram showing how they connect, and code for the key flows that compiles and runs a simple scenario. You are not asked to handle every edge case — you're asked to model the domain cleanly and extensibly.

What "good" looks like

Interviewers reward a clean object model over a feature-complete one. Sensible class names, one responsibility per class, and an obvious place to add the next feature beat a sprawling pile of if/else every time. Breadth of edge cases is secondary to a tidy core.

02  The six-step method

Whenever you're handed a prompt, walk these six steps out loud. They take you from a fuzzy sentence to a class diagram and a code skeleton without ever staring at a blank page.

1

Clarify requirements & scope

Never start coding on assumptions. Ask what's in and out of scope, pin down the must-haves, and write them down. This is always the first move.

→ output: a short bullet list of features
2

Identify the core entities — extract the NOUNS

Underline the nouns in the requirements. "A customer parks a vehicle in a spot and gets a ticket." Nouns become your candidate classes (and enums).

→ output: a list of candidate classes
3

Assign responsibilities — the VERBS, one job per class

Underline the verbs: park, pay, issue ticket. Each becomes a method, placed on the class that owns that data. Aim for one clear responsibility per class (the Single Responsibility Principle).

→ output: methods on the right classes
4

Map relationships & sketch the class diagram

For each pair, ask "is-a or has-a?" A ParkingLot has spots (composition); an HourlyRate is a PricingStrategy (interface). Draw the UML boxes and connectors.

→ output: a UML class diagram
5

Apply SOLID & pick patterns where something varies

Find the axis of change. Pricing might change? That's a Strategy. Object creation gets messy? A Factory. Reach for a pattern because something varies, never to show off.

→ output: chosen patterns, justified
6

Code the skeleton & walk a scenario

Write the enums, dataclasses, and key methods. Then run one concrete story end-to-end ("park a car, leave after 3 hours, pay the fee") to prove the pieces line up.

→ output: runnable code + a demo run
Nouns → classes, verbs → methods

This single trick (sometimes called noun–verb analysis) does most of the heavy lifting. Read the requirements as English, circle the nouns for your classes and the verbs for your methods, and the skeleton almost falls out on its own.

03  Clarifying questions to always ask

Step 1 lives or dies on the questions you ask. A vague prompt is an invitation, not a trap — the interviewer wants you to scope it. Here are the five buckets that pin down almost any brief:

🎯

Functional scope

What must the system actually do? "Park and unpark" — but also reserve spots? Handle payments? List the verbs you're on the hook for.

⭐

Must-have vs nice-to-have

Which features are core for today, and which can be "out of scope, but I'd extend it like X"? This protects your time.

📏

Scale & constraints

One floor or many? Tens of spots or thousands? Single-threaded demo or concurrent? Constraints steer your data structures.

🧑

Actors

Who uses it? A driver, an admin, an attendant? Each actor implies use-cases — and those use-cases become methods.

🔀

What varies / changes?

Where will requirements shift later — pricing rules, vehicle types, payment methods? Those axes of change are exactly where a pattern pays off.

📤

Inputs & outputs

What does the system return — a ticket object, a receipt, a boolean? Naming the boundary types early keeps your method signatures honest.

Don't ask forever

Two or three crisp questions, then state your assumptions and move on: "I'll assume one flat lot with three spot sizes and pay-on-exit — flag me if that's wrong." Endless questioning reads as indecision; bounded scoping reads as senior.

04  Two-minute worked example: a deck of cards

Let's run the method on a tiny prompt — "model a deck of playing cards you can shuffle and draw from" — just to feel the gears turn. This is a method demo, not a full case study, so we'll keep it small.

Step 1 — requirements: a standard 52-card deck; you can shuffle it and draw the top card. Step 2 — nouns: Card, Suit, Rank, Deck. Step 3 — verbs: shuffle() and draw() live on Deck (it owns the cards). Step 4 — relationships: a Deck has 52 Cards (composition); a Card has a Suit and a Rank. Step 5: nothing varies here, so no pattern is warranted — adding one would be gold-plating. Step 6 — code it:

deck.py
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass
from enum import Enum

class Suit(Enum):
    CLUBS = "♣"; DIAMONDS = "♦"; HEARTS = "♥"; SPADES = "♠"

class Rank(Enum):
    TWO = 2; THREE = 3; FOUR = 4; FIVE = 5; SIX = 6; SEVEN = 7
    EIGHT = 8; NINE = 9; TEN = 10; JACK = 11; QUEEN = 12; KING = 13; ACE = 14

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Card:
    rank: Rank
    suit: Suit
    def __str__(self) -> str:
        return f"{self.rank.name.title()} of {self.suit.name.title()}"

class Deck:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self._cards = [Card(r, s) for s in Suit for r in Rank]  # 52 cards

    def shuffle(self) -> None:
        random.shuffle(self._cards)

    def draw(self) -> Card:
        return self._cards.pop()        # take the top card

deck = Deck()
random.seed(7)                          # deterministic for the demo
deck.shuffle()
print(f"{len(deck._cards)} cards, top is: {deck.draw()}")
Output52 cards, top is: Nine of Diamonds

Notice how mechanical that was. We never agonized over a blank page — the nouns gave us classes, the verbs gave us methods, and "nothing varies" told us to skip the pattern. The real case studies are just this, scaled up.

You're handed a vague prompt: "Design a ride-sharing app's matching." What's your first move?

Right: step 1 is always clarify-then-assume. Coding or choosing patterns before you know the scope means designing for a problem you only imagined.

In noun–verb analysis, the nouns in the requirements most directly map to ___.

Right: nouns become classes (and enums); verbs become the methods on them. It's the fastest way to turn an English brief into an object model.

05  Interview tips

The method gets you a good design; these habits get you a good interview. The room is judging how you think as much as what you produce.

Think out loud
Narrate every step. "I see vehicle and spot as nouns, so those are classes." Silence hides your reasoning — and reasoning is what's being scored.
Start simple, then extend
Build the smallest thing that works (one floor, one pricing rule), then layer features. A working core you can grow beats an unfinished palace.
Manage your time
Budget: ~5 min clarifying, ~10 min entities + diagram, the rest coding. If you're sinking into an edge case, say "I'll stub this and come back."
Prefer composition
Wire objects together with has-a relationships rather than deep inheritance trees. Swappable parts keep the design flexible and easy to talk about.
Name things well
ParkingSpot.is_free reads better than spot.flag. Good names are documentation and signal that you've thought about the domain.
Don't gold-plate
No pattern without a reason, no feature outside scope. Over-engineering a simple brief is a red flag, not a flex.
Leave a hook for the next feature

End by pointing at the seam: "If we needed reserved spots, I'd add a ReservationStrategy right here." Showing where change would go proves your design is open for extension — exactly what the Open/Closed Principle is about.

The most common failure

Jumping straight to code with no diagram and no scoping. You end up with one giant class doing everything, and no story for how it grows. Spend the first third of the interview on steps 1–4 — the coding goes far faster once the model is clear.
