Advanced case · capstone

Case study: ride-hailing matching.

A rider taps "find me a ride." We need the nearest free driver, fast — even with thousands of drivers on the map. That's three problems at once: find candidates quickly (a geospatial grid index), pick one by a swappable rule (a matching Strategy), and assign them atomically so no driver gets handed to two riders. This is the capstone — it pulls together Strategy, the grid, and the concurrency from the earlier cases.

01  Step 1 — Requirements & assumptions

Drivers move around the map and report a live (x, y) position. A rider requests a ride at some location, and we must match the nearest available driver — fast, even when there are many drivers — and never assign one driver to two riders.

Clarifying questions I'd ask: What scale — hundreds of drivers, or millions across a city? Do we match on raw distance or on ETA (which respects roads and traffic)? Is there surge pricing that should bias the match? How do driver positions reach us — a stream of updates, or do we read them on demand?

Assumptions, so the core is clear

We work on a flat 2-D plane with simple coordinates instead of real lat/long — straight-line distance, no map projection. Everything is in-memory in one process. Driver positions are passed in (we don't model the GPS pipeline). Those simplifications let the design's three ideas — grid, strategy, atomic assignment — stand on their own.

In scope

A grid index for fast "who's near here?" lookup, a pluggable matching strategy (nearest first), and a dispatcher that claims a driver atomically so two riders can't grab the same one.

Out of scope

Real road networks and ETA routing, surge pricing, the GPS ingestion pipeline, persistence across restarts, multi-process sharding, and payments — all noted as extensions, not built here.

02  Step 2 — The geospatial grid index

The naive answer to "who's near (x, y)?" is to measure the distance to every driver and keep the closest. That's O(n) per request — fine for ten drivers, hopeless when thousands of riders each scan thousands of drivers every second.

A grid index fixes this. Carve the plane into square cells of a fixed size and bucket each driver into the cell their position falls in. Now "who's near (x, y)?" only has to look at the rider's own cell plus its 8 neighbours — the closest driver can't be hiding more than one cell away if the cell is sized to your search radius. Instead of scanning n drivers you inspect a handful, so nearby lookup becomes roughly O(1).

Cell size
The width of each square. Bigger cells mean fewer, fatter buckets (more drivers to check per cell); smaller cells mean more, thinner buckets. Tune it to the typical search radius.
The bucket map
A dict from (cx, cy) cell coordinates to the set of drivers in that cell. Empty cells simply don't appear — no wasted space for the empty map.
The 9-cell scan
Given a query point, compute its cell, then gather drivers from that cell and the eight around it. Anything truly nearby is guaranteed to live in one of those nine.
A city divided into blocks analogy

Think of the map as a city laid out in blocks. To hail a nearby cab you don't survey the whole city — you look down your own block and the ones touching it. The grid is exactly that: the cell is your block, the 8 neighbours are the blocks next door, and everything beyond is too far to bother checking.

03  Step 3 — The matching Strategy + atomic assignment

Once the grid hands us a small list of candidate drivers, which one do we pick? That rule shouldn't be wired into the dispatcher — today it's "nearest available," but tomorrow the business might want rating-weighted matching, or ETA-based (account for traffic), or a surge-aware choice. So we make it a Strategy: an interface with one choose(drivers, x, y) method, and a NearestDriver implementation to start. Swapping the rule never touches the dispatcher.

But picking a driver isn't the whole story — we have to claim them, and that's where concurrency bites. Two riders near the same corner can both have their requests in flight at once. Both call the grid, both see the same free driver, both pick them, and both think they won. The driver gets double-assigned. This is the classic check-then-act race: "find a free driver" and "mark them taken" must happen as one indivisible step. We wrap exactly those two lines in a single threading.Lock so only one rider can be inside the choose-and-claim section at a time (the same critical-section idea from the concurrency primer).

Choose and claim are one operation, not two

If you only lock the "mark taken" line but choose outside the lock, you've fixed nothing — both threads already chose the same driver before either claimed. The lock must span both the selection and the available = False write. Lock, choose, mark, release.

04  Step 4 — The implementation

Three small files. grid.py is the spatial index; matching.py is the pluggable rule; dispatcher.py ties them together and owns the atomic claim.

grid.py
import threading

class Driver:
    def __init__(self, id, x, y):
        self.id = id
        self.x = x
        self.y = y
        self.available = True

class GridIndex:                            # bucket drivers into square cells
    def __init__(self, cell=1.0):
        self.cell = cell
        self.cells = {}                     # (cx, cy) -> set of drivers
        self._lock = threading.Lock()

    def _key(self, x, y):
        return (int(x // self.cell), int(y // self.cell))

    def add(self, driver):
        with self._lock:
            bucket = self.cells.setdefault(self._key(driver.x, driver.y), set())
            bucket.add(driver)

    def nearby(self, x, y):                 # this cell + its 8 neighbours
        cx, cy = self._key(x, y)
        found = []
        with self._lock:
            for dx in (-1, 0, 1):
                for dy in (-1, 0, 1):
                    found += list(self.cells.get((cx + dx, cy + dy), set()))
        return found
matching.py
from math import dist
from typing import Protocol

class MatchStrategy(Protocol):
    def choose(self, drivers, x, y): ...        # return a Driver or None

class NearestDriver:
    def choose(self, drivers, x, y):
        free = [d for d in drivers if d.available]
        if not free:
            return None
        return min(free, key=lambda d: dist((d.x, d.y), (x, y)))
dispatcher.py
import threading

class Dispatcher:
    def __init__(self, index, strategy):
        self.index = index
        self.strategy = strategy
        self._lock = threading.Lock()

    def request_ride(self, x, y):
        candidates = self.index.nearby(x, y)
        with self._lock:                        # claim a driver atomically
            driver = self.strategy.choose(candidates, x, y)
            if driver is None:
                return None
            driver.available = False            # mark taken INSIDE the lock
            return driver
Why the lock wraps both lines

Notice the dispatcher reads candidates from the grid before taking the lock — that's fine, the snapshot can be slightly stale. What must be inside the lock is choose and the available = False write together. That pair is the critical section: between selecting a driver and marking them taken, no other thread may run, so the same driver can never be handed out twice.

05  Walk a scenario

Three drivers on the map; a rider waits at the origin. D2 sits far away in a different cell, so the grid never even returns it — and between the two nearby drivers, the closer one wins.

demo.py
index = GridIndex(cell=1.0)
index.add(Driver("D1", 0.2, 0.2))
index.add(Driver("D2", 5.0, 5.0))            # far away, different cell
index.add(Driver("D3", 0.8, 0.1))
dispatcher = Dispatcher(index, NearestDriver())

driver = dispatcher.request_ride(0.0, 0.0)   # rider at the origin
print(f"Matched {driver.id}")
print(f"{driver.id} available now: {driver.available}")
OutputMatched D1 D1 available now: False

The grid's nearby(0, 0) returns D1 and D3 — both fall in the origin cell — but not D2, five units away in another cell. NearestDriver then measures: D1 at distance ~0.28 beats D3 at ~0.81. The dispatcher claims D1 inside the lock and flips its available to False, so the next rider can't be matched to it.

Why a grid index instead of scanning every driver for each request?

Right: the grid buckets drivers by location, so a request only gathers candidates from 9 cells — independent of how many drivers exist overall. You still measure distances, but only on a handful of candidates, not the whole fleet. Scanning all n per request is the O(n)-per-request trap the grid avoids.

Why wrap "choose a driver" and "mark them unavailable" in one lock?

Right: choose-then-mark is a read-modify-write on shared state. If selection happens outside the lock, two threads can both pick the same driver before either writes available = False. Locking the pair makes it indivisible, so exactly one rider claims any given driver.

06  Extensions — your turn

Exercise 1 · a new strategy

Add a RatingWeightedStrategy (or a surge-aware one) that prefers closer and higher-rated drivers. The point: it should drop in without editing Dispatcher at all.

Hint: it implements the same choose(drivers, x, y) method. Combine distance and rating into a single score and pick the minimum — the dispatcher neither knows nor cares which strategy it holds.

Show a solution solution
rating_strategy.py
from math import dist

class RatingWeightedStrategy:                # same choose() contract
    def __init__(self, w=0.5):
        self.w = w                          # how much rating counts

    def choose(self, drivers, x, y):
        free = [d for d in drivers if d.available]
        if not free:
            return None
        # lower is better: distance, minus a bonus for high rating
        def score(d):
            return dist((d.x, d.y), (x, y)) - self.w * d.rating
        return min(free, key=score)

# dispatcher = Dispatcher(index, RatingWeightedStrategy(w=0.3))

Because Dispatcher depends only on the MatchStrategy contract, the new rule plugs straight in — that's the Open/Closed payoff. Surge pricing would be the same shape: another strategy that weights the score by a per-cell surge multiplier.

Exercise 2 · batch vs greedy, and live positions

Two design questions. (a) We match greedily — first request served, first driver claimed. What does batch matching (collect a tick's worth of requests, then assign globally) buy you, and what does it cost? (b) Drivers move constantly. How would you keep the grid fresh as positions change?

Show a solution solution

(a) Batch vs greedy. Greedy assigns each request the instant it arrives — lowest latency, but a locally optimal choice can be globally poor (rider A grabs the only driver that was perfect for rider B, who now waits much longer). Batch matching buffers all requests in a short window (a "tick"), then solves the assignment together — minimising total wait across everyone (a bipartite-matching / assignment problem). The trade-off is plain: batching is more efficient globally but adds latency equal to the window, since the first request waits for the tick to close. Real systems pick a window small enough that riders don't notice.

(b) Live positions as an Observer. Treat a driver's location update as an event the grid observes. When a driver moves, the grid checks whether the new position falls in a different cell; if so it removes the driver from the old bucket and adds it to the new one — a re-bucket. The driver is the subject; the grid is the observer that reacts to each move. Most updates stay within the same cell and cost nothing, so re-bucketing is cheap and keeps "who's near here?" always correct.

What this design used — the toolkit recap

Geospatial grid index — bucket by cell so nearby lookup is ~O(1) instead of scanning the fleet. Strategy — a pluggable choose rule (nearest now, rating- or surge-weighted later) the dispatcher never has to know about. Atomic assignment — a single Lock around choose-and-claim kills the double-booking race. Observer — driver moves re-bucket their grid cell. And it composes the earlier advanced cases: Strategy from the rate limiter and Splitwise, the critical-section locking from movie booking and the concurrency primer, the Observer from the Pub/Sub queue — one design, every tool.

That's the capstone. You've now built a matcher that's fast, swappable, and correct under concurrency — the three things that separate a toy from a real dispatcher. From here, keep the whole toolkit at hand in the glossary & cheatsheet.
