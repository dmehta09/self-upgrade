Machine-coding drills.

Reading a case study and doing one against a clock are different skills. Each drill here is a 45-minute LLD interview: five phases, interviewer-style nudges, a senior grading rubric, and a model answer at the end. Your scores stay on this device.

01  How a 45-minute loop runs

Real machine-coding rounds reward pacing as much as design. The drill walks you through the same five phases an interviewer expects, with a per-phase budget that auto-advances when time is up:

PhaseBudgetWhat the interviewer is checking
Requirements5 minDo you pin scope and name the invariants before touching code?
Entities & API10 minClean classes, enums, and a small public API with real signatures.
UML & patterns10 minComposition vs inheritance, and patterns named with reasons.
Core code15 minThe 2–3 hardest methods actually written — not stubs.
Extensions & wrap5 minA scenario walkthrough, honest thread-safety notes, what's next.

Work on paper, a whiteboard, or a blank editor — the drill is your interviewer and timekeeper, not your notepad. Hints reveal only if you ask, exactly like nudges in a real round.

The senior bar

Aim for 80%+ on a cold run (no peeking at the lesson the same day). If a phase keeps running over budget, that phase — not the whole problem — is what to practice next.

03  How to self-grade honestly

The rubric only works if you grade what you actually produced, not what you know you could have said. Three rules keep the signal honest:

  • Out loud or on paper counts; in your head doesn't. If you thought "pricing should be a Strategy" but never wrote or said it, the line stays unticked — interviewers can't hear thoughts either.
  • Code lines need code. "I'd use a dict and a linked list" doesn't tick a core-code line; the written _unlink() does.
  • Re-run cold, days later. A score earned right after reading the lesson measures memory; the same score a week later measures skill. The card tracks your best — make the best one a cold one.
Suggested ladder

Warm-ups until both are 85%+ → Core in any order → Advanced once Core feels boring → Expert last, they assume the concurrency lessons. One drill per sitting beats four in a row; spacing is the point.
