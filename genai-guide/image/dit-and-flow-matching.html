Image & diffusion · the 2024–2026 stack

DiT & flow matching IV NEW

The diffusion idea hasn't changed — but two big swaps reshaped the modern image stack. The denoiser stopped being a U-Net and became a transformer (a DiT). And the noising path got straightened from a wandering curve into a straight line (flow matching / rectified flow), so you reach a great image in far fewer steps. Together they're why text rendering and prompt adherence jumped so sharply in 2024–2026.

01  From U-Net to Diffusion Transformer (DiT) NEW

Early diffusion used a U-Net as the denoiser — a convolutional network shaped like a “U” that downsamples an image to capture the big picture, then upsamples back to full detail, with skip connections across. It worked beautifully but, being convolutional, it was built around local neighborhoods and scaled awkwardly.

The DiT (Diffusion Transformer) throws that out and reuses the transformer you already know from LLMs. Chop the latent grid into patches, treat each patch as a token, and let self-attention mix information globally across all patches at every layer. The lesson from text — transformers scale predictably with data and compute — carries straight over to images.

Treat the image like a sentence of patches analogy

A U-Net is like reading a page through a magnifying glass — sharp locally, but you shuffle around to see how far-apart regions relate. A DiT lays all the patches on a table and lets every patch talk to every other one at once, the way attention lets every word consult every other word. Global relationships (a hand matching its arm, text staying coherent) become first-class.

MM-DiT (multimodal DiT, the SD3-era design) goes further: instead of bolting text on via one-way cross-attention, it carries image patches and text tokens as two streams with their own weights and lets them attend to each other bidirectionally. Image can read text and text can read image — which is a big reason prompt adherence and in-image text rendering improved.

Same diffusion training objective underneath — DiT just replaces what network does the denoising. You can describe it as “Vision-Transformer-style backbone for the denoiser.”

03  Flow matching & rectified flow: straighten the path NEW

Classic diffusion's path from noise to image is a wiggly curve, so the sampler has to take many small steps to follow it accurately. Flow matching reframes generation as learning a velocity field — at any point between noise and data, which direction and how fast should I move? Rectified flow trains that field so the path is as close to a straight line as possible.

A winding road vs a straight highway analogy

Old diffusion is a winding mountain road from “noise town” to “image town”: to stay on it you crawl around every bend (many steps). Rectified flow bulldozes a straight highway between the two. A straight line needs far fewer waypoints to traverse accurately — so you reach the destination in a handful of big steps instead of dozens of timid ones.

Flow matching learns the arrow at every point; rectified flow makes those arrows line up into a near-straight path, which a solver can follow in few steps. This is the SD3-era training recipe, paired with the MM-DiT backbone.

It's an evolution of diffusion, not a different universe — same “noise ↔ data” spirit, just a straighter, easier-to-integrate trajectory. As of June 2026 it's the prevailing recipe for frontier image models, though plain diffusion formulations are still used too.

04  Consistency models: few-step, near-real-time NEW

Even a straight path still takes several steps. Consistency models push toward one or two. The trick: train the model so that from any point along the noise-to-image trajectory it jumps directly to the same final image — every point is “consistent” about the destination. If every point maps to the endpoint, you don't need to walk the path at all; you teleport.

A GPS that always names the final destination analogy

Step-by-step diffusion is like a GPS giving one turn at a time. A consistency model is a GPS that, from anywhere on the route, just tells you the final address directly — so you can jump there in one move. Often you take a couple of jumps to clean up, but it's steps you count on one hand, not dozens.

These are frequently produced by distilling a slow many-step model into a fast few-step one. The payoff is interactive, near-real-time generation — type and watch the image form — and they're a key ingredient behind live image and video tools in 2026.

Fewer steps can cost a little quality gotcha

One-/few-step generation is a speed-vs-fidelity trade. Heavily distilled models can lose a touch of fine detail or diversity versus the slow teacher. Teams often keep a fast few-step model for previews and a slower high-step pass for finals — don't claim few-step is strictly better.

05  Why text & prompt adherence jumped in 2024–2026 NEW

Older models famously mangled in-image text (“B1RTHDAY”) and dropped details from long prompts. Several of this module's threads combined to fix that:

Global attention (DiT)
Every patch attends to every other, so letters stay coherent across the image and parts stay consistent — convolutions struggled with this long-range glue.
Joint text+image streams (MM-DiT)
Bidirectional attention between prompt tokens and image patches binds described attributes to the right regions, sharply improving prompt adherence.
Stronger text encoders
Bigger/multiple text encoders (incl. full T5-style language models alongside CLIP) give the model a richer understanding of long, compositional prompts.
Autoregressive image stacks
Some 2025–26 systems generate images as sequences of tokens — like writing “patches of characters” — which is especially good at spelling. Expect more hybrid AR+diffusion designs. as of June 2026

So “why did text rendering get good?” isn't one cause — it's transformers + joint conditioning + better text encoders (and, in some models, autoregressive generation) landing together.

06  What changed: U-Net diffusion → DiT + flow matching

The summary an interviewer is fishing for, in one breath: “The backbone changed from a convolutional U-Net to a transformer (DiT/MM-DiT) for global attention and clean scaling, and the training objective changed from curved diffusion paths to straight-line flow matching / rectified flow for fewer sampling steps — with consistency models distilling that down to near-real-time. Same noise-to-image principle; better backbone and a straighter path.”

How to say it out loud say it

“Modern image models swapped the U-Net denoiser for a Diffusion Transformer — patches as tokens, global attention, predictable scaling — and MM-DiT carries text and image as joint streams. They also straightened the generation path with flow matching / rectified flow, so you need far fewer steps, and consistency models distill that to one or a few steps for real-time use.”

07  Self-check

What's the main reason rectified flow / flow matching needs fewer sampling steps than classic diffusion?
Flow matching learns a velocity field; rectified flow straightens the noise-to-data trajectory. A near-straight line needs few waypoints to follow accurately, so you can take big steps. Latent size and text encoders are separate concerns — flow matching is about the shape of the path.
“What is a DiT, and why replace the U-Net?”Core
Model answer

A DiT is a Diffusion Transformer: the denoiser is a transformer that treats the latent as a sequence of patch tokens and uses self-attention, instead of a convolutional U-Net. You replace the U-Net because attention gives global context at every layer and, crucially, transformers scale predictably — more parameters and data reliably help, the way they do for LLMs. MM-DiT extends it so text and image patches are joint, bidirectional streams, which boosts prompt adherence.

How to say it out loud say it

“It's a transformer denoiser over image patches — global attention and clean scaling beat the convolutional U-Net. MM-DiT makes text and image joint streams for better adherence.”

“How do consistency models get to one or two steps?”Stretch
Model answer

They're trained so that any point along the noise-to-image trajectory maps directly to the same endpoint — the clean image — rather than only to the next point. Since every point “knows” the destination, you can jump there in a single evaluation (or a couple to refine). They're often distilled from a slow many-step teacher. The trade-off is a possible small loss of detail or diversity versus the teacher, so heavy few-step models are great for previews and real-time, with an optional slower pass for finals.

“Train every point on the path to map straight to the final image, so you can jump in one step instead of walking. Usually distilled from a slow model; trades a little fidelity for real-time speed.”
