Image & diffusion · core mechanism

How diffusion works IV NEW

Diffusion is the engine inside nearly every image (and video) generator today. The trick is almost suspiciously simple: take real images and gradually destroy them with noise until they're static — then train a network to run that process backwards. Once it can undo noise, you hand it pure noise and a prompt, and it hallucinates a brand-new picture into existence. Let's make every piece of that precise.

01  The big idea: learn to undo noise

Generating a perfect image from nothing is too hard to learn directly. So diffusion splits it into a long chain of tiny, learnable steps. Training has two directions: a fixed forward process that adds noise (we don't learn this — we just define it), and a learned reverse process that removes it.

A photo dissolving in the rain — played in reverse analogy

Picture a photo left out in the rain: drop by drop the image smears until it's a grey blur. That's the forward process — easy, automatic, destructive. Now imagine playing the film backwards: the blur resolves, drop by drop, back into a sharp photo. Diffusion trains a model to be that rewind button. Learning to take one small step back toward clarity is easy; chain enough of them and you reconstruct a whole image from noise.

02  Forward (noising) vs reverse (denoising)

The forward process takes a clean image and adds a small amount of Gaussian noise, repeatedly, over many timesteps (often hundreds). By the final step the image is indistinguishable from pure random noise. There's nothing to learn here — it's a fixed recipe — but it gives us free training data: at every step we know exactly the clean image, the noisy image, and the noise we added.

The reverse process is what the network learns: given a noisy image and which timestep it's at, take one step back toward clean. Do that from step T down to step 0 and noise becomes an image.

Reverse / sampling runs this right-to-left: from pure noise back to a clean image, one learned denoising step at a time.

Forward and reverse share the same chain of timesteps. Forward is the teacher (it makes the noisy examples); reverse is the student (it learns to walk back).

03  The denoiser predicts the noise

Here's the detail that surprises people. The network isn't trained to output a clean image directly. It's trained to look at a noisy image and predict the noise that was added. Subtract that predicted noise and you get a cleaner image — your step backward. (Predicting the noise, predicting the clean image, or predicting the “velocity” are mathematically interchangeable framings; noise-prediction is the classic one.)

Training is therefore a simple, stable regression: add known noise to a real image, ask the model to guess that noise, and penalize the difference. No adversary, no game — just “how close was your noise estimate?” That stability is precisely why diffusion trains where GANs struggled.

diffusion_train_step.py
t = random_timestep()                 # pick a noise level
noise = gaussian_like(image)          # the target to predict
x_t = sqrt(a[t]) * image + sqrt(1 - a[t]) * noise   # forward: add noise

pred = model(x_t, t, text_embedding)  # model guesses the noise
loss = mse(pred, noise)               # how wrong was the guess?
loss.backward()                       # that's the whole objective

At sampling time you flip it: start from x_T = pure noise, repeatedly call model to estimate the noise, subtract a portion, and step down through the timesteps until you reach x₀.

04  Sampling schedules: DDPM vs DDIM

How many steps, and how big, do you take on the way back down? That's the sampler (or scheduler), and it's a quality-vs-speed dial you can change without retraining the model.

DDPM
The original sampler. Stochastic (adds a touch of fresh randomness each step) and faithful, but slow — often hundreds of steps for a clean image.
DDIM
A deterministic re-derivation of the same model that takes far bigger, smarter steps — good images in tens of steps, and the same seed reproduces the same image. The workhorse for fast sampling.

05  Latent diffusion: the efficiency unlock NEW

Running all those denoising steps on a full-resolution pixel grid (say 1024×1024×3 ≈ 3 million numbers) every step is wildly expensive. Latent diffusion — the idea that made Stable Diffusion practical on consumer GPUs — moves the whole process into a compressed space.

Borrow a VAE: its encoder shrinks the image to a small latent grid (commonly ~8× smaller per side, so dozens of times fewer numbers), diffusion runs entirely in that latent space, and the VAE decoder restores full pixels at the end. Same algorithm, a fraction of the compute.

Pixels only appear at the very end. Everything expensive happens in the small latent grid — and the prompt steers the denoiser the whole way (see conditioning & control).
“Latent” isn't a smaller picture gotcha

A latent isn't a thumbnail you could view. It's a learned, compressed representation — a grid of feature vectors the VAE understands. Don't say diffusion “works on a low-res image.” Say it works in a learned latent space, and the decoder reconstructs full detail from it.

06  Classifier-free guidance: the prompt-adherence dial NEW

Left alone, a prompt nudges the denoiser only gently, and images can wander off-prompt. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is the now-standard trick that lets you turn up prompt obedience.

At each denoising step the model makes two noise predictions: one with your prompt and one without (unconditional). The difference between them points in the direction of “more like the prompt.” You then push the prediction along that direction by a multiplier called the guidance scale. Higher scale = stronger adherence.

Two sketches and an exaggeration dial analogy

Ask an artist to draw “a red bicycle,” and separately to draw “whatever.” Compare the two: the difference is everything that makes it bicycle-and-red. Now tell them, “exaggerate that difference 7×.” You get a very on-prompt picture. Crank it to 20× and it becomes a garish, over-saturated caricature. CFG is exactly that exaggeration knob.

More guidance isn't always better gotcha

Guidance scale trades diversity and realism for prompt fidelity. Too low and the image ignores you; too high and you get over-saturated, contrast-blown, artifact-y images and less variety. There's a sweet spot (often a single-digit value). Also note it roughly doubles per-step cost, since you run the model twice — though flow-matching models often need much less of it.

07  Diffusion in two sentences

The compression an interviewer wants: “Diffusion defines a fixed forward process that gradually turns an image into pure noise, and trains a network to reverse it by predicting and removing the noise at each step. To generate, you start from random noise and run that learned denoiser down through the timesteps — in a compressed latent space for efficiency, with classifier-free guidance to control how strongly the prompt is obeyed.”

How to say it out loud say it

“Add noise to images in a fixed forward process; train a network to predict that noise so it can step backward. Generate by denoising from pure noise down to an image. Run it in a VAE latent space to be cheap, pick a sampler like DDIM for speed, and use classifier-free guidance to dial prompt adherence.”

08  Self-check

During training, what does a standard diffusion network actually learn to output?
The classic objective is noise-prediction: given a noisy image and its timestep, predict the noise that was added (a stable MSE regression). Subtracting a portion of that prediction is one reverse step. Predicting the image directly one-shot is what fails; the real/fake score is a GAN discriminator.
“Why is latent diffusion so much cheaper than pixel diffusion?”Core
Model answer

Because the expensive part — running the denoiser over many steps — happens on a much smaller array. A VAE compresses the image roughly 8× per side, so the latent grid has dozens of times fewer values than the pixel image. You pay the pixel cost only twice (encode once, decode once) instead of on every one of the many diffusion steps. Same algorithm, far less compute and memory.

How to say it out loud say it

“It diffuses in a compressed VAE latent — dozens of times smaller than pixels — so the many denoising steps are cheap. Pixels are only touched at encode and decode.”

“What does the guidance scale control, and what breaks at extremes?”Core
Model answer

It controls how strongly the image follows the prompt. CFG computes a conditional and an unconditional noise prediction each step and amplifies their difference by the guidance scale. Too low and the image drifts off-prompt; too high and you over-saturate, blow out contrast, add artifacts, and lose diversity. The cost is running the model twice per step. Sweet spot is usually a modest single-digit value.

How to say it out loud say it

“It's the prompt-adherence dial: amplify the gap between the prompt-conditioned and unconditional predictions. Low ignores the prompt, high over-cooks the image and kills diversity.”
