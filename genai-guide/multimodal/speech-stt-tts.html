Speech: STT & TTS IV NEW

Speech is two mirror-image jobs. STT (speech-to-text, a.k.a. ASR) is the model's ears: turn a soundwave into words. TTS (text-to-speech) is its voice: turn words into a natural-sounding soundwave. Chain ears → brain → voice and you get a voice assistant; in 2026 the frontier collapses that chain into a single model that listens and speaks in one breath.

01  Ears and a voice

Every speech feature is built from these two halves. Keep them straight and most of the topic falls into place:

STT / ASR (ears)
Audio in → text out. Transcription, captions, voice commands, meeting notes. “ASR” (automatic speech recognition) and “STT” mean the same thing.
TTS (voice)
Text in → audio out. Read-aloud, narration, the spoken side of a voice agent. Modern TTS sounds eerily human.
A court stenographer and a voice actor analogy

STT is the stenographer: someone speaks and they write down every word, ideally noting who said what. TTS is the voice actor: hand them a script and they perform it aloud, with the right pacing and emotion. A voice assistant is a stenographer and a voice actor working back-to-back with a thinker in between — and the newest models fuse all three into one performer.

02  STT: the Whisper lineage NEW

Modern transcription is a transformer job. The audio is sliced into short frames, turned into a spectrogram-like representation, and fed to an encoder-decoder model that outputs text tokens — the same architecture that powers translation. The open Whisper family popularised this recipe and most current systems descend from it, trained on enormous multilingual, noisy, real-world audio so they're robust to accents and background noise.

Streaming vs batch
Batch transcribes a whole finished file — highest accuracy, no rush (podcasts, archives). Streaming emits words as you speak, with sub-second latency for live captions and voice agents — and must cope with not yet knowing how the sentence ends.
Diarization
“Who spoke when.” Diarization segments the audio by speaker and labels turns (Speaker 1 / Speaker 2). It's a separate step layered on top of transcription — getting the words right and getting the speaker right are different problems.
Word timestamps
Aligning each word to a moment in the audio — essential for subtitles and for letting a voice agent be interrupted cleanly.
STT models can “hallucinate” silence gotcha

Because Whisper-style models generate text rather than strictly transcribe, they can invent plausible words over silence, music, or noise — confidently producing a sentence that was never spoken. The fix isn't a smarter prompt: add voice-activity detection to skip non-speech, and don't blindly trust low-confidence segments in high-stakes transcripts (legal, medical).

03  TTS: neural-codec & diffusion voices NEW

Old TTS sounded robotic because it stitched together recorded snippets. Modern TTS generates the audio. Two dominant approaches in 2026:

Neural-codec (token) TTS
An audio codec compresses speech into a sequence of discrete audio tokens (more on those next). A transformer — just like an LLM — predicts those tokens from the text, then the codec decodes them back to a waveform. Speech becomes a next-token problem.
Diffusion TTS
The same denoising idea behind image generation, applied to audio: start from noise and refine it into a clean spectrogram/waveform conditioned on the text. Often excellent at smooth, natural prosody.

Two terms you should be ready to define:

Prosody
The music of speech — rhythm, stress, intonation, pauses. Good prosody is what separates “human” from “robot”; it's why a model can read the same sentence as a question or a statement.
Voice cloning
Conditioning the voice on a short reference clip so the output speaks in that person's voice. A few seconds of audio can be enough — which is exactly why it's an ethics minefield (next section).

04  Real-time speech-to-speech & voice agents NEW

The classic voice assistant is a three-box pipeline: STT → LLM → TTS. It works, but every hand-off adds latency and throws away information — the LLM only sees a flat transcript, never your tone, hesitation or sarcasm.

Unified speech-to-speech models hit conversational latency (a few hundred ms), handle barge-in (you interrupt, it stops talking), and preserve emotion and laughter — making real-time voice agents mainstream (as of June 2026).

Designing one of these end-to-end — latency budgets, interruption handling, turn-taking, when to fall back to the cascade — is a favourite system-design prompt. See the real-time voice case for the full walk-through.

05  Audio tokenization & neural codecs

The idea that unlocks all of the above: you can turn continuous audio into a sequence of discrete tokens, just like text. A neural codec is an encoder-decoder trained to compress a waveform into a compact stream of integer codes and reconstruct it faithfully. Once audio is “tokens,” the entire transformer toolbox applies — a model can predict the next audio token exactly as it predicts the next word.

An alphabet for sound analogy

Text already comes in neat letters. Sound doesn't — it's a smooth wiggle. A neural codec invents an alphabet for audio: a small set of symbols that can spell out any voice. Now “generate speech” is just “write in the sound-alphabet,” and the same model that writes sentences can write soundtracks. This shared-token trick is also why one any-to-any model can speak and type.

06  Ethics: consent, cloning & deepfakes

Voice cloning from a few seconds of audio is genuinely useful (accessibility, dubbing, restoring a lost voice) and genuinely dangerous (scam calls impersonating a relative, fake statements from public figures). A responsible practitioner names the guardrails without being asked:

Consent
Clone a voice only with the speaker's explicit permission. Treat a voiceprint like biometric data, not like a font.
Disclosure
Synthetic speech should be labelled as synthetic — especially anything that could be mistaken for a real person.
Watermarking & provenance
Inaudible watermarks (SynthID-style) and provenance metadata let platforms detect AI-generated audio and trace it back. Regulation (e.g. the EU AI Act) increasingly requires this.

This connects directly to the safety module — see watermarking & governance for how detection and disclosure actually work.

How to say it out loud say it

“Speech is two jobs: STT turns audio into text — Whisper-lineage transformers, with streaming for live use and diarization to label speakers — and TTS turns text into audio, either neural-codec token models or diffusion, with prosody and optional voice cloning. The big 2026 shift is unified speech-to-speech models that listen and talk in one pass, preserving tone and supporting interruption. The enabler underneath is neural-codec audio tokenization. And cloning demands consent, disclosure and watermarking.”

07  Self-check

Why does a unified speech-to-speech model often beat a classic STT → LLM → TTS cascade for a voice agent?
Each box in the cascade adds latency, and converting speech to a plain transcript discards prosody, hesitation and emotion before the LLM ever sees it. A unified model works in audio end-to-end, hitting conversational latency and keeping the nuance — plus clean barge-in.
“How do modern STT and TTS pipelines work?”Core
Model answer

STT slices audio into frames, turns them into a spectrogram-like input, and runs an encoder-decoder transformer (Whisper-lineage) to emit text — batch for accuracy, streaming for live, with diarization layered on to label speakers. TTS goes the other way: either a neural-codec model that predicts discrete audio tokens then decodes them to a waveform, or a diffusion model that denoises into audio — both conditioned on text, with prosody control and optional voice cloning from a reference clip.

How to say it out loud say it

“STT: audio → frames → encoder-decoder transformer → text, streaming or batch, plus diarization. TTS: text → audio tokens (codec) or denoised waveform (diffusion), with prosody and optional cloning.”
