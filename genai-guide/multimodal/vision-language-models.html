Vision-language models IV NEW

A vision-language model (VLM) is an LLM that can also see. Show it a photo, a chart, a screenshot, or a scanned invoice and it reads, reasons and answers in words — captioning a scene, answering questions about a diagram, pulling fields out of a form. The clever part is how little had to change: a VLM is mostly a normal text LLM with a small bit of plumbing in front that turns pixels into something the language model already understands.

01  An LLM that learned to see

You already know an LLM reads a sequence of word-tokens and predicts the next one. A VLM does the exact same thing — except some of the “tokens” fed in didn't come from words, they came from an image. The image gets chopped up, translated into the same kind of vectors the model uses for text, and slotted into the prompt right alongside your question. From the language model's point of view, a picture is just more context to read.

A translator clipped onto a reader analogy

Imagine a brilliant reader who only understands one language. To let them “read” a painting, you hire a translator who looks at the picture and writes a dense description in that one language — then hands the note to the reader. The reader never sees pixels; they just read the translated note. In a VLM the vision encoder + projector is that translator, and the LLM is the reader. Train them together and the translation gets good enough that the reader can answer almost anything about the image.

02  The architecture: encoder + projector + LLM NEW

Almost every 2026 VLM follows the same three-part recipe. It's the single most useful thing to be able to draw on a whiteboard:

The image becomes a handful of “image tokens” that sit in the prompt next to your text tokens. The LLM attends over both at once — which is why it can answer questions that mix what it sees with what you ask.
1 · Vision encoder
Usually a Vision Transformer (ViT) — it splits the image into patches and turns them into feature vectors. Frequently a CLIP-pretrained encoder, because CLIP already learned image features that line up with language.
2 · Projector (the “glue”)
A small trained module — an MLP or a few cross-attention layers — that maps the vision features into the LLM's token embedding space, so a patch “looks like” a token. This is the cheap, learnable bridge; often it's the main thing you train.
3 · LLM
An ordinary decoder-only language model. It treats the projected image vectors as extra context tokens and generates text as usual.

A common training shortcut: freeze the vision encoder and the LLM (both expensive, both already good) and just train the little projector to align them. That's why capable VLMs can be built without retraining a giant model from scratch.

03  CLIP: the contrastive backbone

Why does a vision encoder produce features that an LLM can make sense of? Largely thanks to CLIP-style contrastive training. You take hundreds of millions of (image, caption) pairs off the web and train two encoders — one for images, one for text — with one objective: the vector for an image and the vector for its caption should be close together, while mismatched pairs are pushed apart.

Teaching pictures and words to share a map analogy

Picture one big map where every concept has a spot. CLIP's training pulls a photo of a beagle and the words “a beagle” onto the same spot, and shoves “a fire truck” far away. After enough pairs, images and text live on a shared map — so “find the picture that matches this sentence” becomes “find the nearest point.” That shared space is the foundation of multimodal search and the reason VLM vision features are language-friendly.

This is the same contrastive, shared-embedding idea that powers text-to-image generation (the prompt and the image must agree) — see text-to-image control. CLIP gives you understanding and retrieval; bolt a generator on the other side and you get creation.

04  What VLMs can actually do

Once an LLM can see, a whole family of tasks opens up. These are the ones worth naming in an interview:

Captioning
Describe an image in natural language. The “hello world” of vision-language.
Visual Q&A (VQA)
Answer a question about an image — “how many people are wearing hats?”, “is this safe to eat?”
Document & OCR
Read text inside the image — invoices, forms, handwriting, screenshots — and often return structured fields. A huge enterprise use-case.
Grounding
Point to where something is — return a bounding box or coordinates for “the broken tile.” Vision with a finger, not just a voice.
Charts & diagrams
Read a graph or flowchart and reason over it — extract the trend, transcribe the table, explain the schematic.
VLMs hallucinate about images too gotcha

A VLM can confidently describe an object that isn't there, misread a digit on a form, or “see” text that the resolution can't actually support. The image is compressed into a few hundred tokens — fine detail gets lost. For anything high-stakes (medical, legal, financial), treat VLM output as a draft to verify, ask it to quote exactly what it sees, and feed higher-resolution crops rather than one tiny thumbnail.

05  Multimodal RAG & document understanding NEW

VLMs change how retrieval works. In classic RAG you first run OCR to flatten a PDF into text, then embed the text — and you lose the layout, the figures, the tables. Multimodal RAG skips the lossy step: you embed and retrieve the page image itself, then hand the relevant pages straight to a VLM to read.

Embed the page, not just the text
Approaches like ColPali use a VLM to embed page images directly, so a chart or a stamped signature is retrievable — no brittle OCR pre-step.
Retrieve pages, answer with a VLM
The top-k retrieved images go into a vision-capable model that reads them in place, preserving tables, figures and layout.
Why it wins
Real documents are visual. Keeping the pixels means the model sees the two-column layout, the highlighted cell, the diagram callout — context that pure-text RAG throws away.

This is the cutting edge of document AI — the advanced retrieval page goes deeper on multimodal RAG and late-interaction retrieval over images and tables.

06  So how does a model “see” an image?

Time to make the whole pipeline concrete in one breath. An image is a grid of pixels. The vision encoder cuts it into fixed-size patches (say 16×16 pixels), flattens each patch, and runs them through a Vision Transformer — exactly like attention over words, but the “words” are tiles of the picture. Out come feature vectors, one per patch. The projector nudges those vectors into the LLM's embedding space, producing a short sequence of image tokens. Those tokens are placed in the prompt next to your text, and the LLM attends over everything together to write its answer.

Text side↔Vision side
Sentence↔Image
Word → token↔Patch → patch feature
Token embedding↔Projected image token
Tokenizer↔Vision encoder + projector

Higher resolution = more patches = more image tokens = more cost and slower responses. Many VLMs tile big images into several crops, which is great for reading fine print but multiplies the token bill.

How to say it out loud say it

“A VLM is a normal LLM with a vision front-end. A ViT-style encoder — often CLIP-pretrained — turns the image into patch features; a small projector maps those into the LLM's token space; the LLM reads them as extra context tokens and answers in text. CLIP's contrastive training is why image features line up with language. It's great for captioning, VQA, OCR and grounding, but it can still hallucinate, so I treat its read of an image as a draft to verify.”

07  Self-check

In the standard VLM recipe, what is the job of the projector?
The projector is the small, often-the-only-trained bridge between the frozen vision encoder and the frozen LLM. It aligns image features to text-token space, turning patches into “image tokens” the language model attends over.
“Walk me through how a vision-language model is built.”Core
Model answer

Three parts. A vision encoder (a ViT, often CLIP-pretrained) turns the image into patch features. A projector — an MLP or cross-attention adapter — maps those features into the LLM's token-embedding space. The LLM then treats them as extra context tokens alongside the text prompt and generates an answer. A common pattern is to freeze the encoder and LLM and only train the projector, which makes building a VLM relatively cheap.

How to say it out loud say it

“Vision encoder turns the image into features, a projector aligns them to the LLM's token space, the LLM reads them as context. Often you freeze the encoder and LLM and only train the projector.”

“Why might multimodal RAG beat OCR-then-text RAG on real documents?”Core
Model answer

Because OCR-first flattens a visual document into plain text and throws away layout, tables, figures and emphasis — and OCR errors poison everything downstream. Multimodal RAG embeds and retrieves the page image directly (e.g. ColPali-style) and hands it to a VLM that reads it in place, so two-column layouts, charts and stamps survive. You only pay for it where documents are genuinely visual.

How to say it out loud say it

“OCR-first loses layout and compounds OCR errors. Multimodal RAG embeds the page image and lets a VLM read it in place — tables, charts and layout survive. Use it where documents are visual.”
