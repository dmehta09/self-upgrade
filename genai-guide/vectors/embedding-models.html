Choosing & using embeddings IV NEW

The embedding model is the most consequential — and most quietly chosen — component of any vector-search system. Pick well and similar things land close together for your domain; pick badly and no clever database or reranker can save you. This page is the practical decision guide: the families and dimensions on offer, the 2025-era Matryoshka trick that lets you trade quality for speed by truncating a vector, the symmetric-vs-asymmetric distinction interviewers love, and a clean checklist for choosing one.

01  What an embedding model actually is

An embedding model is a (usually small) neural network — most often an encoder-style transformer — whose only job is: text in, one fixed-length vector out. It's trained with contrastive learning: shown pairs that should be similar (a question and its answer, a sentence and its paraphrase) and pairs that shouldn't, it nudges its weights until “similar” pairs sit close and unrelated ones sit far apart. The output vector is your embedding.

A sommelier who scores every wine on the same card analogy

Imagine a sommelier who tastes any wine and fills in the same scorecard — body, sweetness, tannin, hundreds of subtle notes. Two different wines that taste alike end up with near-identical cards; you can compare any two wines just by comparing cards, without re-tasting. An embedding model is that sommelier for text: it reads anything and emits a comparable scorecard of numbers. And just like you must use one consistent sommelier, you must embed your documents and your queries with the same model — two sommeliers fill the card differently and the scores stop meaning anything.

This is a different model from your chat LLM. A generative LLM predicts the next token; an embedding model produces one vector for a whole passage. They're trained differently and you pick them independently.

02  Families & dimensions

You'll choose along two big axes: where it runs (a hosted API vs an open-weight model you host yourself) and how many dimensions the output vector has. More dimensions can capture more nuance but cost more to store and compare — every extra dimension is more bytes per vector and more arithmetic per comparison, multiplied across millions of vectors.

More dimensions ≠ automatically better retrieval gotcha

It's tempting to grab the highest-dimension model “for quality.” But a well-trained 768-dim model routinely beats a weaker 3072-dim one, and you pay 4× the storage and compare-cost for the bigger one regardless. Dimension is a storage/latency knob, not a quality score. Benchmark retrieval quality first; choose dimensions for your budget second.

03  Matryoshka embeddings: one vector, many sizes NEW

Normally a model's dimension is fixed: a 1024-dim model gives you 1024 numbers, take it or leave it. Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) changes that. The model is trained so that the most important information is packed into the earliest dimensions. That means you can chop a long vector down to its first N numbers and it still works — quality degrades gracefully instead of falling off a cliff.

Russian nesting dolls analogy

MRL is named after matryoshka — Russian nesting dolls. The full 1024-dim vector is the biggest doll; inside it sits a complete, usable 512-dim doll, and inside that a 256-dim one, and so on. Each smaller doll is a self-contained coarser version of the same meaning — not a broken-off fragment. So you embed once at full size, then truncate to whatever size each job can afford.

Why this is a big deal: you embed once and get every size for free. Use a tiny 256-dim slice for a fast first-pass search over millions of vectors, then re-score the few survivors with the full vector. Storage and search cost scale with the dimension you keep, so truncating from 1024 → 256 is roughly a 4× saving for a small, often-acceptable quality dip.

matryoshka.py
import numpy as np

# An MRL model returns a full-size vector, e.g. 1024-dim.
full = embed("how do I reset my password?")        # shape: (1024,)

# Truncate to a smaller "doll" — just slice the front…
small = full[:256]

# …then RE-NORMALISE to unit length so cosine/dot still behave.
small = small / np.linalg.norm(small)
# `small` is a valid 256-dim embedding: 4x cheaper to store & compare,
# with only a small drop in retrieval quality.
Only truncate models trained for it — and re-normalise gotcha

Truncation only works on models explicitly trained with MRL; chopping a normal embedding throws away real information and wrecks results. Two more rules: after slicing, re-normalise to unit length, and embed both your documents and queries at the same truncated size — comparing a 256-dim query to 1024-dim documents is meaningless.

04  Symmetric vs asymmetric embeddings

Here's a subtlety that trips up a lot of people. In search, the two things you compare are not alike: a short query (“refund policy?”) versus a long document (three paragraphs of terms). Asymmetric embedding models are trained for exactly this mismatch and often want you to tell them which side is which — usually via a short instruction or prefix like query: and passage:.

Symmetric
Both sides are the same kind of text — e.g. finding duplicate questions, or sentence-to-sentence similarity. Encode both the same way.
Asymmetric
Query and document differ in length/shape (search, RAG). Encode each with its own role hint so the model maps a tiny question near the long passage that answers it.
asymmetric.py
# Many asymmetric models (e.g. E5/BGE-style) expect a role prefix.
# Use the prefix the model's card specifies — don't invent your own.
q_vec   = embed("query: how do I get a refund?")
doc_vec = embed("passage: Refunds are issued within 30 days of purchase…")

score = cosine(q_vec, doc_vec)   # mapped into the same space, by role
Forgetting the prefix silently tanks recall gotcha

If a model was trained with query:/passage: prefixes (or task instructions) and you omit them — or swap them — results get noticeably worse with no error message. It's one of the most common “my retrieval is mysteriously bad” bugs. Always read the model card and apply the exact prefix/instruction it expects, on the correct side.

05  Domain fit, sequence length & language

Three more properties decide whether a model fits your corpus:

Domain fit
A model trained on general web text may fumble medical, legal or code jargon. Check whether it was trained (or fine-tuned) on text like yours; specialised embedding models exist for code and biomed.
Max sequence length
Every embedding model has a token limit (commonly ~512, increasingly 8K+). Text past the limit is silently truncated — the tail is dropped before encoding. If your chunks exceed it, you're embedding only the beginning. Match your chunk size to this limit.
Multilingual
A multilingual model places the same meaning near each other across languages, so an English query can retrieve a German passage. English-only models can't do this. Match the model to your corpus's languages.
Silent truncation eats the end of your chunk gotcha

Feed a 1,500-token chunk to a 512-token model and it won't warn you — it just encodes the first 512 tokens and discards the rest. Your “document embedding” then represents only the opening. Either chunk smaller or pick a long-context embedding model; this links straight to chunking.

How to say it out loud say it

“I start from constraints — privacy and budget decide API versus self-hosted. Then I check domain fit, languages, and that the model's max sequence length covers my chunk size. I shortlist from MTEB but pick the winner by evaluating recall on my own labelled query–document pairs. If it's a Matryoshka model I truncate the dimensions to the smallest size that still passes my quality bar, and I never forget the query/passage prefixes on asymmetric models.”

07  Self-check

Your team wants to cut vector storage costs by 4×. The embedding model was trained with Matryoshka Representation Learning. What's the cheapest safe move?
MRL packs the most important signal into the earliest dimensions, so keeping the first 25% (and re-normalising) gives a valid, much cheaper vector with a modest quality dip — no re-training, no new model. Random or evenly-spaced dropping breaks the learned ordering.
“What are Matryoshka embeddings and why are they useful?”Core
Model answer

They're embeddings trained so the most important information sits in the earliest dimensions, so you can truncate a long vector to its first N numbers and it still works — quality degrades gracefully. You embed once and get many sizes for free: a tiny slice for cheap first-pass search over millions of vectors, the full vector to rerank survivors. Cost scales with the dimensions you keep, so it's a direct storage/latency lever.

How to say it out loud say it

“Matryoshka = nesting dolls for vectors. The model front-loads the important dimensions, so I can chop a 1024-dim vector to 256, re-normalise, and keep most of the quality at a quarter of the cost — embedding once, using any size.”

“Why would a query and a document use different embedding instructions?”Core
Model answer

Because retrieval is asymmetric: a short query and a long passage are different shapes of text. Asymmetric embedding models are trained with role prefixes or instructions (like query: vs passage:) so a tiny question maps near the long passage that answers it. Symmetric tasks — duplicate detection, sentence similarity — use the same encoding on both sides. Forgetting the prefix on an asymmetric model silently hurts recall.

How to say it out loud say it

“Search is query-vs-document, not like-vs-like. Asymmetric models take a role hint so a short question lands near the long answer. Symmetric tasks encode both sides identically. Use the prefix the model card specifies.”
