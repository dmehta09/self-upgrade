Prompting & context · overview

Prompting & context NEW

A model can only answer using what's in front of it right now — the text you hand it. This module is about getting the right words in front of the model: how to phrase a request (prompting), and the bigger discipline of deciding what the model sees at all (context engineering). Master this and most of your “the model is dumb” problems turn out to be “the model couldn't see what it needed.”

01  Why this is the highest-leverage skill

Here's the uncomfortable truth: in 2026 the model you call is usually already very capable. The thing that makes your app good or bad is almost never “which model” — it's what you put in the prompt and how you assemble it. A frontier model fed vague, cluttered, or contradictory input will look stupid; a smaller model fed clean, well-structured input will look brilliant.

That's why prompting is the first thing interviewers probe: it's cheap to change, it explains most quality problems, and it reveals whether you actually understand how the model reads its input.

Briefing a brilliant new hire who has no memory analogy

Picture an expert contractor who is sharp, fast, and knows almost everything — but who forgets everything the moment they finish a task and only knows what's written on the one note card you hand them. You wouldn't blame them for guessing if your card said “fix the thing.” Prompting is writing a good card; context engineering is deciding which facts, examples, and files even make it onto the card.

02  From “prompt engineering” to “context engineering” NEW

A few years ago the craft was called prompt engineering: tweaking the wording of a single instruction until the model behaved — magic phrases, “you are an expert,” clever rephrasings. That still matters, but it was always a narrow slice.

As apps grew into chatbots-with-history, RAG pipelines, and agents that call tools in a loop, the hard part stopped being “the wording of one sentence” and became managing everything in the window over many steps: the system prompt, the chat history, retrieved documents, tool outputs, memories. The field renamed itself context engineering — the discipline of deciding what the model sees, when, and in what form (as of June 2026, this is the term you'll hear in interviews).

Prompt engineering
Craft the wording of a request: instructions, examples, output format, persona. Mostly about one message.
Context engineering
The superset: curate the whole window across turns and tools — what to include, summarize, drop, or fetch. Prompting is one tool inside it.
It's a rename, not a replacement note

Don't say prompt engineering is “dead.” Say it grew up: prompting is the writing-one-message skill, and context engineering is the broader system-level skill that contains it. Interviewers like candidates who frame it that way.

03  The context window is the model's RAM

Everything in this module hangs on one idea. The context window is the fixed-size space — measured in tokens — that holds everything the model can attend to for a single response: your system prompt, the conversation so far, any documents you pasted, tool results, and the answer it's currently writing.

RAM, not a hard drive analogy

The window is like a computer's RAM: fast, but small and temporary. The model has no access to anything that isn't loaded into RAM right now — it can't “remember” a past chat or “open” a file unless that text is sitting in the window. Long-term storage (databases, files, vector stores) is the disk; getting the right bytes from disk into RAM at the right moment is the whole job of context engineering.

Two consequences fall straight out of this and run through every page below: the window is finite (even at 1M+ tokens, as of June 2026), and it's not free — every token on it costs money and a little quality, because the model has to split its attention across all of them.

A bigger window does not mean “just paste everything” gotcha

The most common beginner mistake is treating a 1M-token window as a junk drawer: dump the whole knowledge base in and hope. More tokens cost more, add latency, and — counter-intuitively — can lower accuracy, because relevant facts get buried (the “lost in the middle” effect, covered in Context engineering). Curate, don't dump.

05  Self-check

Why did the field rebrand “prompt engineering” into “context engineering”?
Prompting is still real and still inside the discipline — but multi-turn chat, RAG, and agents made the central challenge “what goes in the finite window, when, and in what form.” That superset is context engineering.
“What's the difference between prompt engineering and context engineering?”Warm-up
Model answer

Prompt engineering is crafting the wording of a single request — instructions, examples, output format, persona. Context engineering is the broader discipline of deciding everything that enters the context window over time: system prompt, history, retrieved docs, tool results, memory — what to include, summarize, or drop. Prompting is a tool inside context engineering.

How to say it out loud say it

“Prompting is wording one message well; context engineering is curating the whole window across a conversation or agent run. Same goal — get the right tokens in front of the model — just different scope.”

Follow-ups: Where does RAG fit? (It's a context source.) · What breaks if the window fills up? (Cost, latency, lost-in-the-middle.)
“Why is the context window a useful thing to think of as RAM?”Core
Model answer

Because it's fast, finite, and temporary, and the model can only act on what's loaded into it right now. Anything not in the window — past chats, files, knowledge bases — is effectively on “disk” and invisible until you load it in. That framing makes cost, latency, forgetting, and retrieval all fall out naturally: you're constantly choosing what to keep in RAM.

How to say it out loud say it

“The window is RAM: small, fast, and the only thing the model can see. Databases and files are disk. Context engineering is deciding what to page into RAM and when.”

Follow-ups: If windows are 1M+ tokens now, is this solved? (No — cost, latency, and lost-in-the-middle persist.)
