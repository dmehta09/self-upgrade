Fine-tuning · supervised fine-tuning

Supervised fine-tuning IV

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the workhorse of model adaptation, and its idea is almost embarrassingly simple: show the model thousands of examples of "when you see this input, produce this output," and it learns to imitate them. No clever reward, no reinforcement — just high-quality demonstrations. Everything that makes SFT succeed or fail comes down to the data.

01  SFT = learning by imitation

You hand the model pairs of (prompt, ideal answer). It reads the prompt, predicts the answer one token at a time, and is gently corrected wherever its guess differs from your example. Repeat over the whole dataset and it shifts toward producing answers that look like your demonstrations. That's it — it's the same next-token training as pretraining, but on a small, deliberately chosen set of "this is what good looks like" examples.

An apprentice copying a master's worked examples analogy

Picture a junior chef given a binder of the head chef's perfected recipes. They cook each one, compare to the photo, and adjust until their dish matches. They're not inventing cuisine — they're absorbing the master's habits by imitation. SFT is exactly that: the model practises copying your "gold" answers until producing that kind of answer becomes second nature. If your binder is full of sloppy recipes, that's what it learns.

02  Instruction tuning: the most famous SFT

Instruction tuning is just SFT where the examples are (instruction, helpful response) pairs across a huge variety of tasks — summarise this, fix this code, explain this like I'm ten. It's the step that turns a raw next-token predictor into something that follows instructions instead of just continuing your text. The big chat models you use were instruction-tuned (then aligned further — see preference optimization).

When you fine-tune for your own use case, you're usually doing a narrow version of this: instead of "be helpful at everything," you teach "be excellent at this one task in our style."

SFT is step one of post-training note

In a full pipeline, SFT comes first (teach the model the shape of good answers), then preference optimization refines which good answer to prefer. SFT can't easily learn "A is better than B" — it only ever sees one "correct" answer per prompt. That ceiling is exactly why the next page exists.

03  Dataset design — where SFT is won or lost

The model can only become as good as the examples you feed it. Three principles dominate:

Quality > quantity
A few hundred clean, correct, on-style examples beat tens of thousands of noisy ones. One confidently-wrong answer teaches the model to be confidently wrong. Curate ruthlessly.
Format consistency
Every example should follow the same structure — same roles, same delimiters, same answer shape. Inconsistency teaches the model that anything goes, and your outputs get unpredictable.
Coverage & diversity
Span the real range of inputs you'll see (edge cases, refusals, different phrasings) so the model generalises instead of memorising a narrow slice.

And the discipline that ties it together: hold out a separate eval split the model never trains on. Without it you can't tell improvement from memorisation.

No held-out eval = you're guessing gotcha

If you measure success on examples the model trained on, a high score can just mean it memorised them. Always carve off a held-out set (and ideally a fresh, real-world test set) before training, and judge the fine-tune there. "It looks better in the demo" is not an eval. (More in Evaluation.)

04  Chat templates & masking the prompt

Chat models expect a specific chat template — special tokens that mark where the system, user, and assistant turns begin and end. Your training data must use the exact template the base model was trained with, or you'll quietly degrade it. Most tooling applies this for you, but knowing it exists is interview gold.

The subtler point is loss masking. During SFT you only want the model to learn to produce the assistant's reply — not to reproduce the user's question. So the loss is computed only on the completion tokens; the prompt tokens are masked out (they're still read as context, just not "graded").

Mask the prompt, train on the completion. Otherwise the model wastes capacity learning to echo questions — and can get worse at answering them.

# One SFT example: prompt is context, completion is what we grade.
example = {
    "messages": [
        {"role": "system",    "content": "You are a terse SQL assistant."},
        {"role": "user",      "content": "Users older than 30, by signup date."},
        {"role": "assistant", "content": "SELECT * FROM users WHERE age > 30 ORDER BY signup_date;"},
    ]
}
# The trainer applies the model's chat template, then masks the
# system+user tokens so loss is computed ONLY on the assistant turn.
# (TRL's SFTTrainer does this for you when given `messages`.)

05  Catastrophic forgetting

Push SFT too hard on a narrow dataset and the model gets great at your task while quietly losing abilities it used to have — general reasoning, other formats, even basic chat manners. This is catastrophic forgetting: new training overwrites old weights. It's the number-one way naive fine-tunes go wrong.

A specialist that forgot how to be a generalist gotcha

Train hard on only customer-support transcripts and your model may ace support but suddenly fumble code, math, or following a new instruction format. Mitigations: keep learning rates low, train for fewer epochs, mix in some general/diverse data, and — the big one — use parameter-efficient methods like LoRA that touch far fewer weights so the base model's skills survive. And always eval on general tasks too, not just your target task.

How to say it out loud say it

“SFT is imitation learning on (prompt, ideal-answer) pairs — I apply the model's chat template and mask the prompt so loss falls only on the completion. The dataset is everything: a few hundred clean, consistent, diverse examples plus a held-out eval split. My biggest risk is catastrophic forgetting, so I keep the learning rate and epochs low, often mix in general data, and lean on LoRA to limit how much I disturb the base model.”

06  Self-check

During SFT on chat data, why do we mask the prompt tokens and compute loss only on the assistant's completion?
The prompt is still read as context — it's not hidden — but it isn't "graded." We only want the model to get better at generating the assistant turn. Computing loss on the prompt too would teach it to reproduce user questions, diluting the skill we actually care about.
“You have budget for either 500 hand-checked examples or 50,000 scraped ones. Which, and why?”Core
Model answer

The 500 hand-checked examples, almost always. SFT is imitation — the model copies whatever it's shown, mistakes included. 50,000 noisy examples teach it to reproduce that noise: wrong formats, hallucinations, inconsistent style. A few hundred clean, consistent, well-covered examples give a sharper, more reliable signal, and they're cheap to eval against. If I needed more volume later, I'd grow the clean set carefully — or generate synthetic data and filter it hard — never just dump in raw scrape.

How to say it out loud say it

“Quality beats quantity in SFT — the model imitates exactly what you show it. I'd take the 500 clean examples; noise just gets memorised.”

Follow-ups: How would you scale the clean set? · How do you catch a bad example?
“Your fine-tune nailed the target task but got worse at everything else. What happened, and what do you do?”Core
Model answer

That's catastrophic forgetting — training hard on a narrow set overwrote general abilities. I'd dial back the learning rate and number of epochs, mix some general/diverse data back into the set, and switch to a parameter-efficient method like LoRA so I'm only nudging small adapters instead of rewriting the whole model. And I'd make sure my eval includes general tasks, not just the target, so I catch the regression before shipping.

How to say it out loud say it

“Classic catastrophic forgetting — fix it with lower LR, fewer epochs, some general data mixed in, and LoRA so the base model's skills survive.”
