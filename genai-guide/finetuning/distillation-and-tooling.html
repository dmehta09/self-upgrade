Fine-tuning · distillation & tooling

Distillation & tooling IV NEW

You've adapted a model — now make it cheap and fast to run. Distillation teaches a small "student" model to copy a big "teacher," giving you most of the quality at a fraction of the cost. This page covers distillation (including distilling reasoning), how it differs from quantization, the standard toolchain you'd name in an interview, and the discipline that ties it all together: eval before and after.

01  Knowledge distillation NEW

Knowledge distillation trains a small student model to mimic a large teacher. You run the teacher on lots of prompts, capture its outputs, and train the student to reproduce them. The student ends up far smaller and cheaper to serve, yet keeps a surprising amount of the teacher's quality — because it's learning from a rich, expert signal instead of raw data alone.

A master chef training an apprentice analogy

A world-class chef can't be in every kitchen — too expensive, too slow. So they train an apprentice by cooking alongside them: the apprentice watches not just what the master makes but how they weigh the choices. Soon the apprentice runs a busy kitchen alone at a tenth of the cost, food almost as good. Distillation is that apprenticeship: the small model learns the big model's judgement, then serves the crowd cheaply.

Two flavours worth naming: copying the teacher's soft predictions (its probability distribution over tokens, which carries more nuance than a single answer), or simply training on the teacher's generated answers as if they were labelled data (often called sequence-level or "dark knowledge"-free distillation). In 2025–26 a hot use is distilling reasoning: take a big reasoning model's chain-of-thought traces and train a small model on them, so a compact model inherits step-by-step problem-solving it couldn't learn alone.

02  Distillation vs quantization

Both make a model cheaper to run, but they're different levers and you can stack them:

DistillationQuantization
What changesA new, smaller model (fewer parameters)Same model, weights stored in lower precision
HowTrain a student to copy a teacherRound 16-bit weights to 8/4-bit
EffortA training run + dataOften post-hoc, minutes to apply
Quality riskStudent may miss teacher's edge casesSmall accuracy drop from rounding

On the quantization side there are two timings: post-training quantization (PTQ) — quantize an already-trained model (fast, the common path) — and quantization-aware training (QAT) — simulate low precision during training so the model adapts to it, recovering more quality at higher effort. The serving-side details (FP8/FP4, AWQ, GPTQ, GGUF) live in Quantization for inference; here just know distillation shrinks the model, quantization shrinks the numbers, and a cheap specialist often uses both.

03  The toolchain NEW

You don't write training loops from scratch. Four libraries dominate the open fine-tuning world (as of June 2026) — know what each is for:

TRL
Hugging Face's "Transformer Reinforcement Learning." The reference implementation of the training algorithms — SFT, DPO, ORPO, KTO, GRPO, PPO. When you need the actual trainers, this is the core.
PEFT
Hugging Face's parameter-efficient library — the home of LoRA/QLoRA adapters. Plugs into TRL so you train adapters instead of full models.
Axolotl
A config-driven wrapper: you describe your fine-tune in a YAML file (model, data, method, hyperparameters) and it orchestrates the run. Great for reproducible, production-style fine-tuning without hand-writing code.
Unsloth
An optimization layer focused on speed and memory — custom kernels that make LoRA/QLoRA fine-tuning markedly faster and lighter, so bigger models fit on smaller GPUs.
How they fit together note

Think layers: PEFT provides the adapters, TRL provides the training algorithms, Unsloth speeds both up, and Axolotl wraps the whole thing in a config so you can run it reproducibly. They're complementary, not competitors. For closed models, the provider's hosted fine-tuning API plays the same role behind a managed endpoint.

04  Synthetic data — and its risks

When you don't have enough real examples, you can generate them with a strong model: have it write training prompts and answers, then fine-tune on those. It's how teams bootstrap datasets fast, and it's the engine behind RLAIF and much reasoning distillation. But it comes with sharp edges.

Synthetic data inherits the teacher's flaws gotcha

If the generator hallucinates, is biased, or has a narrow style, your dataset bakes those in — and training a model on its own kind of output can cause model collapse, where quality and diversity erode over generations. Mitigations: use a stronger model as the generator, filter and verify aggressively (dedupe, check correctness, mix in real data), and never skip a human-checked eval set. Synthetic data is a powerful amplifier, including of mistakes.

05  Eval before and after — non-negotiable

Every adaptation step deserves the same question: did it actually help, and did it break anything? The only way to know is a fixed eval set you run before and after. Measure your target metric (did the specialist get better?) and general capabilities (did it forget anything?), on data the model never trained on.

“It feels better” is not evidence gotcha

A demo that looks good can hide a regression on cases you didn't try. Fine-tuning can quietly trade general ability for narrow gains (catastrophic forgetting again). Without a before/after eval you can't tell a real improvement from a vibe — and in production, eval is a release gate, not an afterthought. (Full treatment in Evaluation.)

How to say it out loud say it

“To ship a cheap, fast specialist I'd distill a strong teacher into a small student, then quantize it for serving — distillation shrinks the model, quantization shrinks the numbers. I'd use LoRA/QLoRA via PEFT and TRL, maybe Axolotl for a reproducible config and Unsloth for speed, and bootstrap data synthetically but filter it hard to avoid baking in the teacher's errors. And I'd gate the whole thing on a before/after eval — target metric up, general ability not down — because without that I'm just guessing.”

06  Self-check

What's the key difference between distillation and quantization?
Distillation changes the model — a smaller student learns to mimic a bigger teacher. Quantization changes the numbers — the same model's weights are stored at 8- or 4-bit precision. They're orthogonal levers, and a cheap, fast specialist often uses both: distill first, then quantize for serving.
“How would you ship a cheap, fast, specialized model?”Core
Model answer

I'd start from a capable teacher (or a model I fine-tuned to behave well) and distill it into a smaller student so I keep most of the quality at a fraction of the cost — distilling its answers, and its reasoning traces if the task needs reasoning. I'd train the student efficiently with LoRA/QLoRA via PEFT and TRL, possibly orchestrated by Axolotl and sped up by Unsloth. For data, I'd bootstrap synthetically from the teacher but filter and verify hard, mixing in real examples to avoid model collapse. Then I'd quantize the student for serving to cut memory and latency further. Throughout, I gate on a before/after eval: target metric must improve and general ability must not regress. Distillation shrinks the model, quantization shrinks the numbers, and eval proves it all worked.

How to say it out loud say it

“Distill a strong teacher into a small student, train it with QLoRA, quantize for serving, bootstrap data synthetically but filter it — and gate everything on a before/after eval.”

“What's each of TRL, PEFT, Axolotl, and Unsloth for?”Warm-up
Model answer

TRL implements the training algorithms — SFT, DPO, ORPO, KTO, GRPO, PPO. PEFT provides parameter-efficient adapters like LoRA/QLoRA. Axolotl is a config-driven wrapper that orchestrates a fine-tune from a YAML file for reproducibility. Unsloth is an optimization layer with custom kernels that make LoRA/QLoRA training faster and more memory-efficient. They layer together: PEFT adapters, TRL algorithms, Unsloth for speed, Axolotl to tie it into a reproducible config.

How to say it out loud say it

“TRL = algorithms, PEFT = adapters, Unsloth = speed, Axolotl = reproducible config. They stack, they don't compete.”
