Fine-tuning · parameter-efficient tuning

LoRA & QLoRA IV

Fully fine-tuning a modern model means updating billions of weights — huge memory, huge cost, a fresh multi-gigabyte copy per task. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) sidesteps all of that: freeze the giant base model and train a tiny add-on. LoRA is the dominant flavour, and QLoRA shrinks the bill so far you can fine-tune a 7B model on a single consumer GPU.

01  The big idea: don't rewrite the textbook

Full fine-tuning edits every page of a 1,000-page textbook to add your notes — slow, and you need a whole new copy of the book for each topic. PEFT keeps the original book pristine and adds a thin layer of annotations on top. The base model's knowledge stays frozen; your task-specific tweak lives in a small, separate file.

Sticky notes on a textbook, not rewriting it analogy

You don't reprint a textbook to adapt it for one class — you stick a few annotated notes in the margins. The book underneath is untouched, your notes are tiny, and you can peel them off or swap in a different set for a different class. LoRA adapters are those sticky notes: small, removable, and you can keep dozens for dozens of tasks while the expensive base model stays exactly as it was.

02  LoRA: low-rank adapters

The insight behind LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation): the change a fine-tune needs to make to a big weight matrix is usually "low-rank" — it can be captured by two skinny matrices multiplied together, with far fewer numbers than the matrix itself. So instead of editing the original weights, LoRA freezes them and learns a small A × B update alongside. At inference, the model's output = frozen weights + (scaled) adapter.

Only A and B (a tiny fraction of params — often well under 1%) are trained. The base W never moves, which is why LoRA largely sidesteps catastrophic forgetting.

Three knobs you must be able to name:

Rank (r)
How "wide" the detour is — the capacity of the adapter. Small r (8–16) is often plenty; bigger r = more expressive but more params and more overfitting risk.
Alpha (α)
A scaling factor on the adapter's contribution. The effective strength is roughly α/r; teams commonly set α to a small multiple of r.
Target modules
Which weight matrices get adapters — typically the attention projections (q, k, v, o) and often the MLP layers. More targets = more coverage and more params.

03  QLoRA: LoRA on a 4-bit base NEW

LoRA shrinks what you train, but you still have to load the full base model into GPU memory — and a 7B model in 16-bit is ~14 GB before you've trained anything. QLoRA fixes that: load the frozen base model in 4-bit (quantized), then train LoRA adapters on top in higher precision. The base barely fits in memory; the small adapters do the learning.

The headline result that made QLoRA famous: you can fine-tune a 7B model on a single consumer GPU (think ~16 GB), and even much larger models on one data-center card — work that used to need a cluster.

4-bit base
The frozen weights are stored in a compact 4-bit format (e.g. NF4), slashing memory. They're never updated, so the precision loss barely matters.
Higher-precision adapters
The LoRA A/B matrices train in 16-bit, so the part that's actually learning keeps its accuracy.
Net effect
Near-full-fine-tune quality at a fraction of the memory — the reason hobbyists and startups can fine-tune at all (as of June 2026, the default way most people fine-tune).
QLoRA quantizes for training — it's not the same as inference quantization gotcha

Don't conflate the two. QLoRA quantizes the frozen base during fine-tuning to save training memory; the adapter still trains in higher precision. Inference quantization (FP8/FP4, AWQ, GPTQ, GGUF) is a separate decision about how to serve the finished model. You can QLoRA-train and then serve in any precision you like. (See Quantization for inference.)

04  Merging, multi-adapter serving & hot-swapping

Because an adapter is a tiny separate file, you get serving superpowers full fine-tuning can't match:

Merging
Fold the adapter's math back into the base weights to get a single standalone model with zero extra inference latency. Great for shipping one specialised model; you lose the ability to swap it out, though.
Multi-adapter serving
Keep the base loaded once and attach many adapters at once — different customers or tasks served from the same GPU, each routed to its own adapter. Massively cheaper than one full model per task.
Hot-swapping
Swap adapters in and out at request time without reloading the multi-gigabyte base. Switch "legal tone" → "marketing tone" in milliseconds.
lora_config.py
# A typical LoRA setup with PEFT (illustrative).
from peft import LoraConfig

config = LoraConfig(
    r=16,                 # rank: capacity of the adapter
    lora_alpha=32,        # scaling (effective strength ~ alpha / r)
    target_modules=["q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj"],
    lora_dropout=0.05,
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
)
# Trains < 1% of params. Later: model.merge_and_unload() to bake it in,
# or keep it separate to hot-swap adapters on a shared base model.

05  Full fine-tune vs PEFT

Full fine-tuneLoRA / QLoRA (PEFT)
Weights trainedAll of them (billions)Tiny adapters (often <1%)
GPU memoryVery high (optimizer states for every weight)Low — QLoRA fits 7B on one consumer GPU
Artifact per taskA full model copy (many GB)A small adapter file (MBs)
Forgetting riskHigher — base weights moveLower — base is frozen
Multi-task servingOne model per taskMany adapters on one base
Peak quality ceilingSlightly higher for big shiftsNear-equal for most tasks

For the vast majority of real adaptation, LoRA/QLoRA wins on cost, speed, safety, and serving flexibility at near-equal quality. Full fine-tuning is reserved for large behaviour shifts or when you own the compute and want every last point.

How to say it out loud say it

“LoRA freezes the base model and learns a small low-rank update — two skinny matrices — so I train under one percent of the weights, with rank, alpha, and target modules as the main knobs. QLoRA loads that frozen base in 4-bit so I can fine-tune a 7B on a single consumer GPU. The adapter is a tiny file, so I can merge it for zero-latency serving, or keep many adapters on one shared base and hot-swap them per request. It's near full-fine-tune quality at a fraction of the cost, and because the base is frozen, it mostly avoids catastrophic forgetting.”

06  Self-check

What does QLoRA add on top of plain LoRA?
QLoRA quantizes the frozen base to 4-bit so the whole model fits in far less GPU memory — that's what lets you fine-tune a 7B on one consumer GPU. The adapters that actually learn stay in higher precision to preserve quality. The base is never updated, so its precision loss barely matters.
“Why does LoRA work — why can a tiny adapter match full fine-tuning?”Core
Model answer

Because the update a fine-tune needs is usually low-rank — the model already knows almost everything, so the change to adapt it is small and structured. LoRA captures that change as two skinny matrices whose product approximates the full weight update, with a fraction of the parameters. You freeze the base and only learn that low-rank detour. For most adaptation the lost expressiveness is negligible, so you get near-full quality while training under one percent of the weights and leaving the base intact.

How to say it out loud say it

“The needed weight change is low-rank, so two skinny matrices approximate it cheaply. Freeze the base, learn the detour — near-full quality at <1% of the params.”

“You need to serve 20 customer-specific variants on a budget. Full models or adapters?”Core
Model answer

Adapters, easily. Twenty full fine-tuned models means twenty multi-gigabyte copies and twenty slots of GPU memory. With LoRA I load the base once and attach twenty small adapters, routing each request to the right one — or hot-swap them on demand. Same hardware serves all twenty at a fraction of the cost, and adding a 21st customer is just another tiny adapter file. If one variant ever needs zero extra latency, I can merge just that adapter into a dedicated copy.

How to say it out loud say it

“One base, twenty adapters, routed or hot-swapped per request — far cheaper than twenty full models, and trivial to add the next customer.”
