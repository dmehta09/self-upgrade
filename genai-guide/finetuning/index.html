Fine-tuning — overview IV

A base model is a brilliant generalist who has read the whole internet but doesn't know your job. Fine-tuning is the bootcamp that turns that generalist into a specialist — by continuing to train it on examples of exactly the behaviour you want. This module walks the full toolkit, but the most important skill is knowing when to reach for it at all, because most of the time a cheaper rung on the ladder wins.

01  What fine-tuning actually is

A pretrained model already knows grammar, facts, code and reasoning. Fine-tuning takes that finished model and keeps training it on a smaller, curated dataset so it nudges its behaviour toward a narrower goal — a house writing style, a strict output format, a niche domain's vocabulary. You're not teaching it language from scratch; you're shaping habits it already has.

Hire a generalist, then send them to a bootcamp analogy

Imagine you hire a sharp graduate who knows a bit of everything. For most tasks you just brief them well (that's prompting). If they need to look things up, you give them a company wiki (that's RAG). But if you need them to think and write like a seasoned member of your team — your tone, your templates, your jargon — you send them to a focused bootcamp. Fine-tuning is that bootcamp: expensive and slow compared to a briefing, but it changes how they work by default.

Crucially, the bootcamp teaches skills and style, not a live feed of today's facts. That distinction drives the entire "when to fine-tune" decision below.

02  The adaptation ladder NEW

Before fine-tuning anything, climb the ladder from cheapest to most expensive and stop as soon as a rung solves your problem. Most teams should reach for the first two long before the third.

Climb only as high as you must
1 · prompt
just ask better
instructions, examples, format — minutes, no training
2 · RAG
give it the facts
retrieve documents at query time — fresh, citable knowledge
3 · fine-tune
change its habits
train on examples — style, format, domain skill baked in
4 · distill NEW
shrink the cost
teach a small model to copy a big one — cheap, fast serving
Cost, complexity and lead-time rise as you climb. Prompting changes nothing permanently; RAG changes what the model sees; fine-tuning changes what the model is; distillation changes which model you ship. (See RAG vs fine-tune for the head-to-head.)
How to say it out loud say it

“My default order is prompt, then RAG, then fine-tune, then distill. Each rung costs more and takes longer, so I only climb when the rung below provably can't hit the bar. Prompting and RAG solve a surprising share of problems; fine-tuning earns its keep for behaviour I can't reach with instructions or context.”

03  When fine-tuning is the right tool

Fine-tuning shines when the gap is about behaviour, not knowledge — things you can show with examples but struggle to fully describe in a prompt. The four classic wins:

Style & tone
A consistent voice (legal, medical, brand-safe, terse) that a system prompt keeps drifting away from across long outputs.
Format & structure
Always emit a specific schema, DSL, or markup — when prompting + a parser still leaks malformed outputs too often.
Domain skill
A narrow task the base model is mediocre at (a niche classification, a specialised code dialect) where you have labelled examples of "good".
Latency & cost
Bake a behaviour in so you can drop a long few-shot prompt — or move to a smaller model — cutting tokens per call. (This is also the door to distillation.)

The common thread: you can produce a few hundred to a few thousand examples of the exact behaviour you want, and that behaviour is stable (it won't change next week).

04  When NOT to fine-tune

The single most common interview trap is reaching for fine-tuning to inject fresh or changing facts. Don't. Training bakes knowledge in at a frozen moment, can't cite a source, is expensive to refresh, and still hallucinates. For facts that change — prices, policies, your latest docs — use RAG, which fetches the current truth at query time and can point to where it came from.

“We'll fine-tune the model on our knowledge base” — usually wrong gotcha

Fine-tuning teaches behaviour and style, not a reliable, updatable fact store. If you train on your docs, the model learns to sound like them but won't recite them accurately, can't cite, and goes stale the moment a doc changes. Reach for retrieval (RAG) for knowledge; reserve fine-tuning for how the model should behave. The strongest systems often do both: RAG for facts, a light fine-tune for format and tone.

Other "don't bother yet" signals: you haven't tried serious prompting; you have fewer than a few dozen quality examples; or you have no eval to prove the fine-tune actually helped. Without a before/after eval, you're flying blind.

06  Self-check

A team wants their support bot to always answer with the latest refund policy, which changes monthly. What should they reach for first?
Changing facts are a retrieval problem, not a training problem. RAG fetches the live policy and can cite it; monthly fine-tuning is slow, costly, can't cite, and still risks confidently quoting last month's number. Fine-tuning would only help with how the bot phrases answers, not what the current policy is.
“Walk me through how you'd decide between prompting, RAG, and fine-tuning.”Core
Model answer

I climb a ladder cheapest-first. Start with prompting — instructions and a few examples solve more than people expect. If the gap is missing or changing knowledge, add RAG so the model retrieves current, citable facts. I only fine-tune when the gap is behaviour — a style, a strict format, or a domain skill I can demonstrate with examples but can't fully spell out in a prompt. And I never fine-tune for fresh facts; that's a retrieval job. Often the best answer is RAG for knowledge plus a light fine-tune for tone and format, with an eval to prove each step helped.

How to say it out loud say it

“Prompt for behaviour I can describe, RAG for knowledge that changes, fine-tune for behaviour I can only show by example. Cheapest rung that clears the bar wins.”

Follow-ups: When would you do RAG and fine-tuning together? · What's the minimum before a fine-tune is worth it?
“Why does the ladder end with distillation?”Warm-up
Model answer

Once you have a model that behaves well — maybe a big one, or one you fine-tuned — distillation teaches a smaller, cheaper model to copy it. It's the last rung because it's about shipping economics: same behaviour, far lower latency and cost per call. You distill after you've nailed the behaviour, not before.

How to say it out loud say it

“Distillation is the cost rung: copy a strong model's behaviour into a small, fast one. You do it last, once the behaviour is already right.”
