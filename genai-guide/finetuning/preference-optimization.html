Fine-tuning · alignment

Aligning to preferences IV NEW

SFT teaches a model to imitate one good answer. But "good" is fuzzy — for the same prompt there are better and worse replies, and we want the model to prefer the better ones. Preference optimization is the family of methods that teach a model from comparisons — "this answer beats that one" — to make it more helpful, harmless, and honest. This is where RLHF, DPO, and the 2025–26 wave of simpler methods live.

01  Why alignment, and why SFT isn't enough

SFT can only show the model a single "correct" completion per prompt. It can't express "answer A is better than answer B," and it has no way to discourage the subtly-bad-but-plausible answers a model loves to produce. Alignment fills that gap, usually aiming at three goals people summarise as the 3 H's:

Helpful
Actually does what the user wants, clearly and completely.
Harmless
Refuses or redirects unsafe requests; doesn't produce harmful content.
Honest
Admits uncertainty, doesn't fabricate, doesn't manipulate.
A judge ranking two essays, not a teacher marking one analogy

SFT is a teacher handing back one model essay to copy. Preference optimization is a judge holding up two student essays and saying "this one's better." Over thousands of such comparisons, the model learns the taste behind the verdicts — not just one right answer, but a sense of what makes any answer better or worse. That's a far richer signal than imitation alone.

02  RLHF: the original recipe

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) was the breakthrough that made chat models feel aligned. It's a three-stage pipeline: SFT first, then train a reward model on human comparisons, then use reinforcement learning (classically PPO) to push the model toward answers the reward model scores highly.

RLHF in three stages
1 · SFT
teach good answers
imitate demonstrations
2 · reward model
learn human taste
trained on "A > B" labels to score answers
3 · PPO
optimize the policy
RL: maximise reward, stay near the SFT model
A KL penalty keeps the model from drifting too far from the SFT model while chasing reward — otherwise it finds weird, high-scoring gibberish ("reward hacking").
RLHF is powerful but fiddly gotcha

You're juggling several models at once (policy, reference, reward, and a value network for PPO), RL training is notoriously unstable, and a flawed reward model gets gamed — the policy learns to exploit its blind spots (reward hacking). This complexity is precisely why the field hunted for something simpler — which brings us to DPO.

03  DPO: skip the reward model NEW

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) asks a clever question: if all we have is preference pairs, why train a separate reward model and run unstable RL at all? DPO derives a loss that optimizes the model directly on (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples — nudging up the probability of the chosen answer and down the rejected one, while a frozen reference copy keeps it from drifting. No reward model, no PPO, no RL loop — just a contrastive loss you can run like ordinary fine-tuning.

That simplicity is why DPO swept the field: it's far more stable and cheaper to run, and for many use cases matches RLHF quality. As of June 2026 it (and its cousins below) have largely displaced vanilla RLHF for teams without a dedicated RL stack.

The key shift note

RLHF learns a reward function, then optimizes against it. DPO collapses those two steps into one: it treats the model itself as an implicit reward model and optimizes the preference directly. Fewer moving parts, fewer ways to break.

04  The 2025–26 family: ORPO, KTO, RLAIF, GRPO NEW

DPO opened the floodgates. Each newer method removes a different cost or constraint:

ORPO NEW
Odds Ratio Preference Optimization. Goes further than DPO by dropping the reference model too, and folds SFT and preference learning into a single step — one monolithic loss instead of "SFT then align." Simplest pipeline of all.
KTO NEW
Kahneman–Tversky Optimization. Needs only unary feedback — a thumbs-up or thumbs-down on a single answer — not paired comparisons. Perfect when your data is "users flagged this reply," which is far easier to collect than "A vs B."
RLAIF NEW
RL from AI Feedback. Replace expensive human labellers with a strong model that judges which answer is better, generating preference pairs at scale and low cost. Trades some human nuance for enormous throughput.
GRPO NEW
Group Relative Policy Optimization — the RL behind today's reasoning models. For each prompt it samples a group of answers, scores them with a verifiable reward (e.g. did the math/code actually pass?), and pushes toward the ones that beat the group average. No separate critic/value network needed.
GRPO is for verifiable rewards, not vibes gotcha

GRPO shines when correctness is checkable — math answers, unit tests, format validators — because the reward is objective and can't easily be gamed. Don't reach for it to teach fuzzy "niceness"; for taste-based preferences, DPO/ORPO on human (or RLAIF) pairs is the better fit. GRPO is the engine that made reasoning models leap on math and code (popularised by the DeepSeek line).

How to say it out loud say it

“Alignment teaches a model from preferences, not just imitation, aiming for helpful, harmless, honest. RLHF was first — reward model plus PPO — but it's complex and unstable. DPO skips the reward model and optimizes chosen-over-rejected directly, which is why it became the default. ORPO drops the reference model and merges SFT with alignment; KTO needs only thumbs up/down; RLAIF uses an AI judge to scale the pairs; and GRPO is the RL behind reasoning models, scoring a group of samples against a verifiable reward. I pick based on data and goal — DPO for general preference, GRPO when correctness is checkable.”

06  Self-check

The core thing DPO removes compared to RLHF is…
DPO still needs preference pairs (chosen/rejected) and still uses a frozen reference for regularization, but it derives a direct loss on those pairs — no learned reward model and no PPO/RL loop. That's what makes it simpler and more stable, and why it became the default. (ORPO goes one further and drops the reference model too.)
“DPO vs RLHF — why did DPO catch on?”Core
Model answer

RLHF works but is heavy: you train a separate reward model on human comparisons, then run PPO, juggling four models and unstable RL — and a weak reward model gets reward-hacked. DPO showed you can skip all that. It proves the optimal policy has a closed-form link to the preference data, so you can optimize the model directly on chosen/rejected pairs with a simple contrastive loss and a frozen reference. The result is far more stable, cheaper, runs like normal fine-tuning, and matches RLHF quality on many tasks. Lower cost, fewer failure modes, comparable results — so it became the default, with ORPO/KTO/RLAIF as variants for specific data situations.

How to say it out loud say it

“DPO drops the reward model and the RL loop — same preference pairs, a direct contrastive loss. Cheaper, more stable, comparable quality. That's why most teams reach for it first.”

Follow-ups: What does the reference model do in DPO? · When would you still pick RLHF or GRPO?
“Why is GRPO tied to reasoning models specifically?”Stretch
Model answer

Because reasoning tasks have verifiable answers — a math result is right or wrong, code passes tests or it doesn't. GRPO exploits that: for each problem it samples a group of attempts, checks each against the objective reward, and pushes the model toward attempts that beat the group's average, normalizing within the group instead of training a separate value network. That objective reward can't be vibe-gamed the way a learned reward model can, so the model genuinely gets better at getting answers right — which is exactly the signal you want for stronger chain-of-thought reasoning. It's the RL recipe credited with the big reasoning gains (DeepSeek line).

How to say it out loud say it

“GRPO samples a group of answers and rewards the ones that beat the group on a verifiable check — math correct, tests pass. Objective reward, no critic network, so reasoning genuinely improves.”
