Pretraining to post-training IV

We've run the model; now let's ask where its weights came from. A useful assistant isn't trained in one shot — it goes through pretraining (raw knowledge) and then post-training (manners and helpfulness). Understanding this pipeline explains base-vs-instruct models, why there's a knowledge cutoff, and why models hallucinate — all favourite interview territory.

01  School, then job training, then manners

A person becomes useful at work in stages: years of general schooling (broad knowledge), then job-specific training (how to actually do the role), then learning workplace manners (be helpful, clear, appropriate). LLM training mirrors this exactly — broad pretraining, then targeted instruction tuning, then alignment to human preferences.

A graduate vs a great colleague analogy

A fresh graduate knows an enormous amount but might ramble, answer the wrong question, or blurt something inappropriate. A great colleague has the same knowledge plus the instinct to answer what you actually asked, concisely and safely. Pretraining makes the graduate; post-training makes the colleague.

02  Pretraining: next-token prediction at scale

Pretraining is the giant first stage. The objective is the one you already know from the overview: predict the next token. Show the model trillions of tokens of text and code, hide the next token, let it guess, and nudge its weights toward the right answer — billions of times. No labels needed; the text is the supervision (this is “self-supervised” learning).

Out of that simple game, broad capability emerges: grammar, facts, translation, arithmetic, coding, reasoning patterns. It's also the expensive part — vast datasets and huge GPU clusters running for weeks or months. At a concept level, the three ingredients are data (how much, how clean, how diverse), compute (GPU-hours), and parameters (model size).

Objective
Next-token prediction (self-supervised) — no human labels for this stage.
Output
A base model: knowledgeable but not yet good at following instructions or chatting.
Cost
The dominant training expense; done rarely, then refreshed for newer data.

03  Scaling laws — and the shift to “think longer” NEW

Scaling laws are the empirical finding that model quality improves predictably as you grow data, compute and parameters together — bigger and better-fed models are reliably smarter. This insight drove the “just make it bigger” era and is why models ballooned in size.

The 2025–26 shift (worth flagging as recent): returns from sheer size have gotten harder and pricier to buy, so the frontier added a second axis — spend more at inference (test-time compute, the reasoning page). The mental model is no longer “only bigger,” it's “bigger and think longer,” often paired with smarter data and Mixture-of-Experts to grow capacity without growing per-token cost.

Two ways to spend, two ways to improve note

Train-time scaling bakes ability into the weights once; test-time compute buys better answers per request. Modern frontier results lean on both — a strong, well-trained model that also reasons when the task is hard (as of June 2026).

05  Base vs instruct vs reasoning variants

The same family often ships in several flavours, and picking the wrong one is a classic mistake:

VariantWhat it isUse it when
BasePretrained only — completes text, doesn't chat wellYou'll fine-tune it yourself, or want raw completion
Instruct / chatPost-trained to follow instructions and converseAlmost all app development — the default
ReasoningFurther trained to think before answeringHard math, planning, complex coding (see reasoning)

If a “raw” model gives rambling, unhelpful answers, you may have grabbed the base variant instead of the instruct one.

06  Knowledge cutoff & why it drives hallucination

Because knowledge is frozen into the weights during pretraining, every model has a knowledge cutoff — a date after which it simply hasn't seen the world. Ask about something newer and it has two options: admit it doesn't know, or — because it's trained to produce plausible text — confidently make something up. That second option is a hallucination.

A cutoff isn't the only cause of hallucination gotcha

Recency gaps are one source, but models also fabricate within their training window — citations, APIs, numbers — because they optimise for likely, not true. So “just retrain it” doesn't fix hallucination. The real mitigations are retrieval (give it fresh, grounded facts — RAG), tools (let it look things up or compute), and evaluation/guardrails. Mentioning the cutoff is good; pinning all hallucination on it is the trap.

How to say it out loud say it

“Training has two big phases. Pretraining is self-supervised next-token prediction over a huge corpus — it gives a knowledgeable base model and is where scaling laws bite. Post-training turns that base into an assistant: SFT to follow instructions, then preference optimization like RLHF or DPO to match human taste — yielding the instruct model we use, with reasoning variants for hard tasks. And because knowledge is frozen at a cutoff and the model favours plausible over true, it can hallucinate — which is exactly why retrieval, tools and evaluation matter.”

07  Self-check

What's the main difference between pretraining and post-training?
Pretraining = self-supervised next-token prediction at scale → a knowledgeable base model. Post-training = SFT then preference methods (RLHF/DPO) → an instruction-following, aligned assistant. (It's pretraining that's self-supervised, not the reverse.)
“Why do models hallucinate, and does a knowledge cutoff explain it?”Core
Model answer

They optimise for plausible text, not verified truth, so without grounding they can produce confident fabrications. A knowledge cutoff is one trigger — anything after pretraining is unknown, so it may guess — but models also hallucinate within their training window (fake citations, APIs, numbers). The fixes aren't “retrain”; they're retrieval (RAG) for fresh grounded facts, tool use, and evaluation/guardrails.

How to say it out loud say it

“Hallucination comes from optimising for likely, not true. The cutoff is one cause, but it isn't the whole story — so we add retrieval, tools and evaluation rather than just retraining.”

“What's the difference between a base model and an instruct model?”Warm-up
Model answer

A base model is pretrained only — it's great at continuing text but doesn't reliably follow instructions or chat. An instruct (or chat) model has been post-trained with SFT and preference optimization so it answers questions, follows directions and behaves safely. For almost all app work you want the instruct variant; you'd use the base model mainly if you're fine-tuning it yourself.

How to say it out loud say it

“Base = pretrained text-completer; instruct = post-trained to follow instructions and chat safely. Use instruct for apps; use base if you're going to fine-tune.”
