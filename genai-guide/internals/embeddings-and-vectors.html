Embeddings: meaning as coordinates IV

Stage 1 of the forward pass turns each token into a list of numbers called an embedding. The magic is which numbers: the model learns to place things with similar meaning close together, so “meaning” becomes literally a location in space. This one idea powers search, RAG, recommendations and clustering — and it's the foundation everything else in the model builds on.

01  A giant map of meaning

Imagine a map so detailed that every word, sentence, or document gets its own pin — and the rule is: things that mean similar things are pinned close together. “Dog” sits near “puppy” and “cat”; “bank (river)” sits far from “bank (money)”. An embedding is just that pin's coordinates, except instead of 2 numbers (latitude, longitude) it has hundreds or thousands.

A city where neighbourhoods are topics analogy

Picture a city where the “food” district, the “sports” district and the “finance” district each cluster together, and related ideas are on the same street. To find things related to a query, you don't read every building — you just go to the right neighbourhood and look nearby. That “go to the neighbourhood” move is exactly how vector search works.

02  What an embedding actually is

An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers (a vector) that represents a piece of content. A 1,024-dimensional embedding is just 1,024 numbers. Each number isn't individually meaningful — you can't point at slot 7 and say “that's the dog-ness axis.” What matters is the whole pattern, and especially how one vector's pattern compares to another's.

Crucially, embeddings are learned, not hand-written. During training the model nudges these numbers so that text used in similar ways ends up with similar vectors. That's why an embedding captures semantics (meaning) and not just spelling: “car” and “automobile” look nothing alike as strings but land close together as vectors.

Vector
An ordered list of numbers, e.g. [0.21, -0.08, …]. “Embedding” = a vector that stands for some content.
Dimensions
How many numbers are in the vector (e.g. 384, 768, 1024, 3072). More dims can hold more nuance, but cost more to store and compare.
Semantic
About meaning, not surface form. Embeddings are semantic: synonyms land near each other.

03  Token vs sentence vs document embeddings

The word “embedding” gets used at different scales, and interviewers love to check you know the difference:

KindRepresentsWhere it lives
Token embeddingA single tokenInside the LLM — stage 1 of the forward pass, before attention mixes them.
Sentence / passage embeddingA whole chunk of text as one vectorFrom a dedicated embedding model; used for search & RAG.
Document embeddingA long document (often via pooling its chunks)Same idea, larger input; usually you chunk first.

Inside an LLM, every token starts as a token embedding, and then attention contextualises it (the vector for “bank” shifts depending on whether “river” or “money” is nearby). A sentence embedding collapses many of these contextual vectors into one fixed vector that summarises the whole chunk.

This page is the intuition. The whole Embeddings & vectors module covers picking an embedding model, Matryoshka truncation, quantization, and ANN indexes (HNSW/IVF) for searching billions of vectors fast. Here we just need: meaning → coordinates → compare by distance.

04  Comparing vectors: cosine, dot, Euclidean

If meaning is location, “how related are these two things?” becomes “how close are these two vectors?” There are three common ways to measure that — no proofs needed, just the intuition:

Cosine similarity
Measures the angle between two vectors — are they pointing the same direction? Ignores length. This is the default for text because we care about topic/meaning, not how long the text was. Ranges −1 (opposite) to 1 (identical direction).
Dot product
Like cosine but also rewards longer vectors. If all vectors are normalised to length 1 (very common), dot product and cosine give the same ranking — which is why engines often use the cheaper dot product under the hood.
Euclidean (L2) distance
The straight-line “ruler” distance between the two pins. Smaller = closer. Sensitive to magnitude, so it's used less for raw text but shows up in some indexes.
Direction vs distance analogy

Two people pointing at the same star are “similar” even if one stands a mile behind the other — that's cosine (direction only). Euclidean asks how far apart the two people physically stand. For meaning we usually care about where they're pointing, so cosine wins.

Practical rule: normalise your vectors, then cosine and dot product agree — pick whichever your vector database makes fastest.

05  Why high-dimensional space works

It feels strange that hundreds of dimensions help — we can't even picture four. But each dimension is room to encode another independent shade of meaning: formality, sentiment, topic, tense, and thousands of subtler distinctions. A 2D map would force “king,” “queen,” “royal,” and “chess” to crowd together; high-dimensional space gives every concept enough elbow room to be near its true neighbours along many axes at once.

The flip side, worth a sentence in an interview: in very high dimensions almost everything is somewhat far from everything else (the “curse of dimensionality”), which is exactly why we need smart approximate nearest-neighbour indexes instead of brute-force comparison at scale — see the vector databases page.

06  “king − man + woman ≈ queen”

The famous demo: take the vector for “king,” subtract “man,” add “woman,” and the closest vector is “queen.” Because directions in the space line up with consistent concepts, relationships become arithmetic — there's a roughly consistent “royalty” direction and a “gender” direction, and you can walk along them.

It's a neat illustration, not a guarantee gotcha

This analogy comes from early word2vec-style embeddings and is great for intuition, but don't oversell it: the result is approximate, depends on the model, and modern contextual embeddings (where a word's vector changes with its sentence) don't behave like clean fixed arrows. Use it to explain the idea, then say “modern embeddings are contextual, so it's fuzzier than the slogan.”

How to say it out loud say it

“An embedding maps text to a vector so that similar meanings sit close together in space. We compare them by direction — cosine similarity, or dot product on normalised vectors — so synonyms score high even with different words. That's what makes semantic search and RAG possible: we embed the query and the documents into the same space and retrieve the nearest neighbours.”

07  Self-check

Why is cosine similarity usually preferred over Euclidean distance for comparing text embeddings?
Cosine looks at the angle between vectors, capturing semantic direction while ignoring magnitude. On normalised vectors it matches dot-product ranking; it does not generally match Euclidean ranking unless vectors are normalised.
“What is an embedding, in plain terms, and why is it useful?”Warm-up
Model answer

It's a vector — a fixed-length list of numbers — that represents a piece of content so that similar meanings are nearby in the space. It's useful because it turns “is this related?” into a fast distance calculation, which powers semantic search, RAG retrieval, clustering, deduplication and recommendations without keyword matching.

How to say it out loud say it

“An embedding is meaning as coordinates: similar text gets nearby vectors, so we can search by meaning instead of exact words.”

“How is a token embedding inside an LLM different from a sentence embedding from an embedding model?”Core
Model answer

A token embedding represents one token and is the model's input at stage 1; attention then contextualises it as it flows through the network. A sentence embedding is a single vector that summarises a whole chunk of text, produced by a dedicated embedding model (often by pooling the contextual token vectors). One is an internal per-token signal; the other is an output you store and search over.

How to say it out loud say it

“Token embeddings are per-token inputs the model contextualises internally; a sentence embedding is one vector summarising a chunk, made for storage and search.”
