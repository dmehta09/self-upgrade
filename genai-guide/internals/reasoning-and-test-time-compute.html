Reasoning & test-time compute IV NEW

For years, models got smarter by getting bigger. Since 2024 a second lever took over: let the model think longer at answer time. “Reasoning” models spend extra tokens working through a problem before replying — trading inference time and cost for accuracy on hard tasks. It's the defining shift of the current generation, and a near-guaranteed interview topic.

01  Show your working before the final answer

Think back to a maths exam: rushing straight to the answer loses marks and gets it wrong; writing out the steps catches mistakes and lands the right result. Reasoning models do exactly this — they generate an intermediate chain of thought (a scratchpad of steps) and only then commit to a final answer. The act of producing those steps measurably improves accuracy on problems that need several moves.

Scratch paper for a hard sum analogy

Ask someone “what's 17 × 24?” and they'll mutter “17 times 20 is 340, plus 17 times 4 is 68, so 408.” The mumbling is the work — skip it and they're more likely to blurt a wrong number. A reasoning model writes that mumbling out as tokens, because each step gives the next step something concrete to build on.

02  The separate “thinking” pass NEW

Mechanically, nothing exotic happens — it's still the same next-token loop from the overview. What's new is training the model to first emit a block of reasoning tokens (the chain of thought), then a clear final answer. Providers usually keep the raw reasoning hidden or summarised and bill it as separate “thinking” or “reasoning” tokens.

A reasoning model's two phases (one continuous generation)
prompt
your question
e.g. a multi-step word problem
thinking
reasoning tokens
step-by-step scratchpad (often hidden, still billed)
answer
final response
the part you actually see
More thinking tokens → more chances to self-correct → higher accuracy on hard problems, at the cost of latency and money. The skill these models learned (via RL on verifiable problems) is how to use that scratchpad well.

03  Test-time compute: spend more to get smarter NEW

Test-time compute (a.k.a. inference-time compute) is the headline idea: you can make a fixed model answer better by letting it do more work when it answers — more reasoning tokens, or sampling several attempts and picking the best. This is a genuine paradigm shift. The old scaling story was “train a bigger model on more data.” The post-2024 addition is “let the model think longer,” which keeps improving results on hard tasks without retraining anything.

Train-time compute
Spent once, up front, to bake capability into the weights. Bigger models, more data.
Test-time compute
Spent per request, at inference, to get a better answer from the same weights. Longer thinking, multiple samples, self-checking.

The two compose: a strong base model that also thinks longer is today's recipe for the hardest benchmarks (as of June 2026).

04  Controlling how hard it thinks NEW

Because thinking costs time and money, current APIs expose a dial — variously a reasoning-effort setting (e.g. low / medium / high) or an explicit thinking-budget (a cap on reasoning tokens). You're directly trading latency and cost against accuracy:

Effort / budgetLatency & costGood for
Low / noneFast, cheapSimple Q&A, formatting, chat, high-volume calls
MediumModerateEveryday tasks with some multi-step logic
High / large budgetSlow, priceyHard math, proofs, complex planning & coding

Treat it like a throttle: turn it up only when the task genuinely needs deliberation, and down for everything else to protect latency and budget.

05  When reasoning helps — and when it hurts NEW

The mature take (and what interviewers want to hear) is that reasoning is not a free upgrade. It shines on some tasks and actively backfires on others:

Reasoning helpsReasoning hurts
Multi-step math & logic puzzlesSimple factual recall (“What's the capital of France?”)
Planning & multi-constraint problemsLatency-sensitive UX (chat, autocomplete, voice)
Hard coding & debuggingHigh-volume / cost-sensitive pipelines
Anything verifiable with clear stepsOpen creative writing (can feel over-engineered)
Overthinking is a real failure mode gotcha

On easy questions, a reasoning model can talk itself out of the right answer, second-guessing an obvious fact through pages of needless steps — while you pay for every one of those tokens and wait longer. “Always use the reasoning model” is the wrong instinct: match the tool to the task. For trivial or latency-critical work, a fast non-reasoning model (or low effort) is usually better.

06  Distilling reasoning into smaller models

Running a big reasoning model for every request is expensive — so a popular move is distillation: have a strong reasoning model generate lots of high-quality chains of thought, then fine-tune a smaller model on them. The small model learns to imitate the reasoning style and ends up punching well above its size on the targeted tasks, at a fraction of the cost and latency. (Full treatment in Distillation & tooling.)

How to say it out loud say it

“Reasoning models are trained to generate a chain-of-thought scratchpad before the final answer, and that extra ‘thinking’ is test-time compute — spending more inference to get a better answer from the same weights. It's controllable via a reasoning-effort or thinking-budget knob. It clearly helps on math, logic, planning and hard coding, but hurts on simple recall and latency-sensitive work, where it adds cost and can even overthink. To get most of the benefit cheaply, you can distill a big reasoning model's chains into a smaller one.”

07  Self-check

What does “test-time compute” mean?
Test-time (inference-time) compute improves answers without changing the weights, by doing more work when responding: longer chain-of-thought, multiple samples, self-checking. It's the complement to train-time scaling and the defining lever of reasoning models.
“When would you NOT use a reasoning model?”Core
Model answer

For simple, fast, or high-volume work: factual lookups, classification, formatting, chat and voice where latency matters, and cost-sensitive pipelines. Reasoning adds tokens, latency and cost, and on easy questions can overthink its way to a worse answer. I'd reserve high reasoning effort for genuinely multi-step problems — hard math, planning, complex coding — and use a fast model or low effort otherwise.

How to say it out loud say it

“I match the tool to the task. Reasoning for multi-step math, planning and hard coding; a fast model for recall, formatting and latency-sensitive chat — reasoning there just adds cost and can overthink.”

“How is a reasoning model different from a normal model, mechanically?”Warm-up
Model answer

It's the same autoregressive next-token loop — no new architecture required. The difference is it's trained (often with reinforcement learning on verifiable problems) to first generate a chain-of-thought scratchpad and then the final answer. Those reasoning tokens are usually hidden or summarised but still generated and billed, and they're what lift accuracy on hard tasks.

How to say it out loud say it

“Same next-token loop, but trained to write a thinking scratchpad before answering. The extra tokens are the ‘reasoning’ — hidden but billed — and that's what raises accuracy.”
