RAG · overview

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

An LLM only knows what it learned in training — frozen, generic, and prone to confidently making things up. RAG fixes that by letting the model look things up first: you fetch the most relevant snippets from your own documents and paste them into the prompt, so the model answers from real evidence instead of memory. It's the single most common way companies put LLMs into production. Prefer the simple workflow pictures when you want the map of every architecture end to end.

01  The open-book exam

Imagine two students sitting the same exam. One memorised the whole textbook months ago and must answer purely from memory — fast, but fuzzy on details and out of date the moment the book gets a new edition. The other walks in with the book open on the desk, flips to the right page, and answers from what's in front of them.

Closed-book vs open-book analogy

A plain LLM is the closed-book student: everything it knows is baked in at training time. RAG turns it into the open-book student — same brain, but now it reads the relevant pages before answering. The model's job shifts from recall to reading comprehension, which it's far better at. Update the book and the answers update too — no retraining required.

That one shift — from recall to read-then-answer — is the whole idea. Everything else is plumbing to get the right pages onto the desk.

02  The naive RAG pipeline

Classic RAG splits into two phases. Indexing happens once, offline: you prepare your documents and store them so they're searchable. Retrieval & generation happens at query time, on every question. Here's the canonical pipeline:

Steps 1–4 are indexing (done once, offline). Steps 5–6 run per query. The retrieved chunks get pasted into the prompt — the model never "searches" on its own; you hand it the evidence. Each step has its own deep-dive in this guide.

Chunking and embedding live in the Embeddings & vectors module; here we focus on what happens once the vectors exist. The next page builds this whole loop from scratch in Python.

03  Why RAG, not just a bigger model

If LLMs are so capable, why bolt on a retrieval system at all? Four reasons keep RAG the production default in 2026:

Fresh & private data
A model's knowledge is frozen at its training cutoff and never saw your internal wiki, last night's tickets, or this morning's prices. RAG injects data the model couldn't possibly have memorised.
Citations & trust
Because the answer is built from specific retrieved chunks, you can show where it came from — link the source doc. Closed-book answers can't be traced; open-book ones can.
Less hallucination
Grounding the model in real text makes confident fabrication far less likely. The model is reading, not guessing — though, as we'll see, it can still go off-script.
Cheaper & faster to change
Adding a document is an insert, not a training run. Fine-tuning to teach new facts costs GPUs and time and risks forgetting; RAG updates the moment you re-index.

RAG vs fine-tuning is a classic interview question with real nuance — the last page of this module is devoted to answering it well.

04  RAG is a spectrum, not one technique NEW

The biggest misconception in interviews is treating "RAG" as a single fixed recipe. In 2026 it's better understood as a spectrum of sophistication — you climb it only as far as your quality bar demands:

The RAG spectrum (simple → sophisticated)

Start at the left. Each step up adds quality but also latency, cost, and complexity. Most production systems live at "hybrid + reranker" — the strong default (as of June 2026).
How to say it out loud say it

“RAG isn't one thing — it's a spectrum. The baseline is embed-and-retrieve top-k, but in practice the strong default is hybrid search plus a reranker. Beyond that you've got late-interaction models like ColBERT, GraphRAG for multi-hop questions, and agentic RAG that retrieves in a loop. I'd start simple and climb only as far as the eval scores force me to.”

05  Retrieval is the bottleneck, not generation

Here's the mental model that separates a junior answer from a senior one: if your RAG system gives bad answers, the problem is almost always retrieval, not the LLM. Modern models are excellent at reading and summarising — feed them the right chunks and they'll nail it. Feed them irrelevant or missing chunks and even the best model produces garbage. "Garbage in, garbage out" has never been truer.

Don't reach for a bigger model first gotcha

When a RAG demo disappoints, the instinct is to swap in a smarter (pricier) LLM. Usually that's the wrong lever. Check first: did the right chunk even make it into the prompt? If the answer wasn't in the retrieved context, no model can save you. Tune chunking, add hybrid search, add a reranker — fix the retrieval before you touch the generator.

07  Self-check

A colleague says “let's use RAG so the model is smarter at reasoning.” What's the cleanest correction?
RAG is about knowledge, not raw intelligence. It supplies fresh, private, or specific facts at query time so the model can read rather than guess. The model's reasoning ability is unchanged — you've just put the right pages on its desk.
“What is RAG, in one breath?”Warm-up
Model answer

Retrieval-Augmented Generation: before the LLM answers, you search a knowledge store for the most relevant text chunks and paste them into the prompt as context. The model answers from that evidence instead of from memory — so you get fresh, private, citable, less-hallucinated answers without retraining.

How to say it out loud say it

“RAG turns a closed-book model into an open-book one: retrieve relevant chunks, stuff them in the prompt, answer from them. Fresh data, citations, less hallucination — and no fine-tuning.”

“Why is RAG often preferred over fine-tuning for adding knowledge?”Core
Model answer

Because facts change and fine-tuning bakes them in expensively. RAG updates the moment you insert a document — no GPU run, no risk of catastrophic forgetting. It also gives citations and keeps data access controllable (you can filter what a user is allowed to retrieve). Fine-tuning is better for changing behaviour or format, not for injecting facts.

How to say it out loud say it

“Use RAG for knowledge that changes or is private — it's an insert, not a training run, and it's citable. Use fine-tuning for behaviour and style. They're complementary, not rivals.”
