RAG

GraphRAG: graphs meet retrieval IV NEW

Chunk-based RAG answers questions whose answer sits inside one chunk. But some answers don't live anywhere — they're spread across documents, connected by relationships no single passage spells out. GraphRAG spends money at indexing time to build a knowledge graph and pre-written summaries, so that at query time you can walk connections (multi-hop) or read the whole forest at once (global themes). Think of it as upgrading from a box of index cards to a detective's pinboard. Simple map: architectures & workflows → graph.

01  Where chunk-RAG hits a wall

Top-k retrieval has one move: embed the question, fetch the k most similar chunks. That move fails on two whole families of questions:

Multi-hop questions
“Which customers were affected by outages caused by vendor X?” The vendor lives in a contract, the outage in an incident report, the customers in a postmortem. Each document is individually a weak match for the question — and the chunk that connects them doesn't exist. Similarity search can fetch dots; it cannot connect them.
Global / corpus-level questions
“What are the main themes across all our support tickets?” The answer is a property of the whole collection, not of any chunk. Top-k hands the model 5 random-ish tickets and asks it to generalise — it will, confidently, and wrongly.
Index cards vs the detective's pinboard analogy

A vector store is a box of index cards with a brilliant librarian: ask anything, get the five most relevant cards in milliseconds. But ask “how is the vendor connected to the angry customers?” and five cards won't say — the answer is the red string between cards. GraphRAG builds the pinboard up front: every person, product and event pinned, every known relationship strung. Multi-hop questions become “follow the string”; theme questions become “step back and look at the clusters.”

The previous lesson introduced this idea in one breath. Here we open the hood: how the graph gets built, how the two query modes work, and how the 2025–26 variants made the whole thing affordable.

02  The indexing pipeline: pay now, answer later

Microsoft's GraphRAG (the reference shape everyone riffs on) front-loads the hard work. Indexing is a four-stage LLM pipeline:

Every box on the left of the arrowheads feeds the next. The output is two artifacts: a knowledge graph (for walking) and a pyramid of community summaries (for reading).
1 · Entity & relation extraction
An LLM reads each chunk and emits structured triples: Vendor X —supplies→ payments service, Outage-17 —affected→ Acme. People, orgs, products, events become candidate nodes; the verbs between them become edges, each carrying a short description and a pointer back to its source chunk.
2 · Graph construction
Mentions are merged — the “Vendor X” from the contract and the “VendorX Inc.” from the incident report become one node. This entity resolution is what lets facts from different documents finally touch.
3 · Community detection (Leiden)
A graph-clustering algorithm (Leiden, a sharper successor to Louvain) carves the graph into communities of densely connected entities — “everything around the payments vendor,” “the mobile-app complaints cluster.” It runs hierarchically: small communities nest inside bigger ones.
4 · Community summaries
An LLM writes a report for every community at every level — “this cluster is about recurring billing failures involving…”. These pre-baked summaries are what make corpus-wide questions answerable later without re-reading the corpus.
Indexing is the bill — and it keeps arriving gotcha

Count the LLM calls: one (or more) per chunk for extraction, plus one per community for summaries. On a 100k-chunk corpus that's six figures of LLM calls before anyone asks a single question — and the cost scales with corpus size, not query volume. Worse: when documents change, affected entities, communities and summaries must be re-extracted and re-summarised, so a fast-moving corpus pays the toll over and over. This is the number-one reason teams trial GraphRAG and retreat — and exactly what the lazy variants attack.

03  Querying: local search vs global search

The two index artifacts power two distinct query modes — and picking the right one per question is the practical skill:

Local search multi-hop
For entity-anchored questions. Match entities mentioned in the query to graph nodes, then walk their neighborhood: follow edges to connected entities, collect the relationship facts, and pull the raw source chunks behind each edge for grounding. The context handed to the generator is “everything within a couple of hops of Vendor X,” not “whatever sounded similar.”
Global search themes
For corpus-wide questions. Ignore chunks entirely; run a map-reduce over community summaries. Map: every relevant summary answers the question in parallel (“what themes do you see?”). Reduce: rate the partial answers, keep the useful ones, merge them into one synthesis. The hierarchy lets you trade detail for cost — top-level reports for a broad sweep, deeper levels when you need fine grain.

04  LazyGraphRAG: defer the expensive part NEW

If full GraphRAG's sin is paying for every community summary up front — including the thousands no query will ever touch — the fix writes itself: be lazy. LazyGraphRAG-style variants (Microsoft's LazyGraphRAG late 2024, then a wave of follow-ons through 2025–26) flip the cost from index time to query time:

Index time: cheap NLP, no LLM
Extract candidate entities with classic NLP (noun-phrase extraction, co-occurrence) instead of LLM calls, and cluster that lightweight graph. No pre-written summaries at all. Indexing lands near plain vector-RAG cost — Microsoft's headline figure was roughly 0.1% of full GraphRAG's indexing bill.
Query time: summarise only what the question touches
When a global question arrives, use it to pick the few relevant communities, then run the LLM map-reduce over just those — relevance-guided, on demand. Each query costs a bit more; the corpus-sized upfront toll disappears, and stale-summary re-indexing pain mostly goes with it.

This is where the field actually moved in 2025–26: graph structure stays cheap and always-fresh, LLM summarisation happens lazily where a query proves it's needed. In an interview, knowing the lazy variant exists is the difference between “I read the GraphRAG paper” and “I'd actually deploy this.”

Same trade-off, third costume note

You've met this shape before: precompute vs compute-on-demand. Full GraphRAG is “bake everything at index time” (fast queries, huge upfront cost); LazyGraphRAG is “bake on first use” (cheap index, queries pay as they go). Pick by your query volume: many repeated global questions amortise the full bake; sparse or unpredictable ones favour lazy.

05  Choosing: hybrid + rerank vs GraphRAG vs long context

GraphRAG competes with two simpler answers: a tuned hybrid + reranking pipeline, and just stuffing the corpus into a long-context window. Choose by question shape first, then corpus size and budget:

“Walk me through how GraphRAG works and when you'd actually deploy it.”Core
Model answer

At index time an LLM extracts entities and relations from each chunk into a knowledge graph; Leiden clustering groups entities into hierarchical communities, and an LLM writes a summary per community. At query time there are two modes: local search matches query entities to nodes and walks their neighborhood for multi-hop questions, and global search map-reduces over community summaries for corpus-wide themes. I'd deploy it only when eval shows multi-hop or thematic questions failing under hybrid + rerank — and I'd reach for a LazyGraphRAG-style variant first, since cheap NLP indexing with query-time summarisation keeps most of the benefit at near vector-search indexing cost.

How to say it out loud say it

“GraphRAG: LLM-extracted entity graph plus Leiden communities plus per-community summaries at index time; local search walks the graph for multi-hop, global search map-reduces summaries for themes. Expensive to index, so in 2026 I'd start lazy — cheap NLP graph up front, LLM summaries on demand — and only for corpora where connected or global questions actually fail simpler RAG.”

06  Self-check

“Which customers were affected by outages caused by vendor X?” Why does top-k chunk retrieval fail here, even with a great embedding model?
It's a multi-hop question: each fact lives in a different document, and the connection between them isn't written anywhere. Similarity search retrieves chunks that resemble the question — it can't join facts across documents. GraphRAG's local search fixes this by matching “vendor X” to a graph node and walking its edges to the outage and on to the customers.
A user asks “what are the dominant themes across all our incident reports?” Which GraphRAG query mode handles this, and how?
Theme questions have no entity to anchor on and no chunk that holds the answer. Global search ignores chunks: every relevant community summary answers the question in parallel (map), then the partial answers are scored and merged (reduce). The hierarchical summaries were precomputed at index time precisely so this is affordable at query time.
What does LazyGraphRAG change relative to full GraphRAG?
Lazy variants keep the graph structure but stop pre-paying for summaries nobody asked for: noun-phrase/co-occurrence extraction replaces per-chunk LLM calls at index time (near vector-search indexing cost), and the LLM map-reduce runs on demand over just the relevant communities. Most of the multi-hop and thematic benefit, a fraction of the upfront bill — which is why the field converged here in 2025–26.
