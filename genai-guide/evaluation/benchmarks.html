Reading the leaderboards IV NEW

Every new model launch screams a leaderboard score. Benchmarks are useful for a rough sense of capability — but they're marketing-adjacent, and they lie in predictable ways. The senior move is to read them skeptically and then ignore them in favour of your own eval. This page covers the benchmarks that matter (as of June 2026), why contamination and saturation make them mislead, and the one lesson that actually wins interviews.

01  The benchmarks that matter NEW

Names churn, but the categories are stable. These are the ones quoted in 2026 launches (as of June 2026):

BenchmarkMeasuresNote
MMLU-ProBroad knowledge & reasoning across many subjects (a harder successor to MMLU)The general-knowledge default
GPQA DiamondGraduate-level science questions written to be “Google-proof”Hard reasoning, resists memorisation
SWE-bench VerifiedResolving real GitHub issues — does the patch actually fix the bug?The headline agentic-coding benchmark
AIMECompetition math (multi-step problem solving)A favourite for reasoning models
Arena / human-preferenceCrowd votes on head-to-head answers → an Elo-style rankingCaptures “which feels better” — but is a popularity signal, gameable by style

The split is worth naming: static benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, AIME, SWE-bench) have fixed questions and an answer key; arena-style rankings are live human preference. Each fails differently — read on.

02  Contamination & saturation — why leaderboards mislead NEW

Two failure modes quietly inflate scores. Knowing both names marks you as someone who's actually read the fine print:

Contamination (the leaked exam)
If the benchmark's questions and answers were in the model's training data, it can recite them rather than reason. Since models train on huge web scrapes, popular public benchmarks almost certainly leak in — so a high score may reflect memorisation, not skill.
Saturation (the ceiling)
When the top models all score 90%+, the benchmark stops discriminating — everyone “passes,” and the remaining gap is noise or test errors. MMLU saturated, which is exactly why harder successors like MMLU-Pro and GPQA exist.
A student who got the answer key analogy

Imagine grading students on an exam whose answer key leaked the night before. A 95% tells you nothing about who actually understands the material — they could've just memorised. That's contamination. And if the exam is so easy that everyone scores 95% anyway, the test can't tell your top students apart — that's saturation. Public LLM benchmarks suffer from both, which is why a leaderboard rank is a weak proxy for "will this work for me."

Benchmarks measure the benchmark, not your task gotcha

Even a clean, unsaturated benchmark tests its distribution of questions — not your support tickets, your codebase, your tone. A model that tops SWE-bench may flop on your internal framework; a great MMLU score says nothing about whether it follows your formatting rules. Never pick a model for production off a public leaderboard alone. Vendors also sometimes report cherry-picked configs, so treat launch numbers as a starting hypothesis, not proof.

03  Reading a launch claim like a skeptic

When a model launch flashes a benchmark chart, run a quick mental checklist before you believe it:

Same settings?
Was every model run with the same prompt, the same number of attempts, and the same reasoning effort? "Best-of-many tries" vs "single shot" are wildly different numbers.
Cherry-picked subset?
Vendors quote the benchmarks they win. Look for the ones they didn't show, not just the ones they did.
How big is the gap?
A 0.5-point lead on a saturated benchmark is noise. Treat tiny margins as ties.
Reasoning vs knowledge?
A reasoning model that aces AIME may be slower and worse at plain knowledge recall — match the benchmark to your task type.

None of this means benchmarks are worthless — they're a real signal of the capability class. It means you read them the way you'd read any vendor's own marketing: as a hypothesis to verify, not a verdict.

04  The real lesson: build your own eval set

Here's the punchline that ties the whole module together. Public benchmarks are fine for a shortlist — "these three models are roughly in the right class." But the decision is made on a task-specific eval set you build yourself: a few dozen-to-hundred real examples from your use case, scored with your metrics (faithfulness, your format, your tone). That eval can't be contaminated, can't be saturated, and measures exactly the thing you care about.

How to say it out loud — “why not just trust MMLU?” say it

“Two reasons. First, contamination — MMLU is all over the web, so models may have trained on it and recite answers rather than reason. Second, saturation — top models all score in the 90s, so it no longer discriminates, which is why harder successors like MMLU-Pro and GPQA exist. And even a clean benchmark tests its own questions, not my support tickets. So I use leaderboards only to shortlist, then decide on a task-specific eval set built from real examples and scored with my own metrics, alongside cost and latency.”

05  Self-check

A model tops the MMLU leaderboard. Why is that weak evidence it'll work well for your customer-support bot?
High public-benchmark scores can reflect memorisation (contamination) or a saturated ceiling, and even when clean they measure the benchmark's tasks, not yours. You shortlist with leaderboards but decide with a task-specific eval set on real examples.
