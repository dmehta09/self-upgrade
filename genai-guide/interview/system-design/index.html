The 6-step GenAI design method IV

“Design a chatbot over our docs.” “Design an AI coding assistant.” The GenAI system-design round feels open-ended, but it's really a checklist. Run these six steps out loud, every time, and you'll sound structured and senior — even on a problem you've never seen.

01  Why a method beats winging it

Interviewers aren't grading whether you know one magic architecture. They're watching how you think: do you scope before building, surface trade-offs, and remember cost, latency and safety? A repeatable method makes all of that visible. Narrate each step — “first I'd clarify requirements, then pin down SLAs…” — so they can follow your reasoning. (These cases assume the classic HLD toolbox — load balancers, queues, caches, sharding; if that's shaky, run through the System Design Field Guide first.)

Like a doctor, not a vending machine analogy

A weak candidate is a vending machine: question in, memorised architecture out. A strong one is a doctor — they ask where it hurts (requirements), check vitals (SLAs), then prescribe. The diagnosis is half the score.

02  The six steps

1

Requirements & clarifying questions

Who uses it? What does “good” mean (accuracy, tone, citations)? What's explicitly in and out of scope? Volume? Languages? Pin this down before drawing a single box.

2

Constraints, SLAs & NFRs

The GenAI-specific spine: latency (p50/p95, streaming?), throughput (QPS, tokens/s), cost per request, data freshness, safety/abuse tolerance, privacy/data-residency. State numbers, even rough ones.

3

High-level architecture

Draw the boxes and arrows (next section). Keep it to the main request path first; you'll deepen the risky boxes next.

4

Component deep-dives

Zoom into the boxes that matter: chunking & embeddings, retrieval (hybrid + rerank), prompt assembly, the model choice, caching, streaming, tools.

5

Trade-offs & bottlenecks

RAG vs fine-tune vs long-context; rerank or not; self-host vs API; sync vs async. Name where it breaks — stale index, prompt injection, cold starts, cost blow-ups.

6

Evaluation, extensions & scaling

How you'd measure quality (offline eval set + online metrics + guardrails), then how it grows: multi-tenant, sharded vector store, caching layers, model routing.

03  The architecture skeleton (step 3, reusable)

04  The NFRs interviewers want to hear (step 2)

Junior answers jump straight to the model. Senior answers anchor on these first — make them first-class:

Latency
p50/p95; can you stream tokens to hide it? Time-to-first-token vs total.
Throughput
Requests/sec and tokens/sec at peak; batching strategy.
Cost / request
Tokens × price; caching and model routing to control it.
Freshness
How current must answers be? Drives re-indexing cadence (RAG) vs retraining.
Safety / abuse
Prompt injection, toxic output, data exfiltration; where guardrails sit.
Privacy
Can data leave your walls? Often the deciding factor for self-host vs API.

05  Worked case studies

Seven end-to-end walkthroughs apply the method to real prompts. Each ends with “your turn” extensions.

RAG chatbot over docs →

Retrieval quality vs latency vs cost; freshness; grounding & citations.

Agentic assistant →

Tool-calling loop, planning, cost of multi-step, guardrails on actions.

LLM gateway →

Routing, rate-limits, fallback, caching, observability, cost attribution.

Multimodal search →

Joint image+text embeddings, cross-modal retrieval, index size.

Real-time voice →

Streaming STT → LLM → TTS, end-to-end p95, barge-in.

Moderation & guardrails →

Layered filters, precision/recall, latency budget, human-in-the-loop.

…plus a fine-tune & eval pipeline — data curation, training, and regression-gated rollout.

06  Warm-up

You're asked to “design a support chatbot.” What's the best first move?
Scope and constraints first. Requirements + NFRs shape every later decision; jumping to a model or a fixed diagram signals you skip the thinking interviewers are grading.
“How do you keep a GenAI design answer from rambling?”Core
Model answer

Announce the six steps up front and walk them in order, time-boxing each. Spend the most time on the risky boxes (usually retrieval and evaluation). End with trade-offs and scaling so you finish on a senior note.

How to say it out loud say it

“I'll work in six steps — requirements, SLAs, architecture, deep-dives, trade-offs, then eval and scaling — and spend most of my time on retrieval and evaluation since that's where quality and cost live.”
