Design a fine-tune & eval pipeline IV

A spicier prompt than RAG: “we keep a small model in production for a narrow task — say, classifying support tickets or rewriting them into a house style — design a repeatable pipeline to fine-tune it for that task and prove it's actually better before shipping.” The trap is to talk only about training. The senior move is to treat evaluation as the spine — the whole point is a gate that won't let a worse model out the door. We'll run the 6-step method top to bottom and land on the failure modes that separate “I trained a model” from “I ship model improvements on a cadence, safely.”

01  Step 1 — Requirements & clarifying questions

Don't reach for a training framework yet. The first thing to nail is what behaviour we're specialising and whether fine-tuning is even the right tool — interviewers love watching you talk yourself out of training when a prompt would do. Four questions I'd ask:

What task or behaviour?

Be concrete: a classification/extraction task (ticket → category + priority), a style/format task (match a house voice, always emit valid JSON), or a domain skill? The shape of the task decides the data format, the metric, and whether a tiny model can even win.

Why fine-tune at all?

Fine-tune teaches behaviour and form; RAG/prompting inject knowledge. If the gap is “doesn't know our facts,” that's retrieval, not training. I'd justify fine-tuning only for consistent format, latency/cost (a small tuned model beating a big prompted one), or a style a prompt can't hold — see RAG vs fine-tune vs long-context.

What data do we have?

How many labelled examples exist today, how clean, and where from — historical logs, human labels, a bigger “teacher” model? Quantity, licensing and PII all gate the design. “We have 300 messy examples” and “we have 50k clean ones” are different projects.

Who owns the quality bar?

Who decides “better,” and against what? A product owner with a labelled golden set, or just vibes? This is the make-or-break detail: without an agreed eval, you can't gate, and the whole pipeline is theatre.

My assumptions (stated, so I can move on): the task is rewriting raw support tickets into a structured, on-brand summary (category, severity, a one-line house-style synopsis). We run a small open model (≈7–8B) self-hosted because volume is high and latency matters. We have ~20k historical tickets with human-edited “gold” rewrites, growing weekly, and a product owner who signs off on a frozen eval set. Fine-tune is justified by format consistency + per-call cost at our volume, not by knowledge. I'll flag where a different answer flips the design.

02  Step 2 — Constraints, SLAs & NFRs

For a training pipeline the NFRs are less about per-request latency and more about how much better, how cheaply, how safely, and how often. Pin rough numbers — every later trade-off references them:

Quality lift
A target, agreed up front: e.g. on the gold set, primary task metric +8–15 pts over the prompted-baseline, and the LLM-judge “house-style” score ≥ 4.3/5, with no regression on a held-out safety/refusal set. “Better” must be a number before we train.
Inference budget
The tuned small model must serve at p95 ≤ 700 ms and ~5–10× cheaper per call than the big prompted model it replaces — that cost/latency win is usually the reason we're fine-tuning, so it's a hard constraint, not a nice-to-have.
Training cost / cadence
One LoRA run should be hours, not days, and cost on the order of tens of dollars of GPU — cheap enough to re-run weekly or on demand. If a run costs thousands, you won't iterate, and iteration is the point.
Eval coverage
A frozen golden set of ~300–1,000 labelled examples spanning the real distribution (common cases + rare/hard slices + adversarial), plus per-slice breakdowns so a gain isn't hiding a regression on a minority class.
Rollback safety
Every shipped model is versioned and instantly reversible. Canary at small traffic %, automatic rollback on a live-metric drop, and the previous adapter kept warm. Mean-time-to-rollback in seconds, not a redeploy.
Reproducibility
Given a model version I can name its exact base checkpoint, data snapshot, hyper-params and eval scores. Non-reproducible training is the silent killer — you can't debug a regression you can't recreate.

03  Step 3 — High-level architecture

Like RAG, this is two loops, and saying so explicitly is the move: a build/train loop that produces a candidate model, and a gated release loop that only lets it out if it clears eval — then a feedback loop from production back into the data. Draw it as one pipeline with a gate in the middle.

The gate is the heart of the system: a candidate that doesn't beat the current production model on the frozen test set never reaches canary. The dashed return arrow — production failures and edited outputs flowing back into the labelled set — is what makes it a pipeline and not a one-off science project.
Coaching an athlete, with a stopwatch analogy

Fine-tuning is coaching: the base model is a generally-fit athlete, your data is the drills for one event, and the adapter is the muscle memory you add. But coaching without a stopwatch is just vibes — the eval harness is the timed trial on a fixed track (the frozen test set). You only put the new technique into the race (production) if it beats the old time. And you keep timing during the race, because looking good in practice doesn't mean faster on the day.

04  Step 4 — Component deep-dives

Zoom into the boxes that decide whether this works. I'd lead with the data and the eval harness, because that's where this pipeline lives or dies — the training step is almost the easy part:

Dataset design
Quality > quantity: a few thousand clean, on-distribution examples beat 50k noisy ones. Dedup aggressively (near-duplicates inflate scores and waste compute), normalise format, and split before anything else into train/val/test — the test set is frozen and sacred. Watch for label noise and PII. See SFT & data.
Which adaptation?
SFT on input→output pairs is the default first step. Do it parameter-efficiently with LoRA/QLoRA — you train a small adapter, not the full model, so runs are cheap, reversible and stackable; full fine-tuning is rarely worth it for a single task. Add DPO (preference optimisation) only once SFT plateaus and you have pairwise “A is better than B” data to push tone/preference. See PEFT · LoRA/QLoRA and preference optimization.
The eval harness
Three layers on the frozen golden set: (1) deterministic task metrics — exact-match/F1 for the category, valid-JSON rate, schema validity; cheap, objective, run every time. (2) LLM-as-judge for the fuzzy “house-style” quality, with a rubric and pairwise comparison vs the baseline to dodge score drift. (3) where retrieval is in play, RAGAS-style faithfulness. Always compare the candidate against the current production model, not against nothing. See Evaluation and LLM-as-judge.
Judge calibration
An LLM judge is itself a model that can be wrong or biased (it favours longer answers, its own style, position A). Calibrate it against a sample of human labels, measure agreement, randomise option order, and use a stronger model as judge than the one under test. An uncalibrated judge gives you confident, wrong green lights.
Experiment tracking
Every run logs: base checkpoint hash, data snapshot version, hyper-params, seed, and the full eval scorecard, in a tracker + model registry. This is what makes a regression debuggable and a winner reproducible — and it's the difference between a pipeline and a folder of mystery checkpoints.
Versioning
Treat data, adapter and eval set as independently versioned artifacts. A model version pins which data snapshot and which eval set produced its scores, so “v7 beat v6” is a claim you can re-run a month later.
How to say it out loud say it

“I start with SFT via LoRA on a small, deduped, split dataset, and I freeze a golden test set before I train. The harness runs deterministic task metrics plus a calibrated LLM-judge, always head-to-head against the current production model — and that comparison is a CI gate. Only DPO once SFT plateaus and I have preference pairs.”

05  Step 5 — Trade-offs & bottlenecks

Senior answers name the forks and pick one with a reason. The four that come up every time here:

Build cadenceContinuous auto-retrainPeriodic / on-demandPeriodic + trigger. Scheduled (e.g. weekly) plus an on-demand run when drift fires. Fully-continuous retraining is a great way to silently train on your own bad outputs — keep a human/gate in the loop.
The four ways a fine-tune pipeline lies to you gotcha

Eval-set leakage / overfitting — training examples (or near-duplicates) bleed into the test set, so scores soar and production doesn't (fix: dedup across splits, freeze the test set, split before augmenting). Distribution shift — the eval set reflects last quarter; real traffic moved, so a “+10 pts” model underperforms live (fix: refresh the golden set from recent traffic, monitor input drift). Catastrophic forgetting — tuning the new task quietly degrades general ability or safety/refusals (fix: keep a held-out general + safety suite in the gate, prefer LoRA, mix in some general data). “Better on vibes, not on the eval” — the demo feels nicer but the numbers don't move, or the LLM-judge is uncalibrated and rubber-stamps it (fix: trust the calibrated head-to-head metric over the demo, and never ship on a single cherry-picked example).

06  Step 6 — Evaluation, extensions & scaling

Finish on a senior note: how this hardens into infrastructure and grows. The throughline in June 2026 is that model changes go through CI like code changes — a model that fails the eval gate is a failing build, full stop.

Regression-gated CI
Wire the harness into CI/CD: a new adapter triggers the eval suite, and the merge/deploy is blocked unless it beats the production model on the frozen set with no slice regressing. The scorecard is a required check, like tests. Now improving the model is a normal, safe PR.
Add preference data + DPO
Once SFT plateaus, mine production for pairs (the human-edited rewrite is “chosen,” the model's draft is “rejected”) and run DPO to sharpen tone/preference cheaply, without a separate reward model. Re-gate on the same eval set so the preference tune can't quietly cost you task accuracy.
Auto-retrain on drift
Monitor input distribution and live quality; when drift or a metric dip crosses a threshold, auto-trigger a retrain on a refreshed snapshot — which then still has to pass the gate and canary before it ships. Automation proposes; the gate disposes.
Scaling the pipeline
Parallelise eval (it's embarrassingly parallel and often the slow part), cache the baseline's scores, serve multiple adapters behind one base model (swap adapters per request/tenant), and treat the golden set as a living, versioned asset that grows from the feedback loop.

Your turn · two extensions

(1) Your weekly auto-retrain quietly starts training on the model's own outputs (logged answers that were never human-checked). What goes wrong, and how do you prevent it? (2) Make the pipeline multi-task / multi-tenant — many small specialisations off one base model. What changes?

Think about where ground-truth comes from in a feedback loop, and what stays shared vs per-tenant when you fan out before you peek.

Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

For the feedback loop: outputs that were never verified aren't ground truth — training on them is a self-reinforcing echo (“model collapse”). You need a label step (human edit/approval, or at least a high-confidence filter) before any logged output is allowed into the training set. For multi-tenant: the base model and eval harness stay shared, but each tenant needs its own adapter, its own frozen golden set, and its own gate — one tenant's win is another's regression.

Show a solution solution

Training on your own outputs. This is the classic feedback-loop trap: raw production generations are predictions, not labels, so retraining on them just amplifies the model's current biases and errors — quality drifts down while the eval set (if it's also stale) fails to notice. The fix is a hard rule that only verified examples enter the training set: the human-edited final rewrite is gold; an unedited model draft is not. Mine production for hard/low-confidence/thumbs-down cases, route those to human labelling, and add the corrected versions. Keep the frozen test set sourced from human labels only, refresh it from recent traffic, and the gate will catch any collapse before it ships. (Bonus: the model-vs-human edit becomes a free DPO preference pair.)

Multi-task / multi-tenant. Keep one base model and serve many LoRA adapters on top, swapped per tenant/task at request time — cheap to store, cheap to add a new one. What must be per-tenant: the dataset, the frozen golden set, and the eval gate (each specialisation is judged on its own metrics; a global average would let one tenant's regression hide behind another's gain). The training harness, the experiment tracker, the registry and the CI gating logic are shared infrastructure. Watch for cross-tenant data leakage (never train one tenant's adapter on another's data) and for the base model itself drifting — if you ever update the base, every adapter must be re-gated against its own set, because adapters are tied to the base they were trained on.

Your fine-tuned model scores +12 points on your eval set, but users complain it's no better — sometimes worse. What's the most likely cause?
A big offline gain that doesn't show up in production almost always means the eval set is lying — near-duplicates bled across the train/test split (leakage), or the frozen set drifted away from live traffic (staleness). Fix the measurement first: dedup across splits, freeze the test set, and refresh it from recent traffic. More epochs or full-FT would just overfit the same flawed yardstick harder.
“How do you decide between fine-tuning, RAG, and just prompting?”Core
Model answer

Match the tool to the gap. If the model lacks knowledge (our docs, fresh facts), that's RAG — retrieval injects it at query time and stays current. If the gap is capability or instruction-following on a one-off, try a better prompt first; it's free and instant. Reach for fine-tuning when you need consistent form/style/format, or when a small tuned model can match a big prompted one at far lower latency/cost at volume — that cost win is often the real driver. They compose: I'd often fine-tune a small model and give it RAG. I never fine-tune to teach facts that change — it's expensive to refresh and goes stale.

How to say it out loud say it

“Prompt for behaviour you can describe, RAG for knowledge that changes, fine-tune for form and for cost/latency at volume. They stack — a tuned small model with RAG is common — and I never fine-tune facts that go stale.”

“Your only judge of quality is an LLM-as-judge. How do you trust it?”Core
Model answer

I treat the judge as a model that itself needs evaluating. First, calibrate it against a sample of human labels and measure agreement — if it doesn't track humans, the rubric or the judge model is wrong. I give it an explicit rubric, ask for pairwise comparisons (candidate vs production) rather than absolute scores to reduce drift, randomise option order to kill position bias, and use a stronger model as judge than the one under test. I also pair it with cheap deterministic metrics (valid-JSON rate, exact-match on the label) so not everything rides on the judge. And I never let an uncalibrated judge be the sole gate.

How to say it out loud say it

“An LLM judge is a model, so I evaluate it: calibrate against human labels, use a rubric and pairwise head-to-head, randomise order, judge with a stronger model, and back it with deterministic checks. An uncalibrated judge is just a confident rubber stamp.”
