Tokens, context & cost IV

A model doesn't read words — it reads tokens, the little chunks text gets chopped into. Tokens are the unit you're billed in, the unit your context window is measured in, and the reason models sometimes can't spell. Get tokens and a surprising amount of GenAI stops being mysterious.

01  Tokens are the LEGO bricks of text

Before a model sees your text, a tokenizer splits it into tokens — usually sub-word pieces. Common words are a single token; rarer words break into several. A rough rule of thumb for English: one token ≈ 4 characters ≈ ¾ of a word.

LEGO, not whole toys analogy

Imagine you can only build with a fixed box of LEGO bricks. Common shapes (“ the”, “ing”, “tion”) come as single bricks. A weird custom shape has to be built from many small bricks. The model thinks in these bricks — so a rare word literally costs more pieces than a common one.

This toy uses a small illustrative vocabulary, so the exact splits aren't byte-identical to GPT or Claude — but the behaviour (whitespace, casing, rare-word fragmenting) is real.

03  Why sub-words? (BPE in one idea)

Most tokenizers are trained with Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). The idea is delightfully simple: start from single characters, then repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, thousands of times. Frequent strings (“ the”, “ing”) end up as single tokens; rare strings never get merged, so they stay in small pieces.

That gives the best of both worlds: a fixed vocabulary (typically ~50k–200k tokens) that can still represent any text — worst case, by falling back to bytes. No “unknown word” problem, and common text stays compact.

04  The quirks that trip models up

  • Whitespace is attached. “ cat” (with a leading space) and “cat” are different tokens. This is why prompt formatting can subtly change behaviour.
  • Casing matters. “Cat”, “cat”, and “CAT” can be different tokens — try it above.
  • Counting letters is hard. Ask “how many r's in strawberry?” and models often miss, because they see a couple of tokens, not the individual letters.
  • Non-English and code cost more. Languages and scripts underrepresented in training fragment into many tokens — so the same sentence can cost 2–3× more tokens in some languages.
The “strawberry” trap gotcha

If someone asks why an LLM miscounts letters or struggles with anagrams, the answer is tokenization: the model never sees individual characters, only token chunks. It's not “bad at counting” in general — it just can't see inside its own bricks.

05  The context window is a desk

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can consider at once — and your prompt and the model's reply both have to fit on it. Think of a desk: instructions, chat history, retrieved documents, and the answer all share the same surface. Fill it with junk and there's less room for what matters (and you pay for every token on it). Windows of 1M+ tokens are common in 2026, but “bigger desk” isn't free — see Context engineering.

06  Tokens → cost & latency

Two practical consequences fall straight out of “everything is tokens”:

Cost
You pay per token, and output tokens usually cost several times more than input. Shorter prompts and answers = cheaper.
Latency
The model emits tokens one at a time, so a longer answer literally takes longer. Time-to-first-token and tokens-per-second are the metrics that matter (see Inference & serving).
How to say it out loud say it

“Models read sub-word tokens produced by a BPE-style tokenizer — common strings are one token, rare ones split into several. That's the unit for both billing and the context window, and it's why models miscount letters: they see tokens, not characters. Output tokens cost more and add latency, so prompt and response length directly drive cost and speed.”

07  Self-check

Why does an LLM often fail to count the letters in a word like “strawberry”?
Tokenization hides characters inside multi-letter tokens. The model reasons over tokens, so character-level questions are genuinely hard for it — a classic tokenization “gotcha.”
“What is tokenization, and why should I care about it as an engineer?”Core
Model answer

It's splitting text into sub-word tokens (usually via BPE) — the unit the model actually processes. You care because it determines cost (per-token billing, output dearer than input), how much fits in the context window, multilingual cost differences, and odd failure modes like miscounting letters.

How to say it out loud say it

“Tokenization chops text into sub-word units the model reads. It drives cost, context limits, multilingual overhead, and quirks like letter-counting — so it's worth understanding before you optimize prompts or budgets.”

“Roughly how many tokens is 1,000 words of English, and why does it matter?”Warm-up
Model answer

About 1,300–1,400 tokens (≈ ¾ word per token). It matters for fitting content in the context window and estimating cost — e.g., stuffing a 100-page doc into a prompt is hundreds of thousands of tokens and real money.

How to say it out loud say it

“Ballpark ¾ of a word per token, so ~1,000 words is ~1,300 tokens. I use that to sanity-check whether content fits the window and what a call will cost.”
