Dynamic Programming

Dynamic programming (DP) sounds scary, but it's one small idea: break a big problem into smaller versions of itself, solve each tiny piece once, and reuse those answers instead of computing them again. It turns slow, repetitive recursion into a quick fill-in-the-blanks. Let's watch a table fill up, learn to spot the DP families, then solve five classics.

00  The idea

Suppose answering "step 5" needs the answers to "step 4" and "step 3", and those need smaller steps still. A naive recursion recomputes "step 2" over and over. DP says: write each small answer down once, then just look it up. You build from the smallest pieces up to the one you want.

You're looking at a DP problem when you spot two tells together:

Overlapping subproblems
The same smaller question comes up again and again (so caching its answer pays off).
Optimal substructure
The best answer to the whole is built from the best answers to its pieces. ("Best route to here = best route to the step below + one move.")
Climbing stairs, one note at a time analogy

You're counting the ways to climb a staircase, taking 1 or 2 steps at a time. Rather than re-counting from the bottom for every question, you scribble the count for each step on a sticky note. Each new step's count is just the sum of the two notes below it. By the time you reach the top, every answer was figured out exactly once — that's the whole trick of DP.

How to build a DP solution

Most DP solutions follow the same recipe:

Define the state
Decide what dp[i] means in one sentence ("fewest coins to make amount i").
Write the recurrence
Express dp[i] using smaller entries ("dp[i] = dp[i-1] + dp[i-2]").
Set the base cases
The smallest answers you know outright (dp[0], dp[1]).
Pick an order
Fill from small to large so every piece you need is already written down.
Top-down vs bottom-up

Two flavours, same answers. Top-down writes the natural recursion and adds a cache (memoization). Bottom-up fills a table from the base cases upward (what we do here). When only the last few entries matter, bottom-up often shrinks to a couple of variables — O(1) space.

01  The DP families

Senior interviews don't ask "do you know DP?" — the interviewer watches whether you recognize which family a problem belongs to, because the family dictates the state shape: what dp[…] means and how many dimensions it needs. Name the family out loud and the recurrence usually writes itself.

Memoization vs tabulation

Two ways to cash in the same insight. Top-down (memoization) is recursion plus a cache: write exactly the recurrence you said out loud, and let the cache absorb the repeats. Bottom-up (tabulation) fills a table smallest-first: no recursion limit to hit, and often less memory, since you can usually keep just the last row or two. Same answers either way — pick whichever you can write fastest under pressure.

02  Climbing Stairs

DP · 1-D Easy ~10 min O(n) time · O(1) space

You're climbing a staircase of n steps. Each move you take either 1 step or 2. How many distinct ways can you reach the top? For n = 5 the answer is 8.

Where did your last step come from? analogy

To be standing on step n, your final move was either a single step (so you were on step n−1) or a double step (so you were on step n−2). Every way of reaching the top therefore ends one of two ways — so the ways to reach n is simply the ways to reach n−1 plus the ways to reach n−2. That's the Fibonacci sequence wearing a staircase costume.

How to solve it

Let ways(k) be the number of ways to reach step k. Then ways(k) = ways(k−1) + ways(k−2), with base cases ways(1) = 1 and ways(2) = 2. We never need more than the last two answers, so instead of a whole table we keep two rolling variables and slide them upward.

Roll two variables, not a whole array

When each answer only looks back a fixed number of steps, you can throw the table away and keep just those few values. That's how O(n) space becomes O(1).

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n)One pass from step 3 up to step n.
SpaceO(1)Just two rolling numbers — the previous two counts.

New to this notation? See the Big-O cheat-sheet.

Why is ways(n) = ways(n−1) + ways(n−2)?

Right. Split all the ways by what the final move was. A 1-step means you came from n−1; a 2-step means you came from n−2. Add those two disjoint groups and you've counted every way exactly once.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Keep two variables, a and b, for the ways to reach the previous two steps. Each loop, slide them forward: a, b = b, a + b. After looping up to n, b holds the answer.

03  House Robber

DP · 1-D Medium ~15 min O(n) time · O(1) space

A row of houses each hold some money. You can't rob two adjacent houses (their alarms are linked). What's the most money you can grab? For [2, 7, 9, 3, 1] it's 12 (rob houses 2, 9, and 1).

At each house, skip or rob analogy

Walk down the street house by house, asking one question at each door: am I better off skipping this house (keeping whatever total I had at the previous house), or robbing it (its cash plus my best total from two houses back, since the one next door is off-limits)? Take the bigger of the two and move on. You never need the whole street's history — just those two running totals.

How to solve it

Let best(i) be the most money using houses 0..i. At house i you either skip it (so the best is unchanged, best(i−1)) or rob it (its money plus best(i−2), since you must skip the neighbour). So:

best(i) = max(best(i−1), money[i] + best(i−2))

As with stairs, each answer only looks two houses back, so two rolling variables — call them prev and cur — are all we need.

Greedy "rob every other house" is wrong

Don't just grab alternating houses. For [2, 1, 1, 2] the alternating grab gives 3, but robbing the two 2s (the ends) gives 4. You must compare skip vs rob at every door — that comparison is the DP.

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n)One walk down the row of houses.
SpaceO(1)Two rolling totals — the best up to the last two houses.

Why does robbing house i add best(i−2) and not best(i−1)?

Exactly. The alarms link neighbours, so robbing house i rules out house i−1. The most recent total you're allowed to build on is therefore best(i−2).
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Keep prev = best total two houses back and cur = best total at the last house. For each house's money n: prev, cur = cur, max(cur, prev + n). Return cur at the end.

04  Coin Change

DP · Unbounded Medium ~25 min O(amount·coins) time · O(amount) space

Given coin values and a target amount, find the fewest coins that add up to it (you have unlimited coins of each value), or −1 if it can't be made. For coins [1, 2, 5] and amount 11 the answer is 3 (5 + 5 + 1).

Build up your change, amount by amount analogy

Work out the best change for every amount from 0 up to your target, jotting each in a row of boxes. To fill the box for amount A, try each coin c: lay it down, then look up the already-solved best for A − c and add one coin. The smallest result across all coins is the answer for A. Because you always reuse smaller boxes you've already filled, you never redo work.

How to solve it

Let dp[a] be the fewest coins to make amount a. Start every entry at a "too big" sentinel (impossible), except dp[0] = 0. Then fill upward:

  1. For each amount a from 1 to the target…
  2. …try every coin c that fits (c <= a).
  3. If using that coin helps, set dp[a] = min(dp[a], dp[a − c] + 1) — one coin on top of the best way to make the leftover.

At the end, dp[amount] is the answer — unless it's still the sentinel, meaning no combination works, so return −1. Closing the loop: each dp[a − c] is a smaller box you already filled, so no amount is ever re-solved.

Why greedy "biggest coin first" can fail

Grabbing the largest coin each time works for everyday money but not all coin sets. For coins [1, 3, 4] and amount 6, greedy takes 4 + 1 + 1 (3 coins); the true best is 3 + 3 (2 coins). DP checks every coin, so it can't be fooled.

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(amount·coins)For each of the amount boxes we try every coin.
SpaceO(amount)One table entry per amount from 0 to the target.

Why fill the table from amount 0 upward rather than starting at the target?

Right. dp[a] depends on smaller amounts dp[a − c]. Filling from 0 upward guarantees those are already final, so each box is computed once from finished pieces — the heart of bottom-up DP.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Make dp of length amount + 1, all set to a big sentinel like amount + 1, but dp[0] = 0. Double loop: for each amount a, for each coin c <= a, do dp[a] = min(dp[a], dp[a - c] + 1). Return dp[amount] if it changed, else -1.

05  Longest Increasing Subsequence

DP · Subsequence Medium ~25 min O(n²) time · O(n) space

Find the length of the longest subsequence (skips allowed, order kept) that strictly increases. For [10, 9, 2, 5, 3, 7, 101, 18] the answer is 4 — for example 2, 5, 7, 101 or 2, 3, 7, 18.

Whose chain can I extend? analogy

Picture the numbers as people stacking blocks in a line. Each new number turns to every smaller number before it and asks: "how long a chain could you end? I'll extend the best one by stacking myself on top." Its own chain length is the best answer it heard, plus one for itself. Nobody re-counts a chain — everyone remembers their own best, which is exactly the DP table.

How to solve it

Start dp as an array of 1s — every element is a chain of length one by itself. Then a double loop: for each i, scan every j < i, and whenever nums[j] < nums[i], consider extending that chain: dp[i] = max(dp[i], dp[j] + 1). The answer is max(dp).

The state ends AT i — so the answer is max(dp), not dp[-1]

This is the key interview subtlety. dp[i] is the best chain ending exactly at i, because "ending at i" is what makes the recurrence checkable — you only need to know whether nums[i] can sit on top. But the longest chain might end anywhere in the array, so you must take the max over all entries. Returning dp[-1] is the classic wrong answer.

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n²)Each index asks every earlier index whether it can extend it.
SpaceO(n)One dp entry per index.
Senior upgrade: O(n log n) with patience sorting go deeper

The classic follow-up. Keep a tails list where tails[k] is the smallest possible tail of an increasing chain of length k + 1. For each number, binary-search the spot it would replace; if it's beyond the end, it extends the longest chain. The trick is named patience sorting (after the card game), and the answer is just len(tails):

tails = []
for x in nums:
    i = bisect.bisect_left(tails, x)   # first tail that is not smaller than x
    if i == len(tails): tails.append(x)
    else: tails[i] = x                 # smaller tail, same chain length
return len(tails)

Name it in the interview, code the O(n²) version unless asked — that's usually the expected play.

Why is the answer max(dp) rather than dp[-1]?

Right. In [10, 9, 2, 5, 3, 7] the best chain ends at the 7, but add a final 1 to the array and dp[-1] would be 1 while the real answer is still 3. The state is "ending at i", so the answer is the best over every possible ending spot.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Start with dp = [1] * len(nums). Outer loop i, inner loop j over range(i); when nums[j] < nums[i], try dp[i] = max(dp[i], dp[j] + 1). Return max(dp).

06  Partition Equal Subset Sum

DP · Knapsack Medium ~25 min O(n·S) time · O(S) space

Can the list be split into two halves with equal sums? For [1, 5, 11, 5] the answer is True — 11 on one side, 1 + 5 + 5 on the other. Reframe it in one line and the problem collapses: "is there a subset summing to total ÷ 2?" — a knapsack.

Splitting the Halloween haul analogy

Two kids tip out their Halloween candy and demand an exactly even split. Here's the insight that saves the evening: you only need to fill one bag up to half the total — whatever's left over is the other bag, automatically. So the question isn't "how do I make two equal piles?" but "can I pick candies that hit exactly half?"

How to solve it

First gate: if the total is odd, an even split is impossible — return False before any DP. Otherwise hunt for a subset hitting target = total // 2. The friendliest version is set-based: start reachable = {0}, and for each number fold in every new sum: reachable |= {s + num} (capped at the target).

The array version is the same idea: a boolean dp[0..target] where dp[s] means "sum s is buildable", and for each number you set dp[s] = dp[s] or dp[s - num] — iterating s from the target down to num.

Right-to-left, or you spend each number twice

Why the backwards loop? Each number may be used once. Going left-to-right, the sums you just turned on with this number are still ahead of you — so the same number feeds its own update and gets counted twice (e.g. a single 5 would make 10 look reachable). This is the classic 0/1-knapsack bug, and spotting it is the senior moment of this problem. Going right-to-left, every dp[s - num] you read is from before this number was offered.

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n·S)For each of the n numbers, touch up to S sums (S = total ÷ 2).
SpaceO(S)One boolean (or set slot) per sum from 0 to the target.
"Pseudo-polynomial"

O(n·S) is called pseudo-polynomial: in plain words, the cost grows with the value of the numbers (the target sum S), not just with how many numbers there are. Small list, huge values — still slow.

With the 1-D boolean array, why iterate the sums right-to-left for each number?

Exactly. Each number is one-shot. Left-to-right, dp[s - num] might already reflect this number, so it gets spent twice. Right-to-left, every cell you read still holds last round's truth — one number, one use. (Fun contrast: Coin Change has unlimited coins, so left-to-right reuse is exactly what you want there.)
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Compute the total; if it's odd, return False. Set target = total // 2. Keep a set reachable = {0}; for each number, union in {s + num for s in reachable if s + num <= target}. Return whether target ended up in the set.

07  Unique Paths

2-D DP Medium ~20 min O(m·n) time · O(m·n) space
Say it out loud

“dp[r][c] = ways to reach this cell = ways from above + ways from left. First row and first column are all 1s.” Name the 2-D grid family.

A robot on an m × n grid starts at the top-left and may only move right or down. How many unique paths reach the bottom-right? For a 3 × 3 grid the answer is 6.

City blocks, only south or east analogy

You're walking a grid of city blocks and can only go south or east. Every intersection's path count is “how I got here from the north” plus “how I got here from the west.” Corners on the top row and left column have only one way along the edge.

How to solve it

  1. Make a table dp of size m × n.
  2. Set the entire first row and first column to 1.
  3. For every other cell: dp[r][c] = dp[r-1][c] + dp[r][c-1].
  4. Return dp[m-1][n-1].

You can compress to one row (O(n) space) since each cell only needs the previous row — nice follow-up.

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(m·n)Fill each cell once.
SpaceO(m·n) → O(n)Full table, or one rolling row.

Why is every cell on the first row equal to 1?

Right. No “above” neighbor exists, so there's exactly one way: the straight rightward walk from the start.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

dp = [[1]*n for _ in range(m)] then nested loops from (1,1) setting dp[r][c] = dp[r-1][c] + dp[r][c-1]. Return the bottom-right.

Time O(m · n); space O(m · n) for the table (or O(n) with a rolling row).

08  Maximum Subarray

DP · 1-D · Kadane Medium ~15 min O(n) time · O(1) space

Given an integer array nums, find the contiguous subarray (at least one number) with the largest sum, and return that sum. For [-2, 1, -3, 4, -1, 2, 1, -5, 4] the answer is 6 from the window [4, -1, 2, 1].

A streak of daily P&L analogy

Each day is a profit or loss. You're hunting the best contiguous stretch of days. At each new day ask: am I better off continuing the streak I already have (add today to it), or starting fresh with today alone? Keep a second notepad of the best streak you've ever seen. That's Kadane's algorithm in one sentence.

How to solve it

Brute force tries every i..j window — O(n²). Kadane keeps two running values:

  1. Let cur be the best sum of a subarray ending at the current index.
  2. At value n: cur = max(n, cur + n) — start fresh or extend.
  3. Let best be the best cur seen anywhere: best = max(best, cur).
  4. Initialize both to the first element (or to −∞ / first pass carefully) so a single-element array works.

This is classic 1-D DP: the state is "best ending here," and the transition only needs the previous state — hence O(1) space.

MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n)One left-to-right pass over the array.
SpaceO(1)Only two scalars: current ending-sum and global best.
Don't reset to 0 if the array can be all-negative

If you clamp cur with max(0, …), an all-negative array would wrongly report 0. The problem requires at least one element — so compare against n itself (restart), not against 0.

What does cur = max(n, cur + n) decide at each step?

Right. If the previous ending-sum is a drag (negative or just worse than starting over), restart at n. Otherwise extend. best remembers the peak of those choices.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Seed best = cur = nums[0]. Then for each later n: cur = max(n, cur + n); best = max(best, cur). Return best.

Line-by-line walkthrough + flow walkthrough

Kadane: at each index, best ending-here = max(start fresh, extend). Track a global best. O(n) time, O(1) space — the interview default over O(n²) windows.

flowchart TD
  start["Seed best = cur = nums 0"] --> loop[For each later n]
  loop --> choose["cur = max n, cur + n"]
  choose --> track["best = max best, cur"]
  track --> more{More numbers?}
  more -->|yes| loop
  more -->|no| ret[Return best]
  ret --> done[Done]
  1. def max_sub_array(nums):Return the largest sum of any contiguous subarray.
  2. best = cur = nums[0]Seed both trackers with the first element (handles length-1 and all-negative).
  3. for n in nums[1:]:Walk every remaining value left to right.
  4. cur = max(n, cur + n)Restart at n, or extend the previous ending-sum — whichever is larger.
  5. best = max(best, cur)Remember the best ending-sum seen anywhere so far.
  6. return bestAfter the last index, best is the answer.

Time O(n) one pass; space O(1) two scalars.
