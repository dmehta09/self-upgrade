The pattern map

Strong candidates don't memorise hundreds of problems. They learn to recognise which of about a dozen patterns a question is secretly asking for. This page is the map: read the tells in a problem, then jump straight to the right tool. No code here — just how to choose.

00  How to spot a pattern

A coding problem rarely says "use two pointers". Instead it leaves clues — the shape of the input, the words it uses, the thing it asks for. The skill is matching those tells (the little symptoms) to the right tool (the pattern). Once you can do that, the code almost writes itself.

A doctor reading symptoms analogy

A good doctor doesn't memorise every illness. A patient walks in, and the doctor listens for symptoms — a sore throat, a fever, a rash. Those symptoms point to a diagnosis, and the diagnosis decides the treatment. Coding problems work the same way: "the array is sorted" or "the longest run of…" are symptoms. Learn to hear them, and the right pattern names itself.

The middle two boxes are the whole game. Get the tells and the pattern right, and the code is the easy part.

So as you read a problem, keep a short checklist in your head: What shape is the input — sorted, a grid, a chain, a tree? What is it really asking for — a pair, a longest run, every arrangement, a shortest path? What would the slow brute force look like, and which pattern makes it fast? The table below turns that checklist into a lookup.

And because hearing the tells is a reflex, not knowledge — train it. The trainer below deals you real problem blurbs; name the pattern before the bar runs out. It deliberately includes the look-alikes that fool people: window vs two pointers, greedy vs DP, trie vs hash map.

01  The map

Find the row whose left cell matches the tell you spotted, then click the tool in the middle to open that pattern. The right cell says why that tool fits — the one-line reason worth remembering.

If you see…Reach for…Why
A sorted array · a pair/triple summing to a target · a palindrome · "work from both ends" Two Pointers Two markers walking inward turn a slow "check every pair" into a single pass.
The best / longest contiguous run — a subarray or substring with some property Sliding Window Grow and shrink one window instead of re-scanning every range from scratch.
You need to remember seen items or counts, with instant lookup Arrays & Hashing A hash map answers "have I seen this?" in O(1), collapsing nested scans to one pass.
You search a sorted thing, or the words "minimise the maximum" Binary Search Each guess throws away half the possibilities, so the work grows like O(log n).
Matching pairs · "next greater element" · an undo / most-recent-first order Stack A last-in-first-out pile naturally pairs openers with closers and tracks what's pending.
Reverse or reorder a chain · detect a loop in it Linked List Re-pointing next-links (sometimes with fast & slow runners) edits the chain in place.
You must walk a tree or hierarchy — by level or all the way down Trees · BFS & DFS Breadth-first visits level by level; depth-first dives down a branch then backs up.
Keep the K best · repeatedly grab the current min or max Heap / Top-K A heap hands you the smallest (or largest) in O(log n) without fully sorting.
Generate all subsets, permutations, or combinations Backtracking Try a choice, recurse, then undo it — exploring the whole tree of possibilities.
A grid or network: reachable? connected? any cycle? what order? Graphs BFS/DFS over nodes and edges answers connectivity, ordering and cycle questions.
Count the ways / find a min cost, with overlapping subproblems Dynamic Programming Solve each smaller piece once, store it, and reuse it instead of recomputing.
Overlapping ranges · scheduling start/end times Intervals Sort by start, then sweep once, merging or counting overlaps as you go.
The strongest tell is the word "sorted"

If the input is sorted — or you're allowed to sort it — two of the fastest patterns light up at once: Two Pointers and Binary Search. Always ask "is this sorted?" first.

02  Not on the map (yet)

This guide is deliberately small. A handful of more specialised patterns are left off on purpose — they show up less often at the mid-level, and each needs its own deep dive to do justice.

Skipped on purpose

We leave out Tries (prefix trees for word lookups), Union-Find (a fast "are these two connected?" structure), Greedy (always grab the locally best choice), and Bit manipulation (tricks with the 1s and 0s of a number). They're worth learning later — but the dozen patterns above already cover the large majority of mid-level interview questions, so master these first.

Ready to start? The very next page, Arrays & Hashing, is the most common tool of all — the hash map that remembers what you've seen. Or revisit the Foundations if any structure above felt unfamiliar.
