Patterns · greedy

Greedy

Take the best choice available right now, commit to it, and never look back — no undo, no regrets. When that's safe it gives stunningly simple one-pass solutions; the real skill (and what interviews actually test) is knowing when it's safe. Let's build that instinct, then solve three classics with it.

00  The idea

Most patterns in this guide are about how to move through data. Greedy is different: it's a decision rule. At every step, pick whatever looks best at this exact moment, lock it in, and move on. No backtracking, no second-guessing, no table of remembered sub-answers. When it works, the code is a single loop. When it doesn't, the answer it gives is confidently, silently wrong.

The kid in the candy shop analogy

A kid walks into a candy shop with limited pocket money and one rule: grab the best candy in front of you, never look back. Sometimes that strategy is provably perfect — there genuinely was no better way to spend the money. And sometimes it's exactly how you get robbed: the shiny lollipop by the door eats the budget that two chocolate bars at the back would have spent better. Greedy is that kid. Our job is to figure out which kind of shop we're standing in.

When is "no regrets" actually safe?

Greedy is safe only when the problem has the greedy-choice property: taking the locally best option can never hurt you later. In plain words — whatever clever plan the perfect answer follows, you could rewrite ("exchange") its first move for the greedy move and the plan would be no worse. If any optimal answer can be massaged to start with the greedy choice without getting worse, then making the greedy choice and repeating is optimal all the way down. That rewriting trick is called an exchange argument, and it's the one-line proof interviewers want to hear: "swapping the greedy choice into an optimal solution never makes it worse."

When greedy fails — the 60-second test

Coins {1, 3, 4}, amount 6. Greedy grabs the biggest coin first: 4 + 1 + 1 — three coins. But the best answer is 3 + 3 — two coins. The locally best move (take the 4) poisoned the future. Before trusting greedy in an interview, spend 60 seconds hunting for a counterexample like this one. If you find even one, greedy is dead and the problem is really Dynamic Programming. If you hunt hard and come up empty, that's strong evidence an exchange argument exists.

Tells that a problem is greedy

"Best / most / fewest" in one pass
The question asks for a maximum, minimum, or "can you…?", and a single sweep carrying one or two summary variables feels like it could plausibly answer it.
Sorting unlocks a sweep
After sorting by the right key, one left-to-right pass is obviously right — the order itself does most of the thinking.
Choices never need undoing
You can't construct a situation where an earlier pick has to be taken back. No "oops, give me that coin back".
The counterexample hunt comes up empty
You spent your 60 seconds genuinely trying to break it and couldn't — and you can sketch the exchange argument in one sentence.

Which of these problems is safe to solve greedily?

Right. The interval version has a clean exchange argument: any optimal answer can be rewritten to keep the earliest-ending interval without getting worse (we prove it in section 03). The other two have future-dependence: with coins {1,3,4} the greedy "biggest coin" pick ruins later choices, and in longest increasing subsequence whether a number helps depends on what comes after it. Both belong to Dynamic Programming.

01  Jump Game

Greedy Medium ~15 min O(n) time · O(1) space

Each slot in the list holds the maximum distance you may jump forward from it (you can always jump less). Starting at index 0, can you reach the last index? Example: [2,3,1,1,4] → True, but [3,2,1,0,4] → False.

Stepping stones in a river analogy

You're crossing a river on stepping stones, and each stone has a number painted on it: the farthest you can leap from that stone. You don't need to plan the exact route stone-by-stone. You only ever need to remember one thing: the farthest stone you could possibly reach so far. If you're standing within that horizon, you're fine; if the next stone is past it, no sequence of leaps could ever have gotten you there.

How to solve it

The brute-force way treats every jump as an edge and explores them all with BFS or DP over the jump edges — O(n²) in the worst case. The greedy insight collapses all of it into one number: reach, the farthest index any sequence of jumps could have gotten you to so far. One pass:

  1. Walk i from left to right. If i > reach, you're standing on a stone you could never have reached — return False.
  2. Otherwise update the horizon: reach = max(reach, i + nums[i]).
  3. The moment reach >= last index, stop early and return True.
MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n)One pass; each index is visited once. (The every-pair baseline is O(n²).)
SpaceO(1)The entire "memory" of the algorithm is the single number reach.

New to this notation? See the Big-O cheat-sheet.

Easy trap: overwriting instead of max

Writing reach = i + nums[i] (no max) is the classic bug: a small jump later would shrink your horizon, as if leaping a short distance made you forget you could have leapt far. The horizon must only ever grow — always take the max.

Why is the greedy "just track the farthest reach" safe here?

Exactly. Reach measures pure possibility, and more possibility can't hurt: whatever jumps an optimal plan makes from a smaller reach, you can still make (or exchange for better ones) with a larger reach. The list isn't sorted, and there are usually many paths — neither matters, because one number summarises them all.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

You don't need to track which jumps were taken — summarize the whole prefix with one number: the farthest index reachable so far. At each step ask only two questions: "am I within that number?" and "does standing here push it further?"

02  Gas Station

Greedy Medium ~20 min O(n) time · O(1) space

There's a circular route of gas stations: gas[i] is the fuel you gain at station i, and cost[i] is the fuel it takes to drive to the next station. Starting with an empty tank, return the index of the starting station from which you can complete the full loop — or -1 if none exists. (If total gas ≥ total cost, exactly one answer exists.) Example: gas=[1,2,3,4,5], cost=[3,4,5,1,2] → 3.

The road trip that sputters out analogy

Picture a road trip where some legs gain you fuel (cheap stations, generous friends) and some drain it. You set off, and somewhere down the road the engine sputters to a stop. The genius move is what you don't do: you don't retry from one town later. Every station between your start and the breakdown was reached with fuel still in the tank — so anyone starting there instead would arrive at the breakdown with even less. No station between your start and here could have done better — so restart from the NEXT station after the breakdown.

How to solve it

The obvious baseline tries every starting station and simulates the whole loop from each — O(n²). The greedy version makes one pass carrying two numbers:

  1. total — the net fuel over the whole loop. If it ends negative, no start can ever work; if it's ≥ 0, exactly one start works.
  2. tank — the fuel of the current attempt. Add each station's net gain to it.
  3. When tank < 0, the attempt is dead — and so is every station between the old start and here (the analogy above). Set start = i + 1 and reset tank = 0.
  4. At the end, return start if total >= 0, else -1. No need to simulate the wrap-around — feasibility of the whole loop is exactly what total checked.
MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n)One pass; each candidate start is abandoned at most once, never re-tried.
SpaceO(1)Three variables: total, tank, start.

We started at station s and the tank went negative at station i. Why can we skip ALL of stations s..i as potential starts, not just s?

That's the key insight. Driving from s, the tank was ≥ 0 at every station up to i (we'd have stopped earlier otherwise). A car starting fresh at some middle station m begins with tank 0, while s's car passed m carrying tank ≥ 0 — so from m onward the fresh car is never ahead of s's car, and it runs dry at i too (or sooner). The in-between stations need not be individually negative; they just inherit the doomed stretch.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Work with the net gain gas[i] - cost[i] per station. Keep two running sums: one that never resets (does the whole loop even have enough fuel?) and one that resets to 0 whenever it dips negative (the current attempt). Every time the second one dips, the next station becomes your new candidate start.

03  Non-overlapping Intervals

Greedy Medium ~20 min O(n log n) time · O(1) space

Given a list of intervals, remove the minimum number of them so the rest never overlap. Example: [[1,2],[2,3],[3,4],[1,3]] → 1 (remove [1,3]). Note that touching endpoints — [1,2] and [2,3] — do not count as overlapping.

The one-room booking calendar analogy

You manage a single meeting room and everyone wants it. To fit the most meetings, always keep the meeting that ENDS earliest — it frees the room soonest, leaving maximum space for whatever comes next. A long meeting that ends late might have been "first come", but keeping it can squeeze out two short ones. And notice the flip: removing the fewest intervals is the same as keeping the most — so the booking trick solves our problem directly.

How to solve it

Trying all subsets is hopeless; the greedy sweep is four lines once you sort by the right key:

  1. Sort by END time — this is the whole trick.
  2. Sweep left to right keeping a prev_end (the end of the last interval you kept).
  3. If the current interval's start >= prev_end, it fits — keep it and advance prev_end to its end.
  4. Otherwise it overlaps the one you kept — count a removal and move on. (Never replace the kept interval with a later-ending one: the early finisher is exactly why we kept it.)
MeasureCostWhy
TimeO(n log n)The sort dominates; the sweep itself is a single O(n) pass.
SpaceO(1)Beyond sorting in place: just prev_end and a removal counter.
Old friend, new proof

This is the Intervals pattern's sort-and-sweep with a greedy proof on top — sort by END, not start. The earliest finisher is the exchange-argument winner: it's the interval any optimal answer can be rewritten to keep.

Why sort by end time and not by start time?

Right. This is the exchange argument from section 00 in action: take any optimal set of kept intervals and swap its first one for the earliest-ending interval overall — the swap can never create a new overlap (it ends no later), so the result is still optimal. Sorting by start genuinely fails: with [1,10], [2,3], [4,5], the early starter hogs the room and you'd keep one meeting instead of two.
Stuck? Reveal a hint hint

Flip the question: instead of "remove the fewest", ask "keep the most non-overlapping". Sort by each interval's second value (the end), then sweep with one variable — the end of the last interval you kept. Each interval either fits after it (keep) or doesn't (count a removal). Remember: start == prev_end is a fit, not an overlap.
