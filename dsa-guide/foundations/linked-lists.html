Linked lists, stacks & queues

Arrays love their tidy row, but sometimes you want to add and remove things constantly without shuffling everyone. Enter three flexible helpers: the linked list — a chain you follow link by link; the stack — a pile where the last thing in is the first out; and the queue — a line where the first to arrive is the first served.

01  Linked lists

A linked list is a chain of little boxes called nodes. Each node holds a value plus an arrow — next — pointing to the box after it. The chain ends when an arrow points at nothing (None).

A treasure hunt of clues analogy

Each node is a clue that tells you where the next clue is hidden. To reach the fifth clue you must follow the first four in order — there's no map that lets you teleport to clue five. That "follow the arrows one by one" rule is the whole personality of a linked list.

The trade-off vs. an array

A linked list flips the array's strengths and weaknesses. There's no instant jump — but there's also no shuffling when you add or remove at the ends.

Reach the i-th node
Slow — O(n). You must walk from the head, following one arrow at a time.
Insert at the front
Instant — O(1). Make a new node and point its arrow at the old head. Nobody shuffles.
Remove the front
Instant — O(1). Just move the head to the second node.
The trick for adding at the front

Inserting at index 0 of an array shuffles everyone (O(n)). A linked list just re-aims one arrow — the new node points at the old head, and the head becomes the new node. One pointer change, no matter how long the chain. That's the linked list's superpower.

OperationCostWhy
Reach the i-th nodeO(n)No teleport — follow arrows from the head.
Insert / remove at the frontO(1)Re-aim one arrow; nobody shuffles.

02  Stacks

A stack is a pile where you only ever touch the top. The last thing you put in is the first thing you take out — computer scientists call this LIFO (last-in, first-out).

A stack of plates analogy

Think of clean plates piled on a counter. You add a plate on top, and you take a plate off the top — never from the middle. The last plate you set down is the first one you grab. That's a stack: add and remove only at one end.

How to use it in Python

You don't need a special type — a plain list is a perfect stack. append puts a value on top; pop takes the top one off. Both are instant O(1) because they touch only the end — no shuffling.

stack.append(x)
Push — drop x on top of the pile.
stack.pop()
Pop — remove and return the top item (the most recent).
stack[-1]
Peek — look at the top without removing it.
Where stacks shine

Stacks are the natural fit for matching things (every open bracket waits for its close) and for undo (the last action you did is the first to reverse). We lean on this heavily on the Stack pattern page.

03  Queues

A queue is the opposite manners: the first thing in is the first thing out — FIFO (first-in, first-out). You add at the back and remove from the front, exactly like a fair line.

A line at the shop analogy

People join a checkout line at the back and are served from the front, in the order they arrived. No cutting. The person who waited longest goes next. That fairness — first come, first served — is exactly what a queue gives your data.

Use collections.deque, not a list

You could use a list and call list.pop(0) to take the front — but that shuffles every remaining element left, which is slow O(n). Python's deque ("deck", a double-ended queue) is built for this: append at the back and popleft at the front are both instant O(1).

q.append(x)
Enqueue — join the back of the line.
q.popleft()
Dequeue — serve and remove the front (the longest-waiting).
deque()
Create an empty queue; from collections import deque first.
The trap: list.pop(0) for a queue

It works, but every front-removal slides the whole list left — turning a loop into O(n²). Always reach for deque.popleft() when you need a queue. We use exactly this for breadth-first search on the next page.

Right. A stack adds and removes at the same end, so a plain list's append/pop are both O(1). A queue adds at one end and removes at the other; a list's pop(0) would shuffle everyone, so use deque.popleft() to keep both ends O(1).
