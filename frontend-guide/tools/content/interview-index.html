How frontend interviews work

A 2026 frontend loop isn’t one “leetcode” gauntlet — it’s a handful of distinct rounds, each grading a different muscle. Know which muscle is on the table and you stop preparing blind.

00 The rounds you’ll face

Most loops are 4–6 rounds. Each has a different rubric — prep for the rubric, not for “frontend” in the abstract.

Senior loops weight system design and judgement heavily; junior loops weight coding and framework recall. Figure out the mix from the recruiter, then drill the rounds that carry the most points.

Think of it like… analogy

A driving test, not a trivia night. They don’t just ask if you know the rules — they put you behind the wheel (coding), check that you can explain a manoeuvre (framework knowledge), and see whether you’d plan a long road trip sensibly (system design). Each is scored on its own card.

01 The answer method: define → trade-off → gotcha

Strong answers have a shape. Lead with a crisp definition so the interviewer knows you actually know it, then show seniority by naming the trade-off and the trap. This works for almost every knowledge question.

1

Define it crisply

One or two sentences that nail the concept in plain terms. “A re-render is React calling your component function again…” No hedging, no padding.

2

Name the trade-off

Show you know when and why, not just what. “You reach for memo when a subtree is expensive and its inputs are stable — otherwise it’s noise.”

3

Drop the gotcha

One sharp caveat marks the senior. “…but a fresh inline object as a prop silently defeats memo.” Then stop talking.

Common trap gotcha

Don’t over-talk. Once you’ve hit define → trade-off → gotcha, stop and let them follow up. Rambling past the answer reads as uncertainty and buries the signal you just gave.

02 Quick warm-up

One to calibrate before you dive into the bank.

An interviewer asks “is re-rendering bad?” Using the answer method, what’s the strongest opener?

Right. Open with a crisp definition that reframes the question, then layer the trade-off (optimize measured hotspots) and the gotcha (inline props defeat memo). “It depends” with no content stalls; “always memo” is wrong and reads junior.

03 Your drill kit

Four surfaces, used in order: learn from the bank, compress with cheat-sheets, burn it in with flashcards, then rehearse the open-ended design round out loud.

Question bank

~55 high-signal Q&As grouped by topic, each with a model answer to say out loud.

Cheat-sheets

Night-before rapid reference: comparison tables for the calls you’ll be asked to make.

Flashcards

Active-recall decks per topic. Flip, mark known, shuffle the gaps to the front.

The design round

Walkthroughs for autocomplete, data tables, and “make it fast” — rehearse the open-ended one.
