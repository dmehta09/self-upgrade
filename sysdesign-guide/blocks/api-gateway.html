API gateway & reverse proxies.

A reverse proxy sits in front of your services and speaks to the outside world on their behalf. An API gateway is a reverse proxy with opinions — it handles the cross-cutting concerns every service would otherwise re-implement: TLS, authentication, rate limiting, routing, and request shaping. It's the single, well-guarded front door.

01  Why a front door

In plain English analogy

It's the reception desk of an office building. Visitors don't wander straight to people's desks — they check in at reception, which verifies their ID, hands them a badge, and points them to the right floor. Your services stay focused on their work; reception handles security and directions once, for everyone.

02  What it takes off your services' plate

🔒

TLS termination & auth

Decrypt HTTPS once; verify the caller's identity (token/JWT) once. Services behind it can trust the request.

🚦

Rate limiting & throttling

Protect everything downstream from abuse and spikes in one place. (See the rate limiter case study.)

🧭

Routing & service discovery

Map public paths to internal services, which may move or scale. Clients see one stable API.

🪄

Aggregation & shaping

Fan one client call out to several services and combine the results (the "backend for frontend" pattern); transform responses.

Don't let it become a monolith

A gateway is great for generic cross-cutting concerns. Pushing business logic into it recreates the monolith you split up — now as a bottleneck everyone shares. Keep it thin; keep domain logic in the services.

03  Reverse proxy vs forward proxy vs load balancer

Easy to confuse:

Reverse proxy
Sits in front of servers, facing clients. Clients think they're talking to one server. (Nginx, Envoy.) An API gateway is a feature-rich reverse proxy.
Forward proxy
Sits in front of clients, facing the internet. Hides who's making the request (corporate proxy, VPN).
Load balancer
A reverse proxy whose main job is spreading traffic across a pool. In practice gateways and LBs overlap; the gateway adds auth/routing on top of balancing.

Where's the best place to enforce a global "100 requests/minute per user" limit?

Right: the gateway is the one place every request passes through, so a limit there is consistent and protects all services. Per-service limits duplicate logic and miss cross-service abuse; client-side limits can't be trusted.
