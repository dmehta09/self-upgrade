Data at scale · sharding

Sharding & partitioning.

When one database can't hold all your data or handle all your writes, you split it across many machines — each holding a shard (a slice of the rows). Done well, you scale writes and storage almost linearly. Done badly, you create a "hot shard" that takes all the traffic, or paint yourself into a corner where re-splitting means moving everything. The whole game is choosing the shard key.

01  Splitting the data

In plain English analogy

One librarian can't manage a billion books. So you open several branches and split the collection by a rule — "A–F here, G–M there." The rule is the shard key. A good rule keeps every branch equally busy; a bad rule sends everyone to the branch holding the one popular author.

03  Hot shards & cross-shard pain

🔥

Hot shard / celebrity key

If your shard key is skewed (e.g., shard by country and 60% are in one), that shard melts. Pick a high-cardinality, evenly-distributed key — or split the hot key further.

🔗

Cross-shard queries & joins

A query spanning shards must hit them all and merge — slow. Transactions across shards are hard. Choose the shard key so your common queries stay within one shard.

The shard key is a one-way door

Changing your shard key later means migrating the entire dataset — painful and risky. Spend real time choosing it: it should spread load evenly and keep your hottest queries on a single shard. This single decision shapes the whole design.

A messaging app shards its messages table by message_id (random). Reading "all messages in a conversation" is now slow. Why, and what's the better shard key?

Right: sharding by a random message_id scatters one conversation across every shard, so reading it is a cross-shard fan-out. Sharding by conversation_id co-locates a conversation, turning the common query into a single-shard read.
