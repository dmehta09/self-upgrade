Data at scale · consensus

Consensus & leader election.

Once data lives on many machines, they have to agree on things — who's the leader, what order writes happened in, whether a value is committed. Consensus algorithms (Raft, Paxos) are how a cluster reaches agreement even when machines crash and networks drop messages. You won't implement Raft in an interview, but you absolutely need the intuition: leader election, quorums, and why "split-brain" is the nightmare they prevent.

01  The problem: split-brain

In plain English analogy

Picture a committee that can only act through one chairperson. If a network glitch splits the committee into two rooms and each elects its own chair, you now have two people both issuing orders — and the org tears itself apart making contradictory decisions. That's split-brain. Consensus is the committee's rule for making sure there's only ever one legitimate chair, even when the room splits.

Many systems pick a single leader to take writes (recall leader–follower replication). If two nodes both believe they're the leader, they accept conflicting writes and corrupt the data. Consensus exists to guarantee at most one leader and an agreed order of operations — the bedrock of correctness in distributed systems.

02  Leader election (the Raft intuition)

Raft makes consensus approachable. Nodes are followers until one's randomised timeout fires; it becomes a candidate, asks everyone for a vote, and if it wins a majority it becomes leader and sends heartbeats to keep the role. Watch it:

Key Raft words to drop: a term (a numbered election round), heartbeats (the leader's "I'm alive" pings), and randomised timeouts (so two nodes rarely time out at once, avoiding split votes). If the leader dies, a follower times out and a new election begins.

03  Why a majority — and odd numbers

The magic ingredient is the majority quorum: an action needs agreement from more than half the nodes. Two majorities can't exist at once, so two leaders can't be elected — split-brain is mathematically prevented. It also means the cluster keeps working as long as a majority is alive.

3 nodes
Majority = 2. Tolerates 1 failure.
5 nodes
Majority = 3. Tolerates 2 failures.
Why odd?
4 nodes also tolerate only 1 failure (majority = 3) — same fault tolerance as 3, but more cost and a higher chance of a tie. Odd sizes are strictly better.
Same idea as quorum reads/writes

This is the cousin of the W + R > N rule from replication. There, overlapping read/write majorities guarantee fresh reads; here, a single election majority guarantees one leader. Majorities are the universal trick for agreement under failure.

Prove it to yourself with N = 5. Set W and R to majorities (3/3), partition two nodes away, and try to commit on the minority side — the refusal you'll see is split-brain prevention:

The experiment that matters

Partition R4 and R5, then hit "Write to far side" with W=3: it fails — two nodes can never form a majority of five, so that side simply cannot make progress. Now switch to the unsafe 2/2 preset and repeat: both sides commit, and healing reports a split-brain conflict. One slider position is a consensus system; the other is a future incident review.

04  Where it shows up & distributed locks

You rarely build consensus yourself — you lean on a coordination service that has: ZooKeeper or etcd (and increasingly Raft built into the database). They provide leader election, configuration, and distributed locks (only one holder at a time) as a service.

  • Database failover — electing the new primary when the old one dies (without two primaries).
  • Kafka / schedulers — a single controller/coordinator chosen by election.
  • Distributed locks & leases — "only one worker runs this job," with a time-bounded lease so a crashed holder's lock expires.
  • Service config & membership — a consistent view of who's in the cluster.
Consensus is a CP choice — use it sparingly

Reaching a majority costs round trips and blocks when a majority isn't reachable (it favours consistency over availability). So put consensus only on the small, critical decisions — who's the leader, is this committed — and keep it off the high-throughput data path. Coordinate rarely; serve often.

Why must a new leader win a majority of votes rather than just any votes?

Right: any two majorities of the same cluster must overlap on at least one node, and a node won't vote for two leaders in the same term — so only one candidate can ever reach a majority. That overlap is exactly what makes split-brain impossible.
