Data at scale · choosing a store

SQL vs NoSQL.

The database is the heart of most designs, and "SQL or NoSQL?" is the question you'll answer on every one. The honest answer is "it depends on the access pattern" — so this page gives you a way to decide rather than a dogma. Relational databases trade some scale for powerful queries and rock-solid guarantees; NoSQL families trade query power for raw horizontal scale and flexible schemas.

01  The families

Relational (SQL)
Tables with a fixed schema, joins, and ACID transactions. PostgreSQL, MySQL. The default until you have a specific reason to leave.
Key-value
A giant hash map: get/put by key, nothing else. Redis, DynamoDB. Insanely fast and scalable for simple lookups.
Document
JSON-like documents, flexible schema, query by fields. MongoDB. Good when each record is self-contained.
Wide-column
Rows with huge, sparse, dynamic columns; built for massive write throughput. Cassandra, Bigtable, ScyllaDB. Great for time-series and feeds.
Graph
Nodes and edges with fast relationship traversal. Neo4j. For social graphs, fraud rings, recommendations.
In plain English analogy

A relational DB is a meticulous spreadsheet with strict columns and a clever accountant who can cross-reference any sheets you ask (joins) — wonderful, until the spreadsheet is too big for one computer. NoSQL stores are more like a wall of labelled boxes: you find a box instantly by its label, but there's no accountant to cross-reference them, so you arrange the boxes to match exactly how you'll look things up.

03  How to decide in an interview

Don't recite "NoSQL scales better." Reason from the data:

  • Relationships & transactions? (money, orders, "must be consistent") → SQL. Modern Postgres scales further than people think.
  • Known, simple access pattern at huge scale? (feed by user, messages by chat) → wide-column / key-value — model the table around that one query.
  • Flexible, evolving records? → document.
  • Traversing connections? → graph.
It's normal to use several

Real systems are polyglot: Postgres for orders, Redis for sessions, Cassandra for the feed, Elasticsearch for search, all in one product. "Right tool per access pattern" is a stronger answer than forcing everything into one database.

You're storing a chat app's messages: billions of writes, always read as "messages in this conversation, newest first," no ad-hoc queries. Best fit?

Right: the access pattern is fixed (by conversation, time-ordered) and write-heavy at massive scale — exactly what wide-column stores like Cassandra are built for. You design the partition key (conversation_id) and clustering (timestamp) around that one query.
