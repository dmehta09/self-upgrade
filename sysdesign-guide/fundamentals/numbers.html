Fundamentals · the numbers

Numbers you must know.

A handful of latency and size numbers let you answer "is that fast?" and "will that fit?" on instinct. You don't need them to the nanosecond — you need the relative scale: memory is ~100× faster than SSD, which is ~100× faster than a cross-country network round trip. Internalise the ladder and estimation becomes intuition.

01  Latency numbers every engineer should know

The classic table (rounded to 2026 hardware). The story it tells: stay in memory if you can, batch your network calls, and never hit disk on the hot path.

OperationTimeIn human terms
L1 cache reference~1 ns1 second
Branch mispredict~3 ns3 seconds
Main memory (RAM) reference~100 ns~2 minutes
Read 1 MB sequentially from RAM~3 µs~1 hour
SSD random read~16 µs~4 hours
Round trip within a datacenter~0.5 ms~6 days
Read 1 MB sequentially from SSD~1 ms~12 days
Disk (HDD) seek~3 ms~1 month
Round trip, same continent~30 ms~1 year
Round trip, intercontinental~150 ms~5 years
In plain English analogy

If an L1 cache read were "grabbing a pen on your desk" (1 second), then reading from RAM is "walking to the next room" (2 minutes), reading from disk is "a road trip" (a month), and a call to another continent is "waiting five years." That's why we cache: to keep work on the desk.

The latency ladder — each rung is a power of ten
The axis is logarithmic — every rung down costs ~100×. Architecture is the art of staying on the highest rung you can: cache before RAM-to-SSD, batch before crossing the network, and never put an ocean on the hot path.

02  Powers of two → data sizes

Storage is counted in powers of two, but for estimation we round to powers of ten. Know the ladder so "16-byte IDs × 1 billion rows" lands as "~16 GB" without a calculator.

PowerExactApproxName
2¹⁰1,024~1 thousandKilobyte (KB)

Handy item sizes: a char = 1 B (ASCII), a UUID = 16 B, an int/long = 4–8 B, a timestamp = 8 B, a tweet ≈ 300 B, a web page ≈ 100 KB–2 MB, a photo ≈ 0.5–5 MB, a minute of HD video ≈ 50 MB.

03  Rules of thumb that fall out

⚡

Memory is ~100,000× faster than a network hop

So a cache hit (~1 ms) vs a cross-region call (~100 ms) is the difference between instant and sluggish. Cache aggressively.

🌍

Speed of light sets a floor

You can't beat ~30 ms across a continent or ~150 ms around the world. To feel fast globally, you must move data near users (CDNs, regional replicas).

📦

Sequential ≫ random

Disks and SSDs love sequential reads. This is why databases use logs and B-trees, and why "append-only" designs are fast.

🔁

One big call beats many small ones

Network round trips dominate. Batch requests; a single 1 MB read is far cheaper than 1,000 tiny ones.

Your API makes 50 sequential calls to other services, each a ~1 ms datacenter round trip. Roughly what's the added latency — and the fix?

Right: 50 × ~1 ms in series ≈ 50 ms. Fan them out in parallel (latency drops to ~1 round trip) or batch them into one call. Round trips, not compute, are usually the bottleneck.

04  Percentiles: p50, p95, p99 (tail latency)

Latency isn't one number — it's a distribution, so we quote percentiles. The "p" just means "percentile" and the number is the cutoff: p99 = 99% of requests are faster than this value; the slowest 1% are worse. So p50 is the median (half are faster), p95 the 95th percentile, and p999 is shorthand for p99.9. Any pN works the same way — p65 would be "the value 65% of requests beat" — but in practice you'll almost always see p50, p95, p99, and p999.

Why we obsess over the tail (p99/p999) rather than the average: averages hide spikes — a system at p50 = 20 ms but p99 = 2 s feels broken to 1 in 100 users, yet its mean looks fine. Worse, tail latency compounds with fan-out:

The tail-at-scale trap

If one page makes 100 backend calls and each has just a 1% chance of being slow (its own p99), the chance that at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. A "rare" per-call tail becomes the common case for the whole request. That's why every SLO in this guide is a p99 (not an average), and why you parallelise, set timeouts on the tail, and sometimes hedge (send a duplicate request and take the first reply).
