Fundamentals · the framework

Reading a system-design question.

A system-design interview isn't a quiz with one right answer — it's a 35-minute conversation where you turn a vague prompt ("design Twitter") into a sensible architecture, out loud, while explaining your trade-offs. This page is the framework every case study in this guide follows. Learn it once and you'll never stare at a blank whiteboard again.

01  What's really being tested

The interviewer doesn't expect you to have memorised the real Twitter. They want to see how you think: can you scope an ambiguous problem, reason about scale with numbers, make a decision and defend it, and notice what breaks when traffic grows 100×?

In plain English analogy

It's like an architect being handed "design me a building." A bad architect starts drawing walls. A good one asks: a house or a hospital? How many people? What's the budget? — and only then sketches. In system design, the questions you ask are worth as much as the boxes you draw.

02  The framework — seven moves

Every design in this guide walks the same path. It keeps you calm and makes sure you never forget the boring-but-critical steps (like estimating scale) that separate strong candidates from the rest.

1

Clarify & scope

Ask questions. Nail the few features that matter, and explicitly push the rest out of scope. A focused design beats a broad, shallow one.

2

Functional & non-functional requirements

What it must do (post a tweet, read a feed) and how well (scale, latency, availability, consistency). The non-functionals drive the architecture.

3

Estimate the scale

Back-of-the-envelope QPS, storage, and bandwidth. These numbers decide whether one database is fine or you need 50 shards.

→ Back-of-envelope math
4

Define the API

A handful of endpoints pins down the contract between client and server, and surfaces hidden requirements.

5

Sketch the data model

The core entities and how they're stored. Choosing SQL vs NoSQL here often follows straight from the access pattern.

6

Draw the high-level architecture

The boxes — clients, load balancer, services, caches, databases, queues — and the request path through them.

7

Deep-dive & scale it

Pick the 2–3 hardest parts, go deep, and identify bottlenecks. This is where the signature trade-off of each system lives.

03  Functional vs non-functional

The single most useful split. Functional requirements are features. Non-functional requirements are the qualities — and they are what actually shape a distributed system.

✅

Functional (what it does)

"A user can post a tweet." "A user sees a feed of people they follow." "A user can search." Verbs and nouns from the prompt.

📐

Non-functional (how well)

Scale (how many users / QPS), latency (how fast), availability (how often up), consistency (how fresh), durability, and cost.

Two questions that unlock most designs

Read-heavy or write-heavy? (A feed is read-heavy; a logging system is write-heavy.) And how fresh must data be? (A bank balance: instantly correct. A like count: a few seconds late is fine.) The answers point you straight at caching, replication, and consistency choices.

04  Always anchor on scale

"Design Twitter" for 100 users is a weekend project; for 300 million it's a different machine entirely. Before drawing boxes, say the numbers out loud: daily active users, reads per second, writes per second, storage per year. Those numbers justify every later decision — "we shard because one box can't hold 50 TB," "we cache because reads outnumber writes 100 : 1." The next lesson makes that math fast.

An interviewer says "design Instagram." What's the strongest first move?

Right: scoping first shows judgement and prevents you from designing the wrong system. Tech name-drops without a reason are a red flag, not a green one.
