Design a cell-based architecture.

Past a certain size, the scariest failures aren't machines dying — they're platform-wide ones: a bad deploy, a poison request, a corrupted config that takes everyone down at once. Cell-based architecture is the answer the biggest operators converged on: partition the whole stack into N self-contained copies ("cells"), give each a slice of customers, and let nothing cross between them. Now any failure — hardware, software, or human — is capped at 1/N of customers. This page is the deep-dive the multi-tenancy lesson points at: blast radius, shuffle sharding, cell routing, and migration.

01  Why cells: the failures redundancy can't fix

Replicas and multi-AZ protect you from infrastructure failure. They do nothing against correlated failure: the bad deploy reaches every replica; the poison pill request crashes every server that touches it; the config typo propagates everywhere in seconds. The fix is not more copies of one big system — it's many small systems that share nothing.

In plain English analogy

A ship's hull is divided into watertight compartments — not because compartments prevent collisions, but so that one breach floods one compartment instead of sinking the ship. Cells are watertight compartments for your platform: the breach still happens; the sinking doesn't.

A cell is
A complete, independent stack — gateway, services, queues, databases — serving a fixed subset of customers, with a capacity ceiling. You scale by adding cells, never by growing one.
A cell is not
A shard (shards split data under shared services), an AZ (an infrastructure boundary, not a software one), or a region (cells live inside regions; a region may hold many cells).
Who runs them
AWS (cell-based architecture is their prescriptive guidance and underlies many AWS services), Slack, DoorDash, Roblox, Salesforce (the original "pods"). When an interviewer asks "how does Slack survive a bad deploy?", this is the answer.

02  The thin router: the only shared thing

If cells share nothing, something must still answer "which cell owns customer X?" That's the cell router — and it is the design's crux, because it's the one component every request touches: it must be boring. Keep it a thin lookup (no business logic), cache its answers aggressively (client-side + edge), and make it static-stable: if the mapping service dies, routers keep serving the last known map.

Directory vs hash
A directory mapping (explicit customer→cell table) beats pure consistent hashing here: you control placement (pin a big customer to a quiet cell, honor residency), and migration is a row update. Hashing places blindly; directories place deliberately. Use consistent hashing only to pick a default for new customers.
Partition key choice
Pick the key ALL traffic naturally carries: tenant/org id for B2B, user id for consumer. If a request can't name its key, it can't be routed — surface that in the API design early.
Cell size
Big enough to amortize the fixed cost (each cell runs full infra), small enough that 1/N is an acceptable loss and a cell fits the largest customer with headroom. Common shape: dozens of cells, each capped well below what the stack has been load-tested to.

03  Shuffle sharding: blast radius below 1/N

Plain cells cap damage at one cell's customer list. Shuffle sharding goes further for request-routing layers: give each customer a random combination of nodes (their "virtual cell"). Two customers then rarely share their full set — so a poison customer who kills their own nodes takes out… mostly just themselves.

The math: with n nodes and shards of size k, there are C(n,k) virtual cells. For n=100, k=5 that's ~75 million combinations — two customers sharing all 5 nodes is a lottery jackpot. AWS Route 53 runs exactly this scheme.

05  Migration, ceilings & what cells cost you

Moving a customer
Directory mapping makes it a controlled dance: dual-write (or replicate) the customer's data old→new cell, drain in-flight work, flip the directory row, clean up. Same playbook as the pool→silo promotion. Practice it routinely — a migration path you've never exercised is a migration path you don't have.
The whale problem
Your largest customer must fit in one cell with headroom — they're the floor on cell size. Whales get dedicated cells (a cell of one = the silo model); shuffle sharding handles the long tail.
Capacity ceilings
Each cell has a tested max. New customers go to the emptiest cell (bin-packing by the directory); cells nearing the ceiling stop accepting. You provision by ADDING CELLS on a predictable cadence — capacity planning becomes counting.
What it costs
N copies of fixed infra (N× control planes, queues, DB clusters — real money); cross-customer features get hard (global search/analytics need an aggregation plane fed async from all cells); operational tooling must be cell-aware (deploys, dashboards, on-call all speak "which cell?"). Cells are an investment you make when blast radius is worth more than efficiency — usually past serious B2B scale or a painful global outage.
The router is your new SPOF — treat it like one

Everything shared got squeezed into one thin layer, so that layer gets the full paranoia budget: static (precomputed) routing data shipped to the edge, no runtime dependency on the mapping store, multiple independent router deployments, and changes to the map rolled out as carefully as code. AWS's phrase for the property you want is static stability: the router keeps working on cached state even when its control plane is down.

06  Trade-offs & interview recap

Cell 2 dies and its customers are down. An engineer proposes the router immediately fail their traffic over to cells 1 and 3, "since they're healthy." What's the senior answer?

Right: cell 2's customers' data lives in cell 2 — other cells couldn't serve them correctly anyway — and the healthy cells' ceilings exist to protect their own customers. Containment, not redistribution, is the design's entire point. Restore the cell; don't breach the compartments.
Say this in the interview

"I'd partition the platform into cells — complete shared-nothing stacks, each owning a directory-assigned slice of customers with a capacity ceiling. A thin, static-stable router is the only shared component. Failures and deploys are then bounded at 1/N: waves roll cell by cell, a dead cell is restored rather than spilled over, and shuffle sharding shrinks the poison-pill blast radius below 1/N. The costs I'm accepting: N× fixed infrastructure, and cross-customer features move to an async aggregation plane."
