Availability, reliability & SLAs.

Availability is the percentage of time your system is up and answering. It's quoted in "nines" — 99.9% is three nines — and each extra nine is exponentially harder and more expensive. Knowing what the nines cost (in downtime and in engineering) lets you pick the right target instead of reflexively saying "five nines."

01  The nines

The headline table. The jump from three nines to four isn't 10× more effort by accident — it usually means removing every manual step from recovery, because there's no time for a human to react.

In plain English analogy

Think of a shop. "Two nines" means you're closed for 3.5 days a year — fine for a corner store. "Five nines" means closed for barely 5 minutes a year — that's a hospital ER, and it costs accordingly. Don't pay hospital prices for a corner-store problem.

02  How you buy availability: redundancy

The core trick is simple: have more than one of everything, and remove single points of failure (SPOFs). If any one component going down can take the system with it, that's your ceiling.

🧱

Redundancy

Multiple servers behind a load balancer, replica databases, multiple availability zones / regions. One dies, traffic shifts to the rest.

🔀

Failover

Detect failure (health checks) and route around it. Active-passive (a standby takes over) or active-active (all live, share load).

🧯

Graceful degradation

When a dependency is down, serve a worse-but-working answer (stale cache, fewer features) instead of an error.

🔁

Retries + circuit breakers

Retry transient failures with backoff; trip a breaker when a dependency is clearly down so you don't pile on.

Availability multiplies

A request that depends on 5 services each at 99.9% has availability 0.999⁵ ≈ 99.5% — worse than any single part. Long dependency chains quietly erode availability, which is an argument for fewer, looser dependencies (and async where possible).

Series divides, parallel multiplies (your way to nines)
Redundancy converts multiplication of failure probabilities into your favour — two 99% replicas beat one 99.9% server. The fine print: the math only holds when failures are independent, which is why replicas go in different AZs.

03  SLI, SLO & SLA

Three terms that get muddled. They nest: you measure an SLI, target it with an SLO, and promise a looser version in an SLA.

SLI — Indicator (the measurement)
A number you actually observe about the service's health, from the user's perspective. "99.95% of requests succeeded in < 200 ms this month." It's a ratio, not a vibe — see the formula below.
SLO — Objective (your internal target)
The threshold you hold that SLI to, over a window. "99.9% of requests succeed, measured over a rolling 30 days." This is what you design for and alert on — the team's reliability goal, set by engineering + product. No money attached.
SLA — Agreement (the customer contract)
A contractual promise to external customers, written by sales/legal, with penalties (service credits, refunds) if breached. Deliberately looser than the SLO so you have margin. "We guarantee 99.5% uptime, or you get a 10% credit." Many internal services have SLOs but no SLA at all.

Choosing a good SLI

An SLI is almost always a ratio: good events ÷ valid events × 100 (successful requests ÷ all requests). Two rules: (1) measure from the user's side — at the load balancer or the client, not deep inside one server, or you'll miss the failures users actually see; (2) latency SLIs use a threshold + percentile ("95% of requests faster than 300 ms"), never an average — averages hide the slow tail (p99). The common menu: availability (success ratio), latency, error rate, throughput, freshness/correctness, and durability. Pick the 2–3 that map to what users care about — not everything you can graph.

Your SLA promises customers 99.5% availability. What should your internal SLO be?

Right: the SLO is your early-warning line, set stricter than the SLA. The gap is your safety margin — if you blow the SLO you still have room before customers can claim credits. Never set the SLO at or below the SLA.

04  Error budgets

An SLO below 100% is permission to fail a little. The leftover — 100% − SLO — is your error budget: a concrete amount of "badness" (downtime, slow or failed requests) you're allowed to spend each window. Flip the nines into a monthly allowance:

SLOError budgetAllowed "bad" time / 30 days
99%1%~7.2 hours
99.9%0.1%~43 min
99.95%0.05%~21.6 min
99.99%0.01%~4.3 min

The budget reframes reliability as a currency both sides spend. Engineers want to ship features (risky); ops wants stability. The error budget settles the fight: while there's budget left, ship freely; when it's exhausted, the error-budget policy kicks in — freeze risky releases and pour effort into reliability until you're back under budget. Downtime stops being "ops's problem" and becomes a shared cost of changing things.

Burn rate: alert on the budget, not the blip

Instead of paging on every error spike, alert on how fast you're burning the budget. Burn rate = how many times faster than "sustainable" you're spending it (1× exactly exhausts it by the window's end). A fast burn (e.g. 14× over an hour — you'd torch a month's budget in days) pages immediately; a slow burn just files a ticket. This is the SRE multi-window, multi-burn-rate approach — it catches real problems without waking anyone for noise.

You run a 99.9% monthly SLO. By day 10 you've already burned 90% of the month's error budget. What's the right move?

Right: burning 90% of the budget with two-thirds of the month left means you're on track to blow the SLO. The error-budget policy converts that signal into action — slow down, stabilise — rather than waiting until you've fully breached and customers are hitting the SLA.
