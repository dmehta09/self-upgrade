Fundamentals · estimation

Back-of-the-envelope math.

Estimation is the step most people skip and interviewers most want to see. It's not about precision — it's about turning "design YouTube" into "≈ 1 million writes a day, 100 : 1 reads, petabytes a year" so your architecture is grounded in reality. The good news: it's three formulas and some rounding.

01  Why bother

Numbers settle arguments. "Should we shard the database?" is unanswerable until you know it must hold 50 TB and one machine holds 2 TB — then the answer is obviously yes, and you can say why. Estimation converts hand-waving into decisions.

In plain English analogy

It's the same as a builder estimating "this wall needs about 500 bricks." Nobody counts each brick — they multiply length × height × bricks-per-square-metre and round. You're doing exactly that for requests and bytes.

02  The recipe — three formulas

Start from one number you can guess or are told — daily active users (DAU) — and a couple of assumptions. Everything else follows.

Writes per second
DAU × (writes per user per day) ÷ 86,400. There are ~86,400 seconds in a day — round to 10⁵ to do it in your head.
Reads per second
writes/s × (read : write ratio). Most consumer apps are heavily read-heavy — 10 : 1 to 1000 : 1.
Peak QPS
average QPS × a peak factor (2–10×). Traffic isn't flat; design for the spike, not the mean.
Storage
writes/day × bytes per item × retention (days) × replication factor. This is what decides sharding.
Bandwidth
QPS × bytes per item. Matters most for media (images, video) — often the real bottleneck.
The one constant to memorise

1 day ≈ 86,400 s ≈ 10⁵ s. So "1 million events/day" ≈ ~12/s average. Everything else is multiplication.
