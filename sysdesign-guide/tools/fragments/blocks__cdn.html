CDN & the edge.

A Content Delivery Network is a fleet of cache servers spread across the globe. When a user requests an image, video, or script, it's served from an edge location physically near them instead of your origin server an ocean away. Since light itself takes ~150 ms to cross the planet, moving bytes closer is the only way to feel fast everywhere.

01  Why distance is the enemy

In plain English analogy

Instead of one central warehouse shipping every order across the country, a CDN is like having a local depot in every city. Popular items sit in the depot near you, so delivery is minutes not days. The first person in a city to order something triggers a restock from the warehouse; everyone after gets it locally.

Same page, two distances
Physics sets the floor: ~250 ms of round trips happen 3–4 times during TLS setup alone. Serving from 20 ms away doesn't make the page a bit faster — it changes its class.

02  What to put on a CDN

🖼️

Static assets

Images, CSS, JS bundles, fonts, and especially video — the bulk of most apps' bandwidth. The obvious win.

⚡

Dynamic acceleration & edge compute

Modern CDNs cache personalised fragments, run code at the edge (auth, A/B tests, redirects), and keep persistent connections back to origin.

The two control knobs are TTL (how long an edge keeps a copy before rechecking) and invalidation (forcing edges to drop a copy now). Because invalidation is slow and global, the common trick is versioned URLs: ship app.v7.js instead of editing app.js, so a new version is simply a new, un-cached URL.

03  Cache hit ratio is the metric

A CDN only helps to the extent it serves from cache. A 95% hit ratio means only 1 in 20 requests bothers your origin — that's the difference between a small origin fleet and a giant one. You raise it by caching aggressively, normalising URLs, and not accidentally marking everything no-cache.
