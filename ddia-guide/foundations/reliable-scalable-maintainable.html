Reliable, scalable & maintainable.

Before any database or queue, three words decide whether a data system is any good: it should keep working when things go wrong (reliable), keep working as it grows (scalable), and keep being pleasant to work on (maintainable). Everything else in the book is detail under these three.

01 The three words

A data-intensive app is limited not by CPU power but by data — its amount, complexity, and rate of change. The job is to keep that data reliable, scalable, and maintainable.

In plain English analogy

Think of a city's water utility. Reliable = clean water keeps flowing even when a pump fails. Scalable = it serves a town today and a city in ten years. Maintainable = a new engineer can fix a leak at 3 a.m. without a map only the founder understands. A data system has the same three duties.

02 Reliability — tolerating faults

Reliability means the system keeps doing the right thing, at acceptable performance, even when things go wrong. The things that go wrong are faults; a fault that stops the whole system serving users is a failure. You can't prevent faults — you build systems that tolerate them so a fault never becomes a failure.

Fault ≠ failure

A fault is one component deviating from spec (a disk dies, a packet drops, a human fat-fingers a config). A failure is the system as a whole stopping. Fault-tolerance is the art of letting many faults happen without a single failure — and the counter-intuitive trick is to trigger faults on purpose (chaos engineering) so you trust the machinery.

Kind of faultExampleDefence
HardwareDisk crash, RAM error, power lossRedundancy: RAID, dual power, replicas across machines
SoftwareA bug triggered by one bad input, cascading failuresIsolation, careful testing, process restarts, measuring & alerting
HumanMisconfiguration — the leading cause of outagesGood interfaces, sandboxes, fast rollback, telemetry

03 Scalability — describing & handling load

Scalability isn't a label (“X is scalable”) — it's a question: if load grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping? To answer it you must first describe load with numbers, then describe performance honestly.

Describe performance with percentiles — the average lies

“Average response time” hides the pain. What users feel is the tail: the slowest few percent of requests. Report percentiles — p50 (median), p95, p99 — not the mean. Drag the control below: a request that must wait on several backends gets slower than any one of them, because it's only as fast as its slowest call. This is tail-latency amplification.

Why tails matter

The slowest requests often belong to your most valuable customers — the ones with the most data. And because a single page may fire dozens of backend calls, the chance that at least one is slow climbs fast. That's why the SLA is written on p99 / p99.9, not the average. Scaling up (a bigger machine) is simple but capped; scaling out (more machines) is the only path past one box — and the rest of this book is mostly about doing it.

04 Maintainability — operability, simplicity, evolvability

The majority of a system's cost is after it ships. Maintainability has three sides:

Operability
Make it easy for ops to keep it running — good monitoring, predictable behaviour, sane defaults, self-healing where possible.
Simplicity
Manage complexity so a newcomer can understand the system. The enemy is accidental complexity — complexity not inherent in the problem. The tool against it is good abstraction.
Evolvability
Make change easy. Requirements always shift; a maintainable system is one you can refactor and extend without fear (the book later calls this agility).
See also sibling guides

“Simplicity = fighting accidental complexity via abstraction” is the entire thesis of A Philosophy of Software Design. And the reliability/availability numbers (five-nines, SLAs) get the system-design treatment in the System Design Field Guide.

Your service's mean response time looks great at 40 ms, but support says some users find it “unusably slow.” What should you look at first?

Right: the mean hides outliers. A great average with a terrible p99 means a real slice of requests — often your heaviest users — are suffering. Measure and target the tail.
