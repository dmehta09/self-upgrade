Storage & retrieval.

A database does two things: when you give it data, store it; when you ask, give it back. How it does that under the hood splits storage engines into two great families — log-structured (LSM-trees) and page-oriented (B-trees) — and the choice quietly decides whether your workload flies or crawls.

01 The simplest database is a log

Appending to a file is the fastest possible write. Every fast storage engine is built on that idea — and then adds an index so reads don't have to scan the whole file.

In plain English analogy

Two ways to run a stockroom. (1) Scribble every change at the bottom of an ever-growing logbook — instant to write, but to find an item you read the whole book. (2) Keep a tidy card catalogue you reorganise on every change — slower to update, instant to look up. That's exactly LSM-trees vs B-trees.

An append-only log is O(1) to write but O(n) to read. So we add an index: a separate structure that points to where data lives. An index always speeds reads and slows writes — that's the fundamental trade-off of all storage engines.

02 The two families

Flip between them. Both keep keys sorted; they differ in where the work happens.

03 Two worlds: OLTP vs OLAP

Storage layout also depends on access pattern. Most apps are OLTP (transaction processing): tiny reads/writes by key, latency-critical. Analytics is OLAP: scan billions of rows over a few columns. They want opposite layouts.

Row-oriented · OLTP

Store all of a row's columns together. One read grabs the whole record — perfect for “fetch user 251.” The default for operational databases.

Column-oriented · OLAP

Store each column's values together. Scanning “average price over a billion sales” touches one column, not whole rows — and similar values compress beautifully. The data-warehouse layout (Parquet, Redshift, BigQuery).

Why analytics gets its own database

Running heavy column-scans on your OLTP database would crush the live app. So data is copied (ETL) into a separate data warehouse with a column-oriented engine — same data, opposite storage layout, tuned for the opposite job.

Right: LSM-trees turn random writes into sequential appends and pack data densely during compaction — ideal for write-heavy ingestion. B-trees shine when reads must be fast and predictable.
