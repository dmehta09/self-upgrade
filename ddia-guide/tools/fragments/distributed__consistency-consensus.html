Part II · Distributed Data · Chapter 9

Consistency & consensus.

Given unreliable networks, lying clocks, and pausing processes, how do a pile of machines agree on anything? This chapter builds the strongest tools we have: linearizability (behave like a single copy) and consensus (get everyone to agree on one value) — and is honest about what they cost.

01 Linearizability — the single-copy illusion

Linearizability is the strongest single-object guarantee: the system behaves as if there is exactly one copy of the data and every operation is atomic. Once one client reads the new value, every later read sees it too — a recency guarantee.

In plain English analogy

Two referees watch the same match on screens fed from different servers. Eventual consistency means one might still show 1–0 while the other shows 1–1 for a few seconds. Linearizable consistency means the instant the goal is official, every screen flips together — there is one truth, and nobody ever sees an older score after a newer one. That single-shared-reality feeling is worth real money, and you pay for it.

Linearizability isn't the same as serializability: serializability is about transactions (grouping operations); linearizability is about recency on a single object. You sometimes want both, and the combination (strict serializability) is the most expensive of all. It's required for things like uniqueness constraints, leader election, and locks.

02 The price: you can't beat a network partition

The famous CAP theorem is narrow but real: when the network partitions (nodes can't reach each other), a replicated system must choose — stay consistent (refuse requests it can't safely serve) or stay available (answer with possibly-stale data). You cannot have both during a partition. There's no choice the rest of the time.

CAP is often mis-stated

It's not “pick 2 of 3.” With no partition you get both C and A; the choice only bites during a partition. And linearizability is also just slow even when the network is fine — many systems drop it not for CAP but for raw latency.

03 Ordering, causality, and total order broadcast

A lot of consistency reduces to ordering events. The minimum you usually want is causal order: if A caused B (you read a message, then reply), everyone must see A before B. Causal order is the strongest consistency that survives a partition — but it's only a partial order; concurrent events stay unordered.

To get a single agreed sequence, you need total order broadcast: deliver the same messages to every node, in the same order. That turns out to be equivalent to consensus — solve one and you've solved the other (and you can build a linearizable store from it).

04 Consensus — agreeing on one value

Consensus means several nodes decide on one value and never change their minds, even as some crash. It sounds simple; the FLP result proves it's impossible to guarantee in a purely asynchronous system with even one faulty node — yet real algorithms (Paxos, Raft, ZAB) work in practice by using timeouts and randomness to make progress. At their heart sits one idea: overlapping majorities.

The same rule powers leaderless replication (w + r > n) and consensus: any two majorities of an odd-sized cluster must share at least one member, so two conflicting decisions can't both reach a majority. That's why consensus systems use an odd number of nodes (3, 5) and a majority quorum.

You rarely implement consensus — you rent it

Getting consensus right is brutally hard, so systems outsource it to a coordination service like ZooKeeper or etcd. They provide leader election, locks, and configuration on top of a consensus core — the fencing tokens from the last chapter come from exactly here. Lean on them; don't roll your own.

A consensus cluster has 5 nodes. A network partition splits it into a group of 3 and a group of 2. Which side can keep making decisions, and why?

Right: only the majority side (3 of 5) can form a quorum. Because every majority overlaps every other majority, the minority side (2) physically cannot reach a conflicting decision — it must wait for the partition to heal. That's the CP choice, and the reason clusters are sized odd.
