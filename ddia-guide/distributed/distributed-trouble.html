Part II · Distributed Data · Chapter 8

The trouble with distributed systems.

On one machine, things are deterministic: an operation works, or the whole machine is down. Spread across a network and that comfort vanishes — messages vanish or arrive late, clocks disagree, and a node can freeze mid-step. This chapter is a catalogue of the ways reality betrays you, so your designs assume the worst.

01 The network is unreliable

When you send a request and hear nothing back, you cannot tell whether the node never got it, is still working on it, crashed, or replied and the reply was lost. A timeout is just a guess dressed up as a fact.

In plain English analogy

You mail a colleague a letter and hear nothing for a week. Did it get lost? Are they on holiday? Did they reply and their letter got lost? You genuinely can't know — all you can do is wait a while and then assume the worst. Distributed nodes live in exactly this fog, and the only tool is the timeout: wait t seconds, then act as if the other side is gone.

02 Clocks lie

Every machine has clocks, and they drift. Synchronisation (NTP) helps but can't make them trustworthy for ordering events across nodes. Crucially, there are two kinds, and confusing them causes bugs:

Time-of-day clock

“Wall clock” — seconds since 1970. Can jump backwards on an NTP correction, or leap. Never use it to measure durations or to order events. Yet “last write wins” often does exactly that — silently dropping writes whose clock happened to run fast.

Monotonic clock

Only ever moves forward; meaningless as an absolute time, but perfect for measuring an elapsed interval on one machine (timeouts, latencies). Use this for durations — never the wall clock.

A timestamp is a confidence interval, not a point

Because of clock uncertainty, a timestamp really means “sometime in this range.” Google's Spanner embraces this: it waits out the uncertainty window before committing, so it can use real time for ordering. Most systems instead order events with logical clocks (counters), which capture causality without trusting wall-clock time.

03 Processes pause — and the leader you trust may be a ghost

A thread can freeze for seconds at any moment: a garbage-collection “stop the world” pause, a VM suspended by the hypervisor, the laptop lid closing. The process can't tell it was paused — it resumes mid-operation believing no time passed. So a node that “knows” it's still the leader may have lost that role minutes ago.
