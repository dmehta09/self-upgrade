Complexity · the enemy

What is complexity?

Ousterhout's whole book rests on one claim: the greatest limit on what we can build in software is our ability to understand the systems we create. Complexity is the name for everything that fights that understanding — and the entire craft of design is the fight to keep it down.

01 A working definition

Complexity is anything about the structure of a system that makes it hard to understand or modify.

In plain English analogy

A complex codebase is a messy kitchen. You can still cook in it — but every dish takes longer, you knock things over reaching for a pan, and a newcomer can't find the salt. Nothing is individually broken; the arrangement is what slows everyone down.

Two things follow. First, complexity is judged by readers and maintainers, not by the author at the moment of writing — code that feels obvious to you today can be a maze to the next person (or to you in six months). Second, it is structural: it lives in how the pieces relate, not in any single clever line.

02 The three symptoms

You know complexity by how it feels when you work. Ousterhout names three tells:

Change amplification
A conceptually simple change forces edits in many places. “Just recolor the banner” → the same hex code is pasted in 40 templates.
Cognitive load
How much you must hold in your head to make a change safely — hidden side effects, ordering rules, who-calls-what. More to know = more to get wrong.
Unknown unknowns
There is something you must change to be correct, but you can't even tell what it is. The worst symptom: you can't fix what you can't see.
Red flag gotcha

The goal of good design is to make a system obvious — so the change you need is the change you can see. Unknown unknowns are deadly precisely because no amount of care helps if you don't know where to look.

03 Two root causes

All three symptoms grow from two underlying causes:

Dependencies

When a piece of code can't be understood or changed in isolation — it's entangled with others. Some dependencies are essential; design is about keeping them simple and obvious, not eliminating them.

Obscurity

When important information isn't obvious — a vague name, a hidden assumption, a magic number. The fix is usually a better abstraction or a comment, not more code.

Here obscurity hides a dependency in plain sight — the reader has no way to know the unit or the rule:

# Obscure: what is 86400? what timezone? is it inclusive?
if now - created > 86400:
    expire(session)

# Obvious: the knowledge is in the name and the type
ONE_DAY = timedelta(days=1)
if now - created > ONE_DAY:
    expire(session)

Same behaviour; the second version removes obscurity, so the reader needs nothing extra in their head.

04 Complexity is incremental

Complexity isn't one catastrophe — it's the accumulation of dozens of small chunks, each of which seemed harmless.

No single dependency or vague name sinks a project. But each adds a grain; comprehensibility erodes a grain at a time until no one fully understands the system and every change is slow and risky. That's why design discipline is a daily habit, not a quarterly cleanup — and why “it's just a little hack” is the most expensive sentence in software.

05 Check yourself

Which symptom of complexity is the most dangerous?

Unknown unknowns. With the first two you at least know the work in front of you. With unknown unknowns you'll ship a bug you never knew was possible — and care and effort don't save you, because you don't know where to apply them. Great design exists to turn unknown unknowns into obvious ones.
