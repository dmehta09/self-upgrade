Metrics · Histograms & Percentiles

Histograms & percentiles.

The average lies. A handful of slow requests get drowned in thousands of fast ones — they stay invisible until your most valuable users start churning. Percentiles expose them. But percentiles have their own trap: you cannot average them across instances.

01 Why averages lie

The mean hides the tail. A p99 shows you the worst experience 1 in 100 users actually had.

If 990 requests complete in 10 ms and 10 requests take 5 000 ms, the average is ~59 ms — a number that describes nobody's real experience. The p99 is 5 000 ms, which is the truth the average erased.

In plain English analogy

The average queue time at a coffee shop might be 2 minutes. But a few customers are stuck for 30 minutes — and they never come back. The average says everything is fine. The p99 tells you about those churning customers. Tail latency is the felt pain.

p50 (median)
Half of requests were faster, half were slower. A good "typical user" baseline.
p95
95% of requests were faster. The experience of a user on a slower-than-usual path.
p99
99% of requests were faster. Your most affected 1 in 100 users — often your power users or large accounts.
p99.9 (p999)
1 in 1 000 users. SLOs for high-value or real-time systems often target this.

02 How histograms work

A Prometheus histogram records every observation into pre-defined buckets (cumulative upper bounds). Each scrape exposes three series: _bucket, _count, _sum. Percentiles are computed server-side from the bucket data.

query.promql
# p99 across ALL pods — valid because we aggregate buckets first
histogram_quantile(
  0.99,
  sum by (le) (
    rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])
  )
)

The sum by (le) merges buckets from every pod before the quantile is computed. This is the correct aggregation pattern.

03 Bucket boundaries & native histograms

Classic histograms require you to declare bucket boundaries at instrumentation time. Choose too few and you lose resolution at the tail; too many and cardinality explodes.

Classic histogram

Fixed buckets declared upfront: [0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, +Inf]. A spike at 800 ms lands in the same bucket as 501 ms — invisible.

Native histogram (Prometheus 3.x)

Exponential buckets auto-generated by the client. Better resolution across all magnitudes. Still opt-in: --enable-feature=native-histograms. Remote-Write 2.0 carries them natively.

Common trap gotcha

Averaging p99s across pods is mathematically wrong. If pod A has p99 = 100 ms and pod B has p99 = 900 ms, the "average p99" of 500 ms is not the p99 of combined traffic — it could be far higher or lower depending on volume and bucket distribution.

Always aggregate buckets first (with sum by (le)), then compute the quantile once on the merged data.

04 Check yourself

Your service has 5 pods. Each reports a Prometheus summary p99 of roughly 200 ms. What is the system-wide p99?

Right. Summary quantiles are per-instance and not additive. Switch to a histogram and use histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(..._bucket[5m]))).

Average request latency is 40 ms but users are complaining. Which metric would most likely reveal the problem?

Right. The average masks a heavy tail. Look at p95 and p99 — if they're 2–10× higher than p50, a meaningful slice of users is getting a very different experience.

05 Interview rapid-fire

Why do we track p99 instead of average latency?warm
Model answer

The average hides the tail. A small number of very slow requests get diluted by the majority of fast ones, making the system look healthy while a fraction of users — often the most active — experience unacceptable latency. p99 shows the worst experience 1 in 100 users actually had, which is far more aligned with how users perceive reliability.

Say it out loud say it

"Averages erase the tail. I care about p99 because it's the experience of real users who are right at the edge of churning — the ones the average hides."

Why can't you average percentiles across pods, and what's the correct approach?core
Model answer

Percentiles are order statistics — they describe the position within a sorted distribution, not additive quantities. Averaging two p99 values gives a number that has no statistical meaning for the combined traffic. The correct approach: use a histogram metric, aggregate the raw bucket counters with sum by (le) across all pods, then apply histogram_quantile(0.99, ...) once on the merged data.

Say it out loud say it

"Percentiles aren't additive — averaging them is meaningless. With a histogram I sum the buckets across pods first, then compute the quantile once on the combined data."

Follow-ups to expect
  • What PromQL query would you write to get a valid cross-pod p99?
  • What are the trade-offs of classic vs native histograms?
What problem do native/exponential histograms in Prometheus 3.x solve?stretch
Model answer

Classic histograms require you to hard-code bucket boundaries at instrumentation time. If your latency spikes into a new range not covered by your buckets, you get poor precision and everything lands in +Inf. Native histograms use exponential bucket boundaries auto-generated at the client, giving good resolution across many orders of magnitude without upfront configuration. They're still opt-in in Prometheus 3.x via --enable-feature=native-histograms and are transmitted via Remote-Write 2.0.

Say it out loud say it

"Classic histograms need bucket boundaries upfront — miss the range and you lose precision. Native histograms auto-generate exponential buckets, so they self-adapt. Still experimental in Prometheus 3.x but the clear direction."
