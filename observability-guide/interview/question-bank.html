Observability question bank.

30+ questions grouped by theme. Each has a crisp model answer and a spoken version. Open the ones you're unsure about; close the ones you can recite cold.

01 Foundations

The warm-up layer — expect at least two of these early to gauge baseline understanding.

What's the difference between observability and monitoring?warm-up
Model answer

Monitoring answers known questions: dashboards and thresholds on failure modes you predicted. Observability means you can ask novel questions about system state from its telemetry outputs — without knowing the question in advance. Observability subsumes monitoring; it's the foundation for debugging unknown unknowns.

Say it out loud say it

"Monitoring is answering questions you thought of before the incident. Observability is being able to ask questions you didn't think of."

What are the three pillars of observability and when do you use each?core
Model answer

Metrics for aggregated health and alerting — cheap, good for SLOs. Traces for latency attribution across services — show you which span on the critical path is slow. Logs for root cause and payload inspection — they're expensive at scale so you filter by trace_id once you've narrowed down via traces. Together: metrics tell you something's wrong, traces show where, logs explain why.

Say it out loud say it

"Metrics tell you something is wrong, traces tell you where, logs tell you why. You start at metrics and drill down."

What is cardinality and why does it matter for metrics?core
Model answer

Cardinality is the number of unique time series a metric generates — it's the product of all unique label-value combinations. High-cardinality labels like user_id or request_id can create millions of series, blowing up Prometheus memory and query cost. Rule of thumb: keep metric labels to dimensions that have bounded, small value sets (endpoint, method, status). Use traces or logs for per-user or per-request data.

Say it out loud say it

"Cardinality is how many unique time series a metric creates. Add user_id as a label and you've just created one series per user — Prometheus will OOM. That data belongs in traces, not metrics."

What is the RED method?warm-up
Model answer

RED stands for Rate (requests per second), Errors (error rate), and Duration (latency, typically p95/p99). It's a service-level dashboard pattern: if you instrument every service with these three, you can spot degradation at a glance. RED is request-oriented; it pairs with USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) which is resource-oriented.

Say it out loud say it

"RED is Rate, Errors, Duration — three panels on every service dashboard. If one spikes you have a starting point."

What are Google's four golden signals?core
Model answer

From the SRE book: Latency, Traffic, Errors, Saturation. They're the minimum viable dashboard for any service. Latency includes both successful and failed requests (failed requests that are fast can mask a real problem). Saturation = how "full" the service is — CPU, memory, queue depth.

Say it out loud say it

"Latency, Traffic, Errors, Saturation. Basically RED plus Saturation — RED is the pragmatic simplification for microservices."

What is telemetry vs observability?warm-up
Model answer

Telemetry is the act of emitting signals from a system — logs, metrics, traces are telemetry data. Observability is the property of a system that lets you understand its internal state from those outputs. Telemetry is the means; observability is the property you achieve.

Say it out loud say it

"Telemetry is the data you emit. Observability is the property that data gives you — the ability to ask arbitrary questions about system state."

Why are averages dangerous for latency metrics?core
Model answer

Averages hide the tail. If 99% of requests complete in 5 ms and 1% take 2 000 ms, the average looks fine at ~25 ms. But that 1% represents real users having terrible experiences. Always use percentiles (p95, p99) for latency. p99 = at most 1% of requests are slower than this value.

Say it out loud say it

"Averages are liars for latency. A small number of very slow requests get diluted. I always alert on p99, not mean."

02 Signals — logs, metrics, traces

What are the four metric types in Prometheus / OTel?core
Model answer

Counter: monotonically increasing (requests_total, errors_total) — only use rate() or increase() in PromQL. Gauge: up/down value (memory_bytes, queue_depth). Histogram: samples observations into buckets; exposes _bucket, _sum, _count — use histogram_quantile() for percentiles. Summary: computes percentiles client-side — accurate but can't be aggregated across replicas.

Say it out loud say it

"Counter for things that only go up, gauge for things that go up and down, histogram for latency buckets, summary for client-side percentiles you can't aggregate."

Why prefer histogram over summary for latency?core
Model answer

A Prometheus summary computes quantiles in the client — you can't combine two replicas' summaries server-side. A histogram stores bucket counts and lets you compute quantiles at query time across any aggregation dimension (service, region, etc.) using histogram_quantile(). Native histograms in Prometheus 3.x use exponential buckets, eliminating the need to pre-define bucket boundaries.

Say it out loud say it

"Summary percentiles can't be aggregated across replicas. Histograms can — use histogram_quantile() at query time on the merged buckets."

What is a trace exemplar?core
Model answer

An exemplar is a sample data point attached to a histogram bucket that includes a trace_id. When p99 latency spikes, you can click the exemplar point in Grafana and jump directly to the slow trace in Tempo — closing the gap between "the metric says something is slow" and "here's the actual request that was slow." Exemplars are part of OpenMetrics and supported natively in Prometheus 3.x.

Say it out loud say it

"An exemplar is a trace_id embedded in a histogram data point. In Grafana, when p99 spikes I click the dot and jump straight to the offending trace — no manual correlation."

What is head sampling vs tail sampling?core
Model answer

Head sampling decides at the root span (trace start) — fast and cheap but blind to what happens later, so you can miss rare slow or error traces. Tail sampling buffers the complete trace at the Collector, then applies rules (error? slow? new path?) before deciding to keep or drop. Tail sampling keeps the interesting traces at the cost of memory and complexity in the Collector pipeline.

Say it out loud say it

"Head sampling flips a coin at the start — it's cheap but misses rare errors. Tail sampling waits for the full trace and keeps the ones that matter, but needs a Collector buffer."

What is structured logging and why does it matter?core
Model answer

Structured logging emits logs as key-value pairs (typically JSON) rather than free-form strings. This makes them machine-parseable: you can filter on service=checkout AND status=500 in Loki without regex. The critical habit: always include trace_id and span_id so you can jump from a trace span to its correlated logs.

Say it out loud say it

"Structured logs are JSON key-value pairs. You filter without regex, and adding trace_id lets you jump from a trace to its logs in one click."

What is context propagation in distributed tracing?core
Model answer

Context propagation is the mechanism that carries trace_id and span_id across service boundaries — typically in HTTP headers (W3C traceparent format) or messaging metadata. Without it, each service creates an isolated trace and you can't stitch them into a single end-to-end view. OTel's SDK propagators handle this automatically for supported frameworks.

Say it out loud say it

"Context propagation passes the trace_id in the request header — W3C traceparent. Without it every service creates its own orphan trace and you can't follow a request end-to-end."

What is the critical path in a trace?core
Model answer

The critical path is the sequence of sequential spans whose combined duration equals the total end-to-end latency. Spans running in parallel don't add to the total; only the longest sequential chain does. When you're optimizing latency, the only spans that matter are those on the critical path. Grafana Tempo's trace view highlights the critical path automatically.

Say it out loud say it

"The critical path is the longest sequential chain of spans. Making a parallel span faster doesn't help. I look at the critical path first when debugging latency."

What is a wide event / "observability 2.0"?stretch
Model answer

A wide event is a single, highly-contextual structured event per request containing all the fields you might ever want to slice by — user_id, feature flags, region, DB query time, cache hit, etc. Honeycomb popularized this as "observability 2.0": instead of pre-aggregating into separate metrics/logs/traces, you keep arbitrarily high-cardinality raw events and query them ad-hoc. The trade-off is storage cost and the need for a query engine designed for it.

Say it out loud say it

"A wide event is one fat structured record per request with every field you'd ever slice by. Honeycomb's model — you query raw high-cardinality events instead of pre-aggregating into pillars."

03 Standards — OTel, Prometheus, PromQL

What are the three layers of OpenTelemetry?core
Model answer

API: vendor-neutral interfaces your application code calls — nothing ships to a backend. SDK: implements the API; handles sampling, context propagation, batching, export. Collector: a standalone agent/gateway that receives OTLP, transforms (filtering, enrichment), and exports to any backend. Separating API from SDK means you can instrument once and swap backends by changing the SDK config.

Say it out loud say it

"API is the interface your code calls; SDK is the implementation; Collector is the pipeline agent. Instrument with the API once, swap the backend by changing the SDK and Collector config."

What is OTLP?core
Model answer

OTLP (OpenTelemetry Protocol) is the vendor-neutral wire protocol for all three signals — logs, metrics, traces — over gRPC or HTTP/protobuf. It replaces a zoo of agent-specific formats (Jaeger Thrift, Zipkin JSON, etc.). Prometheus 3.x can now ingest OTLP natively at /api/v1/otlp/v1/metrics. Jaeger v2 speaks OTLP end-to-end.

Say it out loud say it

"OTLP is one wire protocol for all three signals. Emit OTLP from your SDK, receive it in the Collector, forward to any backend — no format translation."

Explain Prometheus's pull model. When is push better?core
Model answer

Prometheus scrapes (pulls) metrics from /metrics endpoints on a configurable interval. Benefits: simple auth, no retry storms, you know exactly what's being collected and when. Push is better for short-lived jobs (batch, serverless) that may not be alive when Prometheus scrapes — use the Pushgateway. OTLP metrics can push directly to backends that support it, including Prometheus 3.x natively.

Say it out loud say it

"Pull is simpler and avoids auth sprawl. Push works for ephemeral jobs that might be dead when Prometheus scrapes — that's why Pushgateway exists."

What does rate() do in PromQL and why use it over raw counter values?core
Model answer

rate(counter[5m]) computes the per-second average rate of increase over the last 5 minutes, correctly handling counter resets (e.g. a pod restart). A raw counter only goes up and isn't meaningful for alerting; its derivative (rate) is. Use irate() for the instantaneous rate from the last two data points — good for fast dashboards, bad for sparse series.

Say it out loud say it

"A counter only goes up, so I use rate() to get requests-per-second. It also handles counter resets from pod restarts automatically."

What are OTel semantic conventions?core
Model answer

Semantic conventions are standardized attribute names for common operations — http.request.method, db.system, messaging.system. They ensure that instrumentation from different libraries and languages uses the same keys, so dashboards and alerts are portable. As of ~v1.41, HTTP, database, and messaging conventions are stable; gen_ai attributes are still experimental.

Say it out loud say it

"Semantic conventions are OTel's standardized attribute names — http.request.method, db.system. They mean every team's HTTP spans use the same keys, so one dashboard template works for all services."

What's the difference between LogQL and TraceQL?stretch
Model answer

LogQL is Loki's query language — a log pipeline: you select a stream by labels, then filter/parse/aggregate. Example: {service="checkout"} |= "ERROR". TraceQL is Grafana Tempo's trace query language — you search for spans matching conditions: { span.http.status_code = 500 && duration > 1s }. Both use a curly-brace stream selector but operate on completely different data.

Say it out loud say it

"LogQL queries Loki log streams; TraceQL queries Tempo spans. Both look similar but LogQL works on line data and TraceQL works on span attributes."

What is a native histogram in Prometheus 3.x?stretch
Model answer

A native histogram uses automatic exponential bucket boundaries — you don't pre-define buckets. This gives much better resolution at high values and lower cardinality (fewer series) than classic histograms with manually-defined buckets. It's still experimental in Prometheus 3.x, enabled via --enable-feature=native-histograms. Wire format: transmitted via OTLP or OpenMetrics.

Say it out loud say it

"Native histograms auto-scale buckets exponentially — no more guessing bucket boundaries upfront. Still experimental in Prometheus 3.x but solves the classic histogram config pain."

What does the OTel Collector do and why run it?core
Model answer

The Collector is a standalone pipeline: receive OTLP (or other formats), process (filter, enrich, sample), then export to one or more backends. Running it decouples your apps from backend choices — change the exporter config without redeploying services. It also handles tail sampling, attribute redaction, and batching at the infrastructure layer so apps stay lean.

Say it out loud say it

"The Collector is a pipeline between my app and the backend. Apps emit OTLP; the Collector filters, enriches, and routes. Changing backends is a Collector config change, not a code change."

04 Reliability — SLOs, SLAs, alerting

What is the difference between SLI, SLO, and SLA?core
Model answer

SLI (Service Level Indicator) is the actual measurement — e.g. the ratio of successful requests. SLO (Service Level Objective) is the target you set internally — e.g. 99.9% of requests succeed. SLA (Service Level Agreement) is the contractual commitment to a customer — usually looser than the SLO (e.g. 99.5%) to provide a safety buffer before penalties apply.

Say it out loud say it

"SLI is what you measure, SLO is what you aim for internally, SLA is what you promise the customer — usually 10–50 bp below the SLO so breaching the SLO doesn't immediately breach the contract."

What is an error budget and how do you use it?core
Model answer

The error budget is the allowed amount of unreliability implicit in the SLO. For a 99.9% SLO over 30 days, the budget is 0.1% × 30 × 24 × 60 = ~43 minutes of allowed downtime. When budget is healthy, you can ship fast. When it's nearly gone, you slow down or freeze releases. It turns reliability vs. velocity into a data-driven negotiation rather than tribal warfare.

Say it out loud say it

"Error budget is 100 minus the SLO — the downtime you're allowed. Budget healthy? Ship fast. Budget almost gone? Freeze deployments and invest in reliability."

What is a burn rate alert?core
Model answer

Burn rate measures how fast you're consuming error budget relative to sustainable pace. A 1× burn rate means you're burning budget at exactly the rate allowed (SLO just met at month end). A 14.4× burn rate means you'd exhaust the monthly budget in 1 hour — that's page-worthy. Multi-window burn-rate alerts combine a fast window (catches big spikes) and a slow window (filters transient noise).

Say it out loud say it

"Burn rate is how fast you're spending your error budget. 14.4x means you'd burn the whole month in one hour — page. Two windows catch spikes without false alarms on transients."

What causes alert fatigue and how do you fix it?core
Model answer

Alert fatigue comes from too many alerts that are noisy, transient, or don't require human action. The fixes: alert on symptoms (user-facing SLO burn) not causes (CPU > 80%), use burn-rate windows to filter transient spikes, set actionable thresholds ("if this fires, I know exactly what to do"), and route severity correctly — page only for budget-threatening burns, ticket otherwise.

Say it out loud say it

"Alert on the symptom — SLO burn rate — not the cause. CPU spikes don't always mean user impact. Only page when the user is affected and the budget is being consumed fast."

How do you calculate the 30-day error budget for a 99.9% SLO?warm-up
Model answer

Budget = (1 − SLO) × window. For 99.9%: 0.1% × 30 days × 24 hours × 60 min = 43.2 minutes. In request-based terms: if you serve 1 million requests, your budget is 1 000 failed requests. The budget is both a time budget (minutes of downtime) and a request budget (allowed failure count) — whichever framing fits your SLI.

Say it out loud say it

"99.9% SLO means 0.1% is the budget. Over 30 days that's 43 minutes of allowed downtime. I internalize that number so I know instantly how serious a 20-minute outage is."

What is symptom-based vs cause-based alerting?core
Model answer

Symptom-based alerting fires on user-visible impact (high error rate, high p99 latency, SLO burn). Cause-based alerting fires on internal indicators (CPU > 90%, disk > 80%). Problem with cause-based: CPU can spike without impacting users; or users suffer before CPU triggers. Prefer symptom-based for paging; use cause-based for informational dashboards or capacity planning tickets.

Say it out loud say it

"Alert on the symptom — is the user hurting? Cause-based alerts are noisy because internal metrics don't always correlate with user impact in either direction."

What is a correlation ID / trace ID and why is it critical for incident response?core
Model answer

A correlation ID (or trace ID) is a unique identifier generated at the entry point of a request and propagated through every service, log line, and span for that request. During incident response, a user-reported error with its trace ID lets you pull up the exact trace, the exact span that failed, and the correlated log line in seconds — no guessing which logs are relevant.

Say it out loud say it

"The trace ID ties a user's complaint to the exact logs and spans. Without it you're grepping through millions of lines. With it you're in the right place in 10 seconds."

How does the OTel Collector enable multi-backend or multi-tenant setups?stretch
Model answer

The Collector's pipeline model lets you add multiple exporters to the same pipeline — e.g. send traces to both Tempo and Jaeger simultaneously, or fan out metrics to Prometheus and Datadog. For multi-tenancy, the attributes processor can inject or redact tenant-specific fields, and the routing connector can split traffic by attribute value. This is why the Collector is central to the "OTel for instrumentation + backend of choice" pattern.

Say it out loud say it

"The Collector supports multiple exporters in one pipeline — fan out to Tempo and Datadog simultaneously. The routing connector splits traffic by attributes, which is how you do multi-tenant pipelines."
