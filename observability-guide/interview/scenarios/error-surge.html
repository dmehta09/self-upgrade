Scenario: error-rate surge.

A fast burn-rate alert fires at 14× on the 1-hour window. The checkout service is returning 5xx errors at a rate that would exhaust the monthly error budget in minutes.

01 The situation

SLO: 99.9% of requests succeed. Alert fired: 14.4× burn rate, fast window. Error budget burning fast.

In plain English analogy

The burn-rate alert is like a fuel gauge dropping to empty in real-time instead of gradually. At 1× burn you'd empty the tank in 30 days. At 14.4× you'd empty it in 50 hours — and at this rate in the 1-hour window, you're losing budget much faster. The fast window catches this before the budget is actually gone.

SLO
99.9% availability (success rate)
Burn rate
14.4× on the 1-hour window — exhausts 30-day budget in ~50 hours at sustained pace
Observed error rate
~4.5% (vs. 0.1% budget allowance)
First question
Which endpoint? Which version? Is this correlated to a recent deploy?

02 The burn-rate indicator

Interact with the slider to see how a 4.5% error rate maps to a burn rate multiple — and which alert tier it triggers.

03 Step-by-step investigation

1

Fast burn alert fires → RED dashboard

Open the Grafana RED panel. Error rate for POST /checkout is 4.5% — started ~12 minutes ago. Latency is normal. Traffic is normal. This is a clean error spike, not a latency problem.

2

Filter by endpoint and version

Break down the error panel by http.route and deployment version. The errors are 100% on version v2.14.3, deployed 18 minutes ago. All other versions are clean. Deploy correlation found.

3

Exemplar trace → find the failing span

Click an error exemplar from the error rate panel. The trace shows a createOrder span with status ERROR. Inside it: a call to the payments-service fails with a gRPC UNAVAILABLE status.

4

Correlated logs → confirm the dependency failure

From the failing span, jump to Loki (filtered by trace_id). Log line: {"event": "payments_call_failed", "reason": "connection refused", "endpoint": "payments-svc:9090", "trace_id": "abc123"}. The v2.14.3 deploy changed the payments service endpoint — wrong host in the config.

5

Rollback → watch budget recover

Roll back to v2.14.2. Error rate drops to 0.05% within 3 minutes. The burn-rate chart returns to below 1×. Error budget begins recovering. Incident closed.

Common trap gotcha

Don't alert on the cause (payments-service health check failing) — alert on the symptom (checkout SLO burn). The payment service might have been flapping for hours without a single checkout failing; the checkout SLO burn is what confirms user impact. Symptom-based alerting prevents false pages and missed true positives.

04 Narrate it in an interview (STAR)

STARWhat to say
Situation"99.9% availability SLO for checkout. A 14× burn-rate alert fired on the fast window, 12 minutes after a deploy."
Task"Identify which endpoint and version. Find the root cause fast — the budget was burning at 45×."
Action"RED error panel filtered by version — 100% on v2.14.3. Exemplar trace showed a UNAVAILABLE gRPC call to payments-service. Loki log confirmed wrong endpoint in the config. Rolled back."
Result"Error rate dropped to 0.05% within 3 minutes of rollback. Burn rate fell below 1×. Budget recovered over the following hours."

You get an alert that the payments service health check is failing. Should you page the on-call engineer?

Right. Cause-based alerts (health check down) don't confirm user impact. Maybe checkout has retries or circuit breakers. The SLO burn rate tells you if users are actually being affected — that's the signal to page on.

05 Interview rapid-fire

Why use a multi-window burn-rate alert instead of a simple error-rate threshold?core
Model answer

A single error-rate threshold alerts on spikes that might be transient — a 5-second burst triggers the page even if it self-resolves. Multi-window burn rates (e.g. 14.4× on 1-hour AND 6-hour) require sustained budget consumption to fire. The fast window catches big spikes quickly; the slow window filters noise. Together they give high precision — you only page when the budget is genuinely under threat.

Say it out loud say it

"A raw threshold alert pages on transient spikes. Multi-window burn rates require the problem to be sustained — fast window catches big issues, slow window proves it's not a blip."

What's your rollback decision criteria — how do you decide when to rollback vs. hotfix?stretch
Model answer

If the error rate is high and the root cause is clearly the deploy (version-correlated, started post-deploy), rollback immediately — don't try to hotfix under pressure. Hotfix only if the rollback would take longer than a targeted code fix, or if the previous version had a known breaking issue. The decision criterion: can you get the SLO back within the budget window faster by reverting or by fixing? Always prefer the fastest path back to SLO.

Say it out loud say it

"If the deploy caused it, roll back first and debug second. Don't hotfix under pressure with users still impacted. Restore service, then do a post-mortem."
