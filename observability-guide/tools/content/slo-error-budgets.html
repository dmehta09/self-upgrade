SLO/SLI · error budgets

Error budgets & burn rate.

The error budget converts your SLO from a reliability constraint into a shipping currency. When the budget is healthy you move fast; when it's nearly gone you stop and fix. It's the number that aligns product and engineering on the same terms.

01 The idea

Error budget = 100% − SLO — the allowed unreliability you may spend on deploys, experiments, and risk-taking.

A 99.9% SLO means 0.1% of requests can fail — about 43 minutes of downtime in a 30-day window. That's real time and real request failures you've pre-authorised. The key insight: reliability is a product decision, and the budget makes it explicit and finite.

In plain English analogy

Think of it as a monthly "allowed downtime" allowance — like a data plan. You can spend it on bold releases, risky experiments, or infrastructure maintenance. When it runs out, you stop spending (freeze features) and wait for the next cycle to restore the balance. Spend it wisely — or you get throttled.

Budget for 99.9% / 30 days
0.1% × 43,200 min = 43.2 minutes of allowed downtime (or equivalent request failures).
Budget for 99.5% / 30 days
0.5% = 216 minutes — a more generous allowance, but a weaker reliability promise.
Budget remaining
A live number: (allowed failures − actual failures) ÷ allowed failures. Shown as a percentage in dashboards.

03 The budget policy: fast vs frozen

The budget's job is to make reliability a shared, numeric decision — not an ongoing argument between product and engineering.

Budget healthy (>50%)
  • Ship features aggressively
  • Accept more deployment risk
  • Run chaos experiments
  • Invest in new infrastructure
Budget exhausted (<0%)
  • Freeze non-critical releases
  • Redirect engineering to reliability fixes
  • No new risky experiments
  • Postmortem + remediation takes priority

The policy converts a subjective debate into an objective trigger. Product can't argue "just ship it" when the budget is gone — there's a number on the screen.

Common trap gotcha

Two failure modes: (1) Chasing 100% leaves zero budget — you literally can't innovate without breaching. (2) Consistently ending the window with 90% budget left means your SLO is too lenient and users are getting better reliability than they need — you're being over-cautious. Calibrate the target so the budget is meaningfully spent each cycle.

04 Check your model

A team's 99.9% SLO gives them 43 minutes of error budget per month. They've already used 40 minutes this month. Product wants to deploy a risky new feature. What should happen?

Right. With 3 minutes left, any non-trivial incident would breach the SLO. The policy says freeze risky changes and fix reliability. Lowering the SLO would give more budget, not raising it — and that's a product/business decision, not a technical escape hatch.

05 Interview rapid-fire

What is an error budget and what does it unlock?core
Model answer

An error budget is 100% minus the SLO — the allowed unreliability for a window. For 99.9% over 30 days, that's 43 minutes of failures. The budget turns reliability into a shared, numeric decision: when it's healthy you ship aggressively; when it's exhausted you freeze features and fix reliability. It replaces the subjective "is it safe to deploy?" argument.

Say it out loud say it

"It's the allowed unreliability — 100 minus the SLO. Spend it on bold releases when healthy; freeze when it's gone. It makes reliability a shared number, not a judgment call."

Follow-ups to expect
  • What's the burn rate? (How fast you're spending the budget — covered in the alerting page.)
  • What happens when the budget is consistently unspent? (SLO may be too conservative — lower it to match user needs.)
What does it mean when a team consistently ends each month with 90% of their error budget unspent?warm
Model answer

It signals the SLO is too conservative — you're promising more reliability than users actually need. This wastes engineering effort on over-engineering reliability and leaves potential innovation on the table. The right response is to lower the SLO target to something you'll actually spend close to, freeing the team to take more risk in releases.

Say it out loud say it

"An unspent budget means the SLO is too tight. You're paying reliability tax that users don't need. Loosen the target and reinvest that engineering effort into features."

How does the error budget resolve the classic product vs engineering tension over releases?stretch
Model answer

It replaces a qualitative argument with an objective policy. If budget remains, product's request to ship is automatically endorsed — the system can absorb the risk. If budget is exhausted, the policy says stop. Neither side is overruling the other; both are reading the same number. This is why SRE teams call it a "shared incentive" — both teams want to stay in budget.

Say it out loud say it

"The budget is the referee. Budget healthy? Ship. Budget gone? Fix reliability first. No one's being overruled — there's just a number both teams agreed on in advance."
