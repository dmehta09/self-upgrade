Traces · sampling

Trace sampling.

A high-traffic service can produce millions of traces per minute. You can't store them all — and most are boring successes. Sampling decides which traces to keep, but the wrong strategy throws away the exact traces you needed to debug.

01 Why sampling exists

Cost at scale: a 10 k-RPS service at 5 spans/request = 50,000 spans/sec. At ~1 KB each, that's ~4 TB/day — before replication.

Sampling reduces volume while preserving signal. The art is keeping the traces that matter — slow requests, errors, novel paths — without requiring you to know which ones those are before they finish.

In plain English analogy

You get thousands of receipts a year. You don't file every Starbucks receipt — you keep the interesting ones: the unusually large expenses, the ones that look wrong, and a small random sample for audits. Same idea: keep the interesting traces, not every trace.

Head sampling
Decision made at the start of the request, before any outcome is known. Simple and cheap.
Tail sampling
Decision made after the trace finishes — can inspect outcome (latency, errors). Requires a buffer.
Error / rule-based
Always keep errors and slow requests; probabilistically keep the rest.

02 Head vs tail vs error — the trade-offs

Strategy Decision point Knows outcome? Infra cost Risk
Head (probabilistic) At trace start, in SDK No Near-zero Discards rare errors & outliers
Tail After trace completes, in Collector Yes Buffer memory per active trace Complex; buffer overflow loses traces
Error / rule-based Mix — root spans decide; errors always kept Partially Low-medium May miss slow non-errors
Common trap gotcha

Head sampling throws away the very traces you need. At 1% head sampling, 99% of errors and latency outliers are silently discarded before you ever see them. You'll know something is wrong from your metrics, but the trace that would explain why is gone. Use tail sampling or an error-first rule to guarantee you keep the interesting traces.

03 Where sampling happens

Sampling can live in the SDK (head only), in the OTel Collector (head or tail), or in the backend. Each stage has different visibility — later is more powerful but more expensive.

1

SDK (head only)

The OTel SDK samples at span creation time. The TraceIdRatioBased sampler keeps a deterministic N% by hashing the trace_id. Zero infra overhead, but blind to outcome.

2

OTel Collector — tail processor

The tail_sampling processor buffers complete traces in memory, then applies policies: always keep errors, keep traces over 500 ms, probabilistically sample the rest. Requires enough memory to hold all in-flight traces (typically 1–2 min worth).

3

Backend ingestion

Some backends (Jaeger, Tempo) apply their own ingestion-side sampling or rate limiting. At this point spans have already been transmitted — you've already paid the network cost.

collector-config.yaml
processors:
  tail_sampling:
    decision_wait: 10s          # buffer window
    policies:
      - name: keep-errors
        type: status_code
        status_code: {status_codes: [ERROR]}
      - name: keep-slow
        type: latency
        latency: {threshold_ms: 500}
      - name: probabilistic
        type: probabilistic
        probabilistic: {sampling_percentage: 5}

You're running 1% head sampling. An incident hits: p99 latency spikes for 3 minutes. When you look for traces to debug, what's the expected problem?

Right. Head sampling decides up-front, blind to latency. A 3-minute spike at 1% sampling means you kept ~1% of the slow traces — likely zero or one. Tail sampling (keep all traces >500 ms) would have captured every outlier.

05 Interview rapid-fire

What's the difference between head sampling and tail sampling?core
Model answer

Head sampling decides at the start of the request — before any outcome is known — using a random or hash-based coin flip. It's cheap but discards errors and outliers at the same rate as normal traffic. Tail sampling buffers the complete trace and decides afterward, so it can always keep errors and slow requests while sampling away healthy fast ones.

Say it out loud say it

"Head is a coin flip at the door — fast but blind. Tail waits for the receipt and decides based on what happened — it always keeps the interesting ones."

Follow-ups to expect

How does Grafana Tempo differ from Jaeger for trace storage?core
Model answer

Tempo uses object storage (S3/GCS) exclusively — no search index, just highly compressed chunks, which makes storage very cheap. You query with TraceQL and look up traces by ID (via exemplars from Prometheus or trace_id from Loki logs). Jaeger v2, rebuilt on the OTel Collector, uses pluggable backends and offers tag-based search with a richer UI out of the box.

Say it out loud say it

"Tempo is storage-first — cheap object storage, query with TraceQL, and you arrive at traces via metrics or logs. Jaeger gives you richer tag search out of the box."

How would you design a sampling strategy to never miss errors?stretch
Model answer

Use the OTel Collector's tail_sampling processor with a multi-policy approach: policy 1 always keeps ERROR status spans; policy 2 keeps traces above a latency threshold (e.g. 500 ms); policy 3 probabilistically samples the remaining healthy traffic at a low rate (1–5%). This guarantees 100% error coverage while dramatically cutting total volume.

Say it out loud say it

"Tail sampling in the Collector with three policies: always keep errors, always keep slow traces, and sample the boring fast-and-healthy ones at 5%. You get full coverage where it counts at a fraction of the storage cost."
