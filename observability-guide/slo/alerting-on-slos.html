SLO/SLI · alerting

Multi-burn-rate alerting.

Don't alert on CPU. Alert on how fast you're burning through user goodwill. Multi-burn-rate alerting — popularised by the Google SRE workbook — is the cleanest known way to get paged only when it matters, with enough signal to act.

01 The idea: alert on rate, not threshold

Burn rate = how many multiples of the baseline error rate you're currently experiencing. A 1× burn spends the budget in exactly 30 days; a 14.4× burn spends it in ~2 days.

The insight: a static CPU threshold fires indiscriminately — it has no idea whether users are feeling pain. A burn-rate alert is inherently user-symptomatic: it fires when the error budget is bleeding fast enough to threaten the SLO window.

In plain English analogy

A smoke alarm tuned so a single candle in the next room won't trip it — but a real fire in your kitchen triggers it in seconds. Static CPU/memory alerts are the "over-sensitive" smoke alarm that screams at toast. Burn-rate alerting is the well-calibrated alarm: it ignores small flickers and screams loudly only when there's genuine budget-threatening danger.

Burn rate 1×
Consuming budget at the exact expected pace. No alert. Budget exhausted in exactly 30 days.
Burn rate 14.4×
Spending 2% of the monthly budget in one hour. Treat as a page — human attention required immediately.
Burn rate 6×
Budget exhausted in 5 days. File a ticket; fix within hours, but not a 3 a.m. page.
Burn rate 3×
Budget gone in 10 days. Slow bleed — still ticket-worthy to investigate during business hours.

03 Multi-window: killing false alarms

Pair a short window (fast detection) with a long window (confirmation) — alert only when both are elevated. This kills the two failure modes: missing slow burns and screaming at transient spikes.

Same burn rate >14.4× still present over 6 hours? Now it's confirmed. Both must fire to page.

Common trap gotcha

Static threshold alerts — "CPU > 80%", "error rate > 1%" — have two deadly failure modes: false positives (spike for 30 seconds, page fires, engineer wakes up, nothing is wrong) and false negatives (slow 0.5% error rate for three days drains 15% of budget without ever hitting your threshold). Multi-burn-rate alerting catches both. Page humans only for budget-threatening, actionable events — anything less is pager fatigue in disguise.

04 Check your model

Your SLO is 99.9%. You set a single alert: "fire if error rate > 1% for 5 minutes." A real incident keeps error rate at 0.5% for 72 hours. What happens?

Right. A 0.5% error rate is 5× the baseline of 0.1% — that's a 5× burn rate. Over 72 hours it would consume ~360% of the monthly budget. Your static threshold of 1% never sees it. Multi-burn-rate alerting with a slow-burn tier (3× over 3 days) would catch this and file a ticket within hours.

05 Interview rapid-fire

What is burn rate and why alert on it instead of raw error rate?core
Model answer

Burn rate is your current error rate divided by the baseline error rate implied by your SLO. A 14.4× burn rate means you're consuming the monthly budget 14.4 times faster than planned — you'll exhaust it in roughly 2 days. Alerting on burn rate is better than raw error rate because it's SLO-relative: the same 0.5% error rate is a minor blip if your SLO is 99%, but a crisis if it's 99.9%.

Say it out loud say it

"Burn rate is current error rate divided by the baseline allowed by the SLO. I alert on that multiple — not the raw rate — because the same error count means very different things at different SLO targets."

Follow-ups to expect
  • What's the multi-window trick? (Pair short + long windows to eliminate false alarms.)
  • What burn rate would you page on? (14.4× — 2% of monthly budget in 1 hour.)
Why pair a short window with a long window in multi-burn-rate alerting?core
Model answer

A single short window detects fast but false-alarms on transient spikes — 30 seconds of high errors pages the on-call unnecessarily. A single long window misses fast-moving incidents — a 6-hour average looks fine while errors are spiking right now. Requiring both windows to be elevated simultaneously gives fast detection (short window saw it) with confirmation (long window confirms it's sustained), eliminating both failure modes.

Say it out loud say it

"Short window catches it fast. Long window confirms it's real, not a spike. Both must fire together — that's what separates a page from noise."

Why is "CPU > 80%" a bad alert to page on, and what would you replace it with?warm
Model answer

CPU is a cause, not a user symptom. High CPU might cause latency — or it might be normal batch processing with zero user impact. Paging on a cause leads to alert fatigue: engineers get woken up for non-issues. I'd replace it with an SLO burn-rate alert: fire only when error budget is depleting at a threatening rate. CPU becomes a diagnostic metric you consult during investigation, not an alert trigger.

Say it out loud say it

"CPU is a cause. I only page humans for user-visible symptoms — budget-threatening burn rates. CPU becomes a debugging tool I pull up after the symptom alert fires."
