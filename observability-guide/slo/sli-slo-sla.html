SLI vs SLO vs SLA.

Three terms that sound interchangeable but aren't. Get them precisely wrong in an interview and it signals you've never actually set reliability targets. Get them precisely right and you signal you've shipped with them.

01 The three terms, precisely

An SLI measures reality. An SLO is your internal target for that measure. An SLA is the external contract that penalises you if you breach a looser line.

Each builds on the next. You can't set a target without a measure, and you can't sign a contract without a target to defend. The SLA is always looser than the SLO — you need a buffer so internal teams can catch problems before customers invoke contractual remedies.

In plain English analogy

Your SLI is your exam score — the raw measured number. Your SLO is the passing grade you set for yourself: "I want to score 90% or above." Your SLA is the scholarship contract that says "fall below 75% and you lose funding." You set your personal goal higher than the contract so you never accidentally breach it.

SLI → SLO → SLA relationship
SLI
Measured ratio
good_events ÷ valid_events over time window. E.g. fraction of requests completing in <200 ms.
SLO
Internal target
99.9% of requests <200 ms over a rolling 28-day window. Internal — no penalty clause.
SLA
External contract
99.5% uptime guaranteed. Breach → service credits. Always looser than the SLO.
The gap between SLO (99.9%) and SLA (99.5%) is your buffer — time for engineering to react before customers invoke penalties.

02 Crafting a good SLI

A good SLI measures user-visible outcome, expressed as good events ÷ valid events.

The formula forces you to define what "good" means and what requests are even in scope. It produces a 0–1 ratio that's comparable across windows.

Availability SLI
Successful HTTP responses (2xx/3xx) ÷ all HTTP responses. Excludes client errors (4xx) from valid events.
Latency SLI
Requests completing under threshold (e.g. 200 ms) ÷ all requests. You pick the threshold from real user-experience data.
Correctness SLI
Responses returning the right data ÷ all responses. Harder to measure — requires a test oracle or synthetic probes.
Freshness SLI
Data refreshed within SLA window ÷ expected refreshes. Common for pipelines and caches.

SLIs should come from the user journey, not server internals. Ask: "Would a user notice if this got worse?" If no, don't track it as an SLI.

03 A worked example

A checkout service. The team picks two SLIs from the user's perspective — availability and latency — and sets tighter internal targets than the external SLA.

Common trap gotcha

Two classic mistakes in one: (1) Setting SLIs on causes instead of symptoms — tracking "CPU < 80%" tells you nothing about whether users are actually happy. (2) Setting the SLO at 100% — it's infinitely expensive to achieve, leaves no error budget to deploy or experiment, and gives your team no signal about how much unreliability you can safely absorb.

04 Check your model

Your team wants to SLI "database connection pool utilisation < 70%". What's wrong with this?

Right. SLIs must track something the user experiences: latency, availability, correctness. "Pool utilisation" is an implementation detail — it might predict a problem but it isn't the user's problem itself. SLI it on request success rate or latency instead.

05 Interview rapid-fire

What is the difference between an SLI, an SLO, and an SLA?core
Model answer

An SLI is a measured ratio: good events divided by valid events over a window — for example, the fraction of requests completing under 200 ms. An SLO is the internal target for that ratio — 99.9% over 28 days. An SLA is the external contract that penalises a breach, and it's always set looser than the SLO to give engineering a reaction window.

Say it out loud say it

"SLI is the measurement — fraction of good events. SLO is the internal goal for that measurement. SLA is the external contract, and it's always a softer line than the SLO so you have time to fix things before customers get credits."

Follow-ups to expect
  • Why is the SLA always looser than the SLO?
  • What makes a bad SLI? (Measuring causes, not symptoms.)
Why is a 100% SLO the wrong target?core
Model answer

100% is unachievable in practice — hardware fails, deploys introduce risk, and dependencies are unreliable. Chasing it is infinitely expensive and eliminates your error budget entirely, meaning any release or experiment that introduces even a single error is a breach. Setting a realistic target like 99.9% creates a budget you can deliberately spend on innovation.

Say it out loud say it

"100% leaves no error budget. You can't deploy, can't experiment, and any blip is a contractual failure. A slightly lower target is deliberate — it's the unreliability you're allowed to spend on progress."

How do you choose which SLIs to track?warm
Model answer

Start from the user journey — what does the user experience at each step? Common SLIs are availability (success rate), latency (fraction of requests under a threshold), and correctness (fraction returning right data). Reject any SLI that a user can't directly feel, like CPU utilisation or queue depth — those are causes, not symptoms.

Say it out loud say it

"I start from the user journey and ask 'would a user notice if this got worse?' If yes, it's an SLI candidate — availability, latency, correctness. If no — CPU, memory — it's a diagnostic metric, not an SLI."
