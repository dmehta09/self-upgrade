Delivery, Ownership & Senior Engineering Judgment

Senior interviews test judgment under ambiguity: how you decompose work, coach juniors, make time/quality tradeoffs, and ship operable features. Interviewers listen for ownership of interfaces, risk, and production readiness—not just coding speed.

01  Decision cheat strip

DecomposeVertical slices with clear contracts; seniors own spike/risk
AmbiguityWrite assumptions, decide, confirm; timebox discovery
Tradeoffs2-week slice with explicit debt vs fake 6-week promise
ContractsAPI schema first; integration tests as the handshake
Ship/delaySeverity × blast radius × workaround × rollback
OperableMetrics, alerts, runbooks before 'done'

02  Scenario cards (open → rehearse)

Work closed-book first: say your structure out loud, then open the card. Mark the domain practiced when you can recite the “say it” lines cold.

d12-q01You are given a major new feature and two junior interns. How would you approach, decompose, delegate, review, and deliver it?
First 30 seconds
I'd own the outcome: clarify success metrics, spike the risky seams, cut vertical slices juniors can finish, and review early for interfaces—not only polish at the end.
Model answer
  1. Align on problem, users, non-goals, and definition of done (including observability).
  2. Spike unknowns myself (authz, data model, external API) and publish a short design with contracts.
  3. Break into vertical slices with acceptance tests; assign juniors slices sized to 1–3 days with clear APIs.
  4. Pair on the first slice; establish patterns (FastAPI router shape, error model) they can copy.
  5. Review PRs for correctness, tenancy/security, and operability; give fast feedback cycles.
  6. Integrate continuously; I own release, rollout flags, and the production checklist.

Nest / Node: Same ownership model in Nest: seniors define module boundaries and DTO contracts; juniors implement providers inside those walls.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

I keep the risky design and integration, give juniors well-bounded slices with contracts, and review early so we don't discover architectural mistakes on merge day.

Traps / weak answers
  • Throwing a vague epic at interns and disappearing into meetings.
  • Only reviewing style nits while missing broken API contracts.
Follow-ups
  • How do you handle an intern stuck for more than a day?
  • What do you do if their code works but is unsafe under concurrency?
d12-q02Requirements are ambiguous but the delivery date is fixed. How would you reduce risk before implementation?
First 30 seconds
I'd timebox discovery, write assumptions as acceptance criteria, propose a thinnest lovable slice, and get a lightweight stakeholder confirm—then build against that contract.
Model answer
  1. List open questions ranked by blast radius (billing rules > button copy).
  2. Timebox research/spikes (hours, not weeks); convert answers into written assumptions.
  3. Draft acceptance criteria and out-of-scope; share in Slack/PRD stub for async confirm.
  4. Choose a vertical MVP that hits the date; park nice-to-haves explicitly.
  5. Identify irreversible decisions (schema, public API) and bias them toward change-friendly expand/contract.
  6. Start implementation on the confirmed core while waiting on low-risk polish questions.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

Fixed date plus ambiguity means write assumptions, confirm a thin slice, and protect irreversible decisions. I won't pretend uncertainty away or freeze until a perfect PRD.

Traps / weak answers
  • Building three speculative variants 'just in case'.
  • Silent assumptions that explode in QA week.
Follow-ups
  • What if stakeholders disagree mid-confirm?
  • How do you communicate residual risk to the PM?
d12-q03How would you break a large feature into tasks that junior developers can implement safely and independently?
First 30 seconds
Independence comes from stable contracts and vertical slices. I'd publish API/DB interfaces first, then tasks that each deliver a testable piece without needing to invent cross-cutting design.
Model answer
  1. Define boundaries: routes, DTOs, table ownership, events—checked into a short design doc or OpenAPI stub.
  2. Cut vertical slices (endpoint + validation + persistence + test) rather than 'all frontend' / 'all DB' horizontal layers.
  3. Size tasks to 1–3 days; each has fixtures, acceptance checks, and a named reviewer.
  4. Sequence: foundations (schema migrate expand) → core path → edge cases → polish.
  5. Provide golden-path examples in the repo; forbid juniors from inventing new auth/tenancy patterns.
  6. Integration branch or feature flag so independent work merges without a big-bang.
Nest / Node: Generate shared DTO/OpenAPI types so Nest and FastAPI (or FE) consumers don't invent conflicting shapes.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

Juniors stay safe when contracts exist first and tasks are vertical, small, and copy an established pattern. Independence without interfaces is just parallel confusion.

Traps / weak answers
  • Horizontal tasks that block on each other until the last day.
  • Tasks that require inventing the auth model from scratch.
Follow-ups
  • How do you handle a shared migration two people need?
  • When is a spike task assigned to a junior vs you?
d12-q04What parts of the feature would you own personally and what would you delegate?
First 30 seconds
I own risk, contracts, and production readiness. I delegate well-scoped implementation inside those rails and still review the dangerous edges.
Model answer
  1. Own: problem framing, architecture spike, public API/schema, security/tenancy model, rollout plan.
  2. Own: data migrations strategy, failure modes, metrics/alerts, incident runbook.
  3. Delegate: CRUD handlers following patterns, UI wiring, tests for happy paths, docs polish.
  4. Co-own: tricky queries, concurrency, payment/idempotency—pair or carefully review.
  5. Never delegate 'figure out authz' or 'pick the consistency model' without oversight.
  6. Stay available as a blocker-remover; ownership means outcome, not typing every line.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

I keep the irreversible and high-blast-radius decisions, delegate patterned implementation, and co-own anything that can corrupt data or leak tenants. Outcome stays on me.

Traps / weak answers
  • Delegating everything then blaming juniors for architectural failure.
  • Owning every line and becoming the bottleneck.
Follow-ups
  • How do you create growth opportunities without risking prod?
  • What do you do when a delegate's estimate is wildly off?
d12-q05A junior engineer proposes an architecture you believe will fail under scale. How would you handle the situation?
First 30 seconds
I'd coach with evidence, not authority theater: pressure-test their design against concrete failure modes, offer a simpler alternative, and leave them ownership of the improved approach.
Model answer
  1. Ask them to walk the design for peak load, failure, and multi-tenant cases—teach via questions.
  2. Show a concrete scenario (e.g. N+1, single-node state, unbounded queue) where it breaks.
  3. Propose a minimal change that preserves their good ideas but fixes the hazard.
  4. If time allows, spike both for a day with metrics—data beats opinion.
  5. Document the decision and rationale so the team learns the pattern.
  6. Escalate only if they insist on a clearly unsafe path after coaching; protect production.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

I challenge with scenarios and numbers, not ego. We converge on a design that survives load, and the junior still feels authorship of the fix.

Traps / weak answers
  • Publicly shutting them down without teaching.
  • Accepting a known-bad design to 'be nice'.
Follow-ups
  • What if the junior is right and your scale fears are premature?
  • How do you disagree with a peer senior differently than with a junior?
d12-q06The ideal architecture needs six weeks but the business needs something in two weeks. How would you decide what to ship?
First 30 seconds
I'd separate must-hold invariants from nice architecture. Ship a two-week vertical slice that is safe and measurable, with an explicit backlog for the remaining four weeks.
Model answer
  1. Name non-negotiables: security, tenancy, data integrity, rollback—never trade these away.
  2. Cut scope: one persona, one path, feature flags, manual ops allowed temporarily if documented.
  3. Choose reversible shortcuts (monolith module now, extract later) over irreversible ones (bad public API).
  4. Write the debt list with owners and dates; get PM buy-in that week-3 work is scheduled.
  5. Instrument the MVP so we learn whether the six-week design is still justified.
  6. Refuse fake commitments: don't say 'full platform' in two weeks.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

Two weeks means a safe thin slice plus honest debt—not a fragile full vision. Invariants stay; polish and generality wait with a dated plan.

Traps / weak answers
  • Heroically promising the six-week design in two weeks.
  • Shipping without authz 'temporary' that becomes permanent.
Follow-ups
  • What shortcut would you refuse even under CEO pressure?
  • How do you prevent the temporary path from living a year?
d12-q07A feature is almost complete when a major requirement changes. How would you reassess scope and implementation?
First 30 seconds
I'd stop and re-estimate against the new requirement: what salvage, what rewrite, what date moves. Then propose options with cost—don't silently bolt incompatible behavior on.
Model answer
  1. Clarify the new requirement and whether the old one is obsolete or concurrent.
  2. Map impact: API contracts, schema, UX, migrations already shipped.
  3. Options: (A) adapt current design, (B) feature-flag dual behavior, (C) cut and restart a thin new slice—with calendar costs.
  4. Protect production: if half-shipped schema conflicts, expand/contract carefully; avoid dual-write mess without a plan.
  5. Reset acceptance tests and communicate date/risk to stakeholders immediately.
  6. Capture the change reason so planning improves next time.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

Mid-flight change means pause, impact map, and explicit options with dates. I won't duct-tape incompatible requirements and call it done.

Traps / weak answers
  • Quietly stretching nights to absorb infinite scope.
  • Leaving dead code paths for the old requirement without flags or deletion plan.
Follow-ups
  • How do you handle a change after the public API already shipped?
  • When do you insist on a formal change request?
d12-q08Two engineers are building related components and their APIs are drifting apart. How would you prevent integration problems?
First 30 seconds
I'd freeze a shared contract early—OpenAPI/JSON Schema or proto—plus a consumer-driven test. Drift dies when the handshake is executable, not tribal knowledge.
Model answer
  1. Agree on request/response shapes, error model, and idempotency keys in a short design review.
  2. Check in an OpenAPI stub or shared package; generate clients if possible.
  3. Add contract tests in CI (Pact or schema validation against fixtures).
  4. Schedule a mid-flight integration spike before either side is 'done'.
  5. Own the boundary as senior: breaking changes require versioning or expand/contract.
  6. Daily 10-minute sync while the interface is soft; document decisions in the PR.

Nest / Node: Share DTO packages between Nest services or publish an OpenAPI artifact both validate against in CI.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

Prevent drift with a checked-in contract and CI that fails when either side changes it unilaterally. Integration week should be boring.

Traps / weak answers
  • Waiting until merge day to try the first end-to-end call.
  • Verbal agreements in standup with no schema artifact.
Follow-ups
  • How do you version the contract after GA?
  • What if one side needs a field the other can't produce yet?
d12-q09A production bug is discovered just before release. How would you decide whether to delay the release or proceed?
First 30 seconds
I'd classify severity and blast radius, check for workarounds and rollback confidence, then make an explicit go/no-go with the PM—not a vibes decision.
Model answer
  1. Severity: data loss/security/PII > major wrong results > cosmetic.
  2. Blast radius: % users, paying tenants, irreversible side effects.
  3. Mitigations: feature flag off, config kill switch, hotfix in hours, manual workaround.
  4. Release mechanics: can we roll back cleanly? Are migrations forward-only dangerous?
  5. Proceed only if low severity or mitigated and monitored; otherwise delay with a clear new time.
  6. Record the decision; schedule the fix; don't bury known Sev-1s behind a launch party.
Say it out loud
Say it spoken

Ship-or-delay is risk math: severity, blast radius, workaround, and rollback. I won't launch a known data-corrupting bug to hit a symbolic date.

Traps / weak answers
  • Always ship because 'we can patch tomorrow'.
  • Always delay for trivial UI bugs and destroying trust in dates.
Follow-ups
  • How do feature flags change this calculus?
  • What if legal/compliance is involved?
d12-q10How would you ensure a feature is operable in production, not just functionally correct in development?
First 30 seconds
Done means operable: golden signals, actionable alerts, a runbook, and a rollback path. I'd bake those into the definition of done before calling the feature complete.
Model answer
  1. Define SLIs/SLOs for the feature (latency, error rate, lag) and dashboards from day one.
  2. Emit structured logs/metrics/traces with correlation ids covering success and failure paths.
  3. Alert on symptoms users feel (burn rate, queue age), not only on CPU; include runbook links.
  4. Document failure modes: dependency down, poison message, bad deploy—with steps to mitigate.
  5. Ship behind a flag; verify in canary/prod with synthetic checks; know how to roll back/migrate safely.
  6. Load-test critical paths; confirm Postgres/Mongo indexes and Nginx timeouts match real traffic patterns.

Say it out loud
Say it spoken

A feature isn't done until it has metrics, alerts, and a runbook. Functional demos in dev don't survive contact with production dependencies and real concurrency.

Traps / weak answers
  • Calling done when QA passed on staging with empty data.
  • Alerts with no owner or runbook (pages that teach nothing).
Follow-ups
  • What are the golden signals for a queue-backed FastAPI worker?
  • How do you avoid alert fatigue while staying safe?

03  Self-check

Requirements are fuzzy and the date is fixed. What should a senior do first?
Reduce risk with explicit assumptions and a thin confirmed slice—neither paralysis nor blind coding.
Ideal design needs six weeks; business needs two. Best move?
Make the tradeoff explicit: deliver value safely now, schedule the rest, protect invariants.
A production bug appears hours before release. You decide by…
Risk-based go/no-go with a clear owner and rollback plan beats absolute rules.
What makes a feature 'done' for a senior?
Operability is part of delivery ownership, not a later ops handoff.
