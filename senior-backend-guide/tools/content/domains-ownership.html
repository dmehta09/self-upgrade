Domain · d12

Delivery, Ownership & Senior Engineering Judgment

Senior interviews test ownership under ambiguity: how you clarify outcomes, decompose work, make explicit tradeoffs, and ship operable systems—not just tickets. Mid engineers finish assigned work; seniors own interfaces, risk communication, junior development, and production readiness. Influence shows up as disagree-and-commit, multi-team unblocking, and turning incidents into systemic fixes rather than heroics.

01  Decision cheat strip

Own outcomesvs implement tickets — you own result, risk, and operability
Risk vs %Communicate risk and options, not status percentage theater
Develop vs dumpGrow engineers with contracts and review — don't dump vague tasks
Operable donevs demoed — metrics, alerts, runbook before 'done'
Disagree-commitPush with evidence; then commit or escalate safety
Ship/delaySeverity × blast × workaround × rollback confidence
Reversible firstBias incomplete data toward expand/contract decisions
Debt with dateTemporary shortcuts need owner, metric, repayment date

Work closed-book first: say your structure out loud, then open the card. Mark the domain practiced when you can recite the “say it” lines cold.

d12-q01You are given a major new feature and two junior interns. How would you approach, decompose, delegate, review, and deliver it?
First 30 seconds
I'd own the outcome end-to-end: clarify success metrics and non-goals, spike the risky seams myself, cut vertical slices juniors can finish in 1–3 days, and review early for contracts—not only polish at the end.
Model answer
  1. Clarify / open: align on users, success metrics, non-goals, and definition of done including observability—because vague epics produce thrash vs a thin written contract that juniors can execute against.
  2. Spike unknowns myself (authz, data model, external API, migration risk) and publish a short design with FastAPI router shapes, error model, and DTO contracts before assigning implementation.
  3. Break into vertical slices (endpoint + validation + persistence + tests) sized to 1–3 days; assign juniors clear APIs rather than horizontal layers that block until merge day.
  4. Tradeoff: pair on the first slice to establish patterns they can copy vs letting them invent architecture—pairing costs a day up front because unsupervised invention costs weeks of rewrite.
  5. Failure modes: juniors stuck silently >1 day, unsafe concurrency/tenancy slipping through, and 'works on my laptop' without integration—detect with daily unblock sync and CI against shared fixtures.
  6. Review PRs for correctness, tenancy/security, and operability first; style nits last. I own integration, feature flags, and the production checklist.
  7. Prevent/ops: track slice cycle time and PR review lag as delivery health; require a dashboard stub and alert placeholder before calling the feature done.
  8. Close with a game-day or canary checklist I own: rollback path, runbook link, and who pages if error rate or latency SLO burns after launch.

d12-q02Requirements are ambiguous but the delivery date is fixed. How would you reduce risk before implementation?
First 30 seconds
I'd timebox discovery, write assumptions as acceptance criteria, propose the thinnest lovable slice that hits the date, and get a lightweight stakeholder confirm—then build against that contract rather than guessing in code.
Model answer
  1. Clarify: list open questions ranked by blast radius (billing rules and schema > button copy) because low-signal polish questions should not block irreversible design.
  2. Timebox research/spikes in hours, not weeks; convert answers into written assumptions and out-of-scope so silent guesses don't explode in QA week.
  3. Draft acceptance criteria and share async for lightweight confirm; choose a vertical MVP that hits the date and park nice-to-haves explicitly with owners.
  4. Tradeoff: start confirmed core now vs freezing until a perfect PRD—paralysis misses the date; blind coding burns rework because assumptions were never checked.
  5. Bias irreversible decisions (public API, schema) toward expand/contract and versioning rather than one-way tight coupling when data is incomplete.
  6. Failure modes: stakeholders disagree mid-confirm, residual ambiguity on edge cases, and scope creep disguised as 'clarification'—detect with a decision log and change-cost callouts.
  7. Prevent/ops: publish a risk register (assumption, owner, confirm-by date) and track open high-blast questions as a delivery metric until closed.
  8. Monitor residual risk in weekly status as options+impact, not a green status %; alert stakeholders when a confirm-by date slips past the implementation critical path.

d12-q03How would you break a large feature into tasks that junior developers can implement safely and independently?
First 30 seconds
Independence comes from stable contracts and vertical slices. I'd publish API/DB interfaces first, then tasks that each deliver a testable piece without inventing cross-cutting design.
Model answer
  1. Define boundaries first: routes, DTOs, table ownership, events—checked into a short design doc or OpenAPI stub because independence without interfaces is parallel confusion.
  2. Cut vertical slices (endpoint + validation + persistence + test) rather than horizontal 'all DB' / 'all API' layers that serialize on merge day.
  3. Size tasks to 1–3 days with fixtures, acceptance checks, and a named reviewer; sequence foundations (expand migrate) → core path → edges → polish.
  4. Tradeoff: more up-front contract work vs faster parallel coding—contracts cost a day because missing them costs integration week and rewrite.
  5. Provide golden-path examples in-repo; forbid inventing new auth/tenancy patterns—failure mode is every junior creating a bespoke security model.
  6. Shared migrations and cross-slice dependencies get a single owner and expand/contract order so two people don't fight the same schema PR.
  7. Prevent/ops: feature flag or trunk integration so independent work merges continuously; track blocked-task age and integration failures as process metrics.
  8. Add contract tests in CI and a mid-flight integration spike before either side is 'done'; alert on contract-test failures as a merge blocker.

d12-q04What parts of the feature would you own personally and what would you delegate?
First 30 seconds
I own risk, contracts, and production readiness. I delegate well-scoped implementation inside those rails and still review the dangerous edges—outcome stays with me even when I don't type every line.
Model answer
  1. Own personally: problem framing, architecture spike, public API/schema, security/tenancy model, rollout/rollback plan—because these are high blast-radius and often irreversible.
  2. Own: data migration strategy, failure modes, metrics/alerts, incident runbook, and release checklist; operable delivery is not an ops handoff after demo.
  3. Delegate: CRUD handlers following patterns, UI wiring, happy-path tests, docs polish—work with clear rails and copyable examples.
  4. Co-own or pair: tricky queries, concurrency, idempotency, payments—tradeoff is growth opportunity vs silent data corruption; pair when the failure mode is irreversible.
  5. Never fully delegate 'figure out authz' or 'pick the consistency model' without oversight—failure mode is a polite PR that leaks tenants.
  6. Stay available as blocker-remover; ownership means outcome and risk communication, not being the typing bottleneck on every file.
  7. Prevent/ops: publish an ownership map (RACI-lite) for contracts, migrations, and on-call; track decision log entries for irreversible choices.
  8. Measure review coverage on dangerous edges and require runbook + SLO stub before merge of the last slice; debt backlog age for anything temporarily delegated without rails.

d12-q05A junior engineer proposes an architecture you believe will fail under scale. How would you handle the situation?
First 30 seconds
I'd coach with evidence, not authority theater: pressure-test their design against concrete failure modes, offer a minimal fix that preserves their good ideas, and leave them authorship of the improved approach.
Model answer
  1. Ask them to walk peak load, failure, multi-tenant, and retry paths—teach via questions so the critique lands as engineering, not ego.
  2. Show a concrete scenario (N+1, single-node state, unbounded queue, hot partition) where the proposal breaks, with rough numbers when possible.
  3. Propose a minimal change that keeps their good ideas but removes the hazard—tradeoff: rewrite their design vs surgical fix; prefer surgical because ownership and learning stick.
  4. If time allows, spike both for a day with metrics—data beats opinion when the disagreement is capacity, not safety.
  5. Failure modes of bad coaching: public shutdown that kills initiative, or accepting a known-bad design to 'be nice' that later pages the team.
  6. Document the decision and rationale in a short ADR so the team learns the pattern; escalate only if they insist on a clearly unsafe path after coaching.
  7. Prevent/ops: add a lightweight design review checklist for scale/failure modes before large PRs; track design-review follow-through as a quality metric.
  8. If the spike ships, monitor the chosen SLI (p95 latency, queue age, error rate) in canary and keep a rollback; schedule a game-day for the feared failure mode.

d12-q06The ideal architecture needs six weeks but the business needs something in two weeks. How would you decide what to ship?
First 30 seconds
I'd separate must-hold invariants from nice architecture. Ship a two-week vertical slice that is safe and measurable, with an explicit backlog and dates for the remaining four weeks—not a fake full-platform promise.
Model answer
  1. Name non-negotiables first: security, tenancy, data integrity, rollback—never trade these away because temporary authz holes become permanent breach surface.
  2. Cut scope: one persona, one path, feature flags, documented manual ops if needed—value now vs generality later.
  3. Choose reversible shortcuts (module in monolith now, extract later) over irreversible ones (bad public API, wrong tenancy key)—because expand/contract beats rewrite.
  4. Tradeoff: 2-week safe slice + dated debt vs promising the six-week design in two weeks—the latter fails both trust and quality when reality hits.
  5. Write the debt list with owners and repayment dates; get explicit PM buy-in that week-3+ work is scheduled, not hoped.
  6. Instrument the MVP so metrics tell whether the six-week design is still justified—learn before overbuilding.
  7. Failure modes: 'temporary' path living a year, silent quality cuts, and fake commitments—detect via debt backlog age and SLO error budget on the thin path.
  8. Prevent/ops: track debt items with owners/dates; alert when repayment slips; canary the slice and keep a runbook for the manual-ops fallback.

d12-q07A feature is almost complete when a major requirement changes. How would you reassess scope and implementation?
First 30 seconds
I'd stop and re-estimate against the new requirement: what salvage, what rewrite, what date moves. Then propose options with cost—don't silently bolt incompatible behavior onto a half-finished design.
Model answer
  1. Clarify whether the old requirement is obsolete or concurrent; write the new acceptance criteria before touching code so thrash has a target.
  2. Map impact: API contracts, schema, UX, migrations already shipped, and feature flags already on—know what is reversible vs already in prod.
  3. Options with calendar cost: (A) adapt current design, (B) dual behavior behind flags, (C) cut and restart a thin new slice—tradeoff speed vs cleanliness depends on how entangled the old path is.
  4. Protect production: if half-shipped schema conflicts, use expand/contract; avoid dual-write mess without an explicit cutover and backfill plan.
  5. Failure modes: silent night heroics absorbing infinite scope, dead code paths for the old requirement, and date theater without resetting tests.
  6. Reset acceptance tests and communicate date/risk to stakeholders immediately with a decision log entry—don't surprise them at launch.
  7. Prevent/ops: require a change-impact checklist when requirements flip late; track change-request lead time and escaped rework as planning metrics.
  8. Monitor the dual-path period with explicit metrics/alerts per behavior flag; schedule deletion of the obsolete path with an owner and date.

d12-q08Two engineers are building related components and their APIs are drifting apart. How would you prevent integration problems?

  7. Prevent/ops: daily 10-minute sync while the interface is soft; track contract-test failures and integration bug escape rate as process metrics.

d12-q09A production bug is discovered just before release. How would you decide whether to delay the release or proceed?
First 30 seconds
I'd classify severity and blast radius, check for workarounds and rollback confidence, then make an explicit go/no-go with the PM—not a vibes decision or a launch-party schedule.
Model answer
  1. Severity ladder: data loss / security / PII > major wrong results > degraded UX > cosmetic—because not all bugs deserve the same delay cost.
  2. Blast radius: percent of users, paying tenants, irreversible side effects, exploitability if security-related.
  3. Mitigations: feature flag off, config kill switch, hotfix in hours, manual workaround—proceed only if mitigated and monitored.
  4. Tradeoff: delay vs ship-with-mitigation—delay when integrity/security is at risk; ship when severity is low and rollback is clean, because always-delay destroys date trust.
  5. Release mechanics: can we roll back cleanly? Are migrations forward-only dangerous? Expand/contract unfinished = higher delay bias.
  6. Failure modes: launching a known Sev-1 behind a symbolic date, or delaying trivial UI bugs and training the org that dates are fake.
  7. Record the go/no-go in a decision log with owner; schedule the fix; communicate the new time if delayed.
  8. Prevent/ops: require severity × blast × workaround × rollback checklist in the release runbook; alert on the bug's symptom SLI during/after launch if you proceed mitigated.

  6. Load-test critical paths; confirm indexes and timeouts match real traffic—failure mode is QA-green then production timeout storms.
  7. Prevent/ops: definition-of-done gate requires dashboard + alert + runbook URL + rollback owner before merge of 'done'.

d12-q11How do you define ownership for a backend service, and what do you do when a production problem has no clear owner?
First 30 seconds
Ownership means outcomes for a service's reliability, changes, and customer impact—not just who last merged. When nobody owns a production problem, I temporarily take incident command, restore service, then fix the ownership gap so it cannot recur.
Model answer
  1. Define ownership as: who can change it, who gets paged, who accepts risk tradeoffs, and who owes the runbook/SLO—not merely the original author of the repo.
  2. Publish a service catalog entry: purpose, tier, on-call, deploy path, dependencies, and escalation—because tribal knowledge fails at 2am.
  3. When a problem has no clear owner: declare incident commander, mitigate first, then assign a temporary DRI for the follow-up—never leave a Sev floating in Slack.
  4. Tradeoff: take temporary ownership yourself vs escalate immediately to a director—take it when you can mitigate; escalate when blast radius or cross-team authority exceeds your reach.
  5. Failure modes: 'everyone owns it' means nobody owns it; orphan services with no on-call; and silent handoffs after reorgs that leave pages unanswered.
  6. After mitigate: write a short ownership RFC—primary team, backup, and what 'good' looks like for SLO and change management.
  7. Prevent/ops: track services missing owners or stale on-call rotations as a reliability metric; alert on pages with no acknowledged commander within N minutes.
  8. Monitor incident follow-up rate for orphaned services; game-day ownership gaps; keep a decision log for any permanent reassignment.

d12-q24The team repeatedly makes the same production mistake. How do you turn that into a systemic process or tooling fix?
First 30 seconds

Model answer
  1. Aggregate repeats: same class of incident, same PR miss, same deploy footgun—tag them so 'random' errors become a pattern.
  2. Ask why the mistake is easy: missing checklist, unclear ownership, no test, or toil pressure—because vs blaming individuals each time.
  3. Design a forcing function: CI gate, codeowner review on dangerous paths, scaffold/template, or canary requirement—automation beats reminders.
  4. Tradeoff: heavy process vs lightweight guardrail—prefer the smallest tool that blocks the failure class without drowning throughput.

  7. Prevent/ops: track repeat-incident rate by tag; alert when the same tag fires twice in a window; review tooling gaps in postmortems.
  8. Game-day the fix; monitor that the CI/alert pages on the real condition and doesn't create fatigue that people bypass.

d12-q25A monolith is getting hard to change. How do you decide whether to split into services—and when is that the wrong move?
First 30 seconds
I'd diagnose whether the pain is modularity, ownership, or scaling. Split only when a clear boundary, independent deploy need, and operability maturity exist—otherwise modularize the monolith. Premature services trade one complexity for distributed failure modes.
Model answer
  1. Clarify the pain: slow deploys, ownership conflicts, scaling hotspots, or just messy modules—different pains need different cures.
  2. Prefer modular monolith first: clear module boundaries, CODEOWNERS, separate data access—because vs network hops without solving coupling.
  3. Split when: independent scale/lifecycle, separate failure domains needed, or team ownership can't share a deploy without constant blocking—and contracts are ready.
  4. Wrong move when: the team can't operate one system well yet, boundaries are unclear, or the goal is 'microservices fashion'—distributed monolith is worse.
  5. Tradeoff: service split gains deploy independence vs adds latency, consistency, and on-call surface—pay that tax only for a real boundary.
  6. Failure modes: splitting along technical layers instead of domains, shared DB across 'services', and no observability on the new network edge.
  7. Prevent/ops: if splitting, require SLOs, tracing, and runbooks per service before cutover; track deploy frequency and cross-service error rates.
  8. Strangler with flags and expand/contract; monitor the seam's latency/error SLO and keep a rollback to the modular path during migration.

d12-q26How do you connect backend engineering work to business or customer outcomes, and how do you know a project succeeded?
First 30 seconds
I'd start from the customer/business metric the work should move, instrument a baseline, ship iteratively against that metric, and call success when the outcome moves within agreed bounds—not when the last ticket closes.
Model answer
  1. Translate every initiative to an outcome hypothesis: conversion, latency SLO, cost, support volume, or reliability—because vs 'ship the service' as the goal.
  2. Baseline before build; define leading eng metrics (error rate, p95) and lagging business metrics you influence.
  3. Design the thinnest vertical slice that can falsify or support the hypothesis; avoid multi-month builds with no learning checkpoint.
  4. Tradeoff: engineering elegance vs outcome speed—choose elegance when it protects the outcome metric long-term, not as a vanity end state.
  5. Success criteria written up front with Product: metric, threshold, time window, and non-goals—ticket burn-down alone is not success.
  6. Failure modes: shipping on time to the wrong outcome, measuring only vanity counters, and declaring victory at merge.
  7. Prevent/ops: dashboards linking deploy markers to outcome metrics; review outcome movement in the project closeout.
  8. Alert if the leading SLI degrades after launch even when the feature 'works'; schedule a follow-up if the business metric doesn't move by the agreed window.

d12-q27You have incomplete information and limited time to make a production decision, and an executive wants an answer now. What is your approach?
First 30 seconds
I'd give a timeboxed recommendation with explicit confidence, assumptions, and a reversible default—plus what I'd verify next. Executives get a decision-quality answer now, not false certainty or an unbounded research request.
Model answer
  1. State what you know, what you don't, and the decision deadline—transparency beats fake precision under executive pressure.
  2. Recommend the reversible, lowest-blast option by default (flag off, rollback, rate-limit, degrade) when data is thin—because vs irreversible commits under uncertainty.
  3. Give confidence explicitly (low/med/high) and the one or two checks that would raise it in the next N minutes/hours.
  4. Tradeoff: answer now with caveats vs asking for more time—answer now for mitigate/go-no-go; ask for time only when the irreversible choice can wait a short spike.
  5. Offer options with consequences in business language; name the decision owner if it isn't you.
  6. Failure modes: confident guessing, freezing without a temporary mitigate, or flooding the room with undifferentiated detail.
  7. Log the decision and assumptions immediately so the org can revisit when data arrives.
  8. Prevent/ops: after the immediate action, monitor the symptom SLI closely; set a revisit time; alert if the temporary mitigate's error budget or queue age worsens.

d12-q28Product insists on shipping a feature you believe is unsafe. How do you handle it?
First 30 seconds
I'd document the concrete harm (data loss, security, compliance, irreversible customer damage), propose a safe alternative that still moves their outcome, and escalate with a written risk decision if they still insist—I won't silently ship a known Sev-1.
Model answer
  1. Translate 'unsafe' into specific failure modes and blast radius (who gets hurt, how, whether reversible)—vague discomfort loses to roadmap pressure.
  2. Propose alternatives: flag + limited cohort, delayed ship with mitigation, or reduced scope that preserves the outcome without the hazard.
  3. Tradeoff: partnership vs hard gate—partner on outcomes; hard-gate on integrity/security/compliance where you have duty of care.
  4. Write a short risk memo: recommendation, residual risk if shipped, and who accepts that risk—force an explicit owner.
  5. If Product still insists: escalate to eng leadership/security with the memo; do not quietly merge and hope.
  6. Failure modes: silent compliance then blame, absolute refusal without alternatives, or shipping with no monitoring on the feared failure.
  7. If leadership accepts residual risk in writing: ship only behind maximum mitigations (flag, cohort, kill switch) and heightened monitoring.
  8. Prevent/ops: alert on the feared symptom SLI at tighter thresholds; runbook ready; schedule an immediate follow-up review; track risk-acceptance decisions in the decision log.
